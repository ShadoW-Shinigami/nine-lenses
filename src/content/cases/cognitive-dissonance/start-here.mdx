In an experiment published in 1959, two researchers at Stanford University in California gave students an hour of very boring tasks. Then they paid some of the students $1, and others $20, to tell the next participant that the tasks had been fun. Afterwards, the students who had been paid only $1 said they had enjoyed the tasks more. The students paid $20 did not. Most people expect the opposite. This page explains what you need to know before reading about how and why it happened.

## The people who did it

Leon Festinger (1919-1989) was born in Brooklyn, New York. His father was a Jewish immigrant from Russia. After City College in New York, he studied at the University of Iowa under Kurt Lewin, one of the founders of social psychology, and worked with Lewin's group at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology. He moved to Stanford in 1955, and by the time of this study he was one of the best-known social psychologists in the United States. He had already proposed <Term>social comparison theory</Term> in 1954: the idea that people judge their own opinions and abilities by comparing themselves with others. In 1957 he published *A Theory of Cognitive Dissonance*, the book that this experiment was designed to test.

James M. Carlsmith (1936-1984), later known as J. Merrill Carlsmith, was the junior author. He was a Stanford undergraduate at the time, and the study was his senior honours thesis, supervised by Festinger. It won the 1958 Sigma Xi award for outstanding student research (Lepper et al., c. 1984). Carlsmith went on to a doctorate at Harvard, where he worked with Elliot Aronson, and returned to Stanford as a professor in 1964. He became known for his work on how to design realistic laboratory experiments.

So the paper joins a famous theorist testing his own theory and a student at the start of his career. Cite both authors: Festinger and Carlsmith (1959). The paper thanks three others for help in designing and running the experiment, Leonard Hommel, Judson Mills and Robert Terwilliger, but it does not say who played which role.

## How psychologists explained opinions in the 1950s

An <Term>attitude</Term> is a person's lasting evaluation of something: liking or disliking, favouring or opposing it. An <Term>opinion</Term>, in the language of this paper, is a belief a person holds and could state, such as "the tasks were boring". Social psychologists in the 1950s spent a great deal of effort on how attitudes and opinions change, partly because governments, advertisers and armies all wanted to know how to change them.

The strongest school in American psychology at the time was <Term>behaviourism</Term>, which explains behaviour through its consequences. Its central idea is <Term>reinforcement</Term>: a consequence that makes a behaviour more likely to happen again. Money is a classic reinforcer. In behaviourist terms, the bigger the reward for doing something, the stronger its effect should be.

Applied to opinions, this gives a simple, common-sense prediction. If you pay someone to argue for a view they do not hold, a large payment should make the experience more rewarding, and so should move their opinion more than a small one. Gross calls this common-sense view <Term>incentive theory</Term>.

## Two earlier findings

Two lines of research set up the puzzle. The paper describes both in its first paragraphs.

**Saying it can change your mind.** In two studies, Irving Janis and Bert King (1954, 1956) asked people to make speeches arguing for a view they disagreed with. Students who had to improvise their own speech changed their private opinions more than students who only listened, or who read out a prepared speech. Janis and King explained this through <Term>mental rehearsal</Term>: while thinking up arguments and saying them, the speaker convinces himself.

**A strange result with rewards.** Herbert Kelman (1953) tried to go further. He reasoned that a bigger reward for making a statement against your own opinion should produce more opinion change. His data showed the reverse: a large reward produced less change than a smaller one. But Festinger and Carlsmith point out a weakness. In Kelman's study, not everyone made the statement, and more people agreed when the reward was bigger. So the people who complied were a different mix in each condition, and "no interpretation of the data can be unequivocal" (p. 203). Unequivocal means having only one possible meaning.

## A new idea: cognitive dissonance

Festinger's theory starts from a simple observation. People like their thoughts to fit together. A <Term>cognition</Term>, in Festinger's sense, is any piece of knowledge a person has: a belief, an opinion, or knowledge of something they did. When two cognitions do not fit, for example "smoking causes cancer" and "I smoke", Festinger said the person experiences <Term>cognitive dissonance</Term>, an unpleasant state of tension. The word dissonance comes from music, where it means notes that clash. People are then motivated to reduce the dissonance, for example by changing one of the cognitions.

Festinger was not the first to say that people seek consistency. Fritz Heider's balance theory and Charles Osgood and Percy Tannenbaum's congruity theory, from the 1940s and 1950s, made related claims. What was new was the idea that inconsistency works like a drive, as hunger does, and the bold, testable predictions Festinger drew from it.

<Note title="An idea that began partly with rumours in India">
Hock says the theory "grew out of" rumours that spread "throughout India" after a 1934 earthquake. The truth is more specific. In the foreword to his 1957 book, Festinger said the idea began with a puzzle in data on rumours after the great Bihar-Nepal earthquake of January 1934. The data came from a study by the Indian psychologist Jamuna Prasad (1935), although the foreword does not name him (Stenner, 2025). Many rumours predicted that even worse earthquakes were coming. Why would frightened people spread frightening stories? Festinger suggested that the rumours were not "anxiety provoking" but "anxiety justifying" (as quoted by Harmon-Jones, 2000): they gave people a reason for the fear they already felt. His biographer Stanley Schachter (1994) describes the theory as growing mainly out of Festinger's earlier work on group pressure and social comparison, so the rumours were one starting point, not the only one. The [India page](/cases/cognitive-dissonance/india/) looks at this history more closely.
</Note>

## The puzzle this study addresses

The theory makes a prediction about exactly the situation in Kelman's study. Suppose you say something you do not believe, and you were paid to say it. The payment is a good reason for what you said, so it reduces the clash between "I believe X" and "I said not-X". A big payment explains your behaviour completely. A tiny payment hardly explains it at all, which leaves you with a lot of dissonance. The easiest way out is to decide that you did, in fact, mostly believe what you said.

So the theory predicts that **a small reward will change opinions more than a large one**. That is the reverse of the reward-based prediction. Festinger and Carlsmith designed an experiment to test it cleanly, avoiding the self-selection problem in Kelman's study by making sure that, in the groups they analysed, every paid student told the same lie.

A reward-based account struggles here. The paid student received more reward, so if anything she should feel more positive about the job. The account has no clear reason why the unpaid volunteer would come to value dull work.

Dissonance theory explains it directly. The volunteer holds two cognitions that clash: "I chose to spend six hours on this" and "it was boring". She has no outside reason, such as money, to explain her choice. She reduces the dissonance by changing her view of the work: it was "a great experience". The paid student has a strong outside reason, the ₹2,000, so there is little dissonance and no need to change her opinion. This is the logic of the $1 and $20 conditions.
