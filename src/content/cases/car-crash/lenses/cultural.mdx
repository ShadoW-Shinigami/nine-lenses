## The lens in brief

Cultural psychology studies how culture and mind shape each other. <Term>Culture</Term> here means the shared practices, values, beliefs, languages and ways of living that a group passes on. Cultural psychologists argue that many "basic" psychological processes, including memory, take different forms in different cultures.

The idea that memory is cultural is old. Frederic Bartlett's *Remembering* (1932), which textbooks treat as the starting point for this study, was partly a cultural argument. His English participants changed an unfamiliar Native American story to fit English conventions of what a story should be. People remember, Bartlett suggested, through the habits and expectations of their group.

Two further ideas help here. The <Term>individualistic</Term> and <Term>collectivistic</Term> distinction describes broad differences in whether a culture stresses the person as a separate individual or as a member of groups. And <Term>linguistic relativity</Term> is the idea that the language a person speaks can influence how they notice, think about and remember events.

The study is far from this lens. It treats memory as a process that works the same way in every person, and it never mentions the language, nationality or background of its students.

## How this lens reads the study

**The effect rests on English verbs.** The manipulation was a set of five English verbs: *smashed*, *collided*, *bumped*, *hit* and *contacted*. The paper explains its choice with the work of the linguist Charles Fillmore (1971): English verbs of impact carry information about speed and about likely consequences (p. 586). Other languages have their own verbs for collisions, and they divide up meaning in their own ways. A translator working in Hindi, Tamil or Bengali would have to choose among them, and no study has tested whether any of them carries the same sense of violence as *smashed*. The finding is about how one language shapes memory, and it cannot simply be translated.

**Language shapes what witnesses notice and remember.** <Term>Agentive language</Term> names a person as the doer of an action, as in "He broke the glass", rather than "The glass broke". Caitlin Fausey and Lera Boroditsky (2011) showed English and Spanish speakers videos of people doing things on purpose or by accident. For intentional events the two groups spoke alike. But for accidental events, "English speakers used more agentive language than did Spanish speakers". English speakers also "remembered the agents of accidental events better than did Spanish speakers". A separate study by Fausey, Bria Long, Aya Inamori and Boroditsky (2010) found the same pattern for English and Japanese speakers. It also found that changing the wording used during a laboratory task "changed people's eye-witness memory". The car crash study showed that a questioner's language can change memory. This research suggests that the witness's own language may do so as well.

**Notice the wording of the question.** The speed question asked how fast "the cars" were going when "they" smashed into "each other". Nobody is blamed. Neither driver is the subject of the sentence. Fausey and Boroditsky (2010) found that agentive descriptions increased blame and the fines people recommended, even when they had watched the video themselves. A version of the car crash study asking "How fast was the blue car going when its driver smashed into the red car?" would test a different and, in court, more important question: whose fault was it?

**Speed is judged against local experience.** The students gave speeds in miles per hour, judging American cars in American driver's education films (p. 586). On Indian roads, speed is measured in kilometres per hour. The averages in the study convert to about 51 km/h for *contacted* and about 66 km/h for *smashed*. Traffic is different too. In India, two-wheeler riders made up the largest share of road deaths in 2023 (Ministry of Road Transport and Highways, 2025). A "smash" between a bus and a motorcycle, in mixed traffic, is a different event from two American sedans meeting at a crossroads. People judge speed partly from what they already know about vehicles, as one later study found (Raghunath and colleagues, 2021), and what people know comes from the traffic they live with. The [India page](/cases/car-crash/india/) has the data.

**What counts as the main detail differs.** Takahiko Masuda and Richard Nisbett (2001) found that Japanese participants describing scenes "made more statements about contextual information and relationships" than American participants. They also recognised objects better when the objects were shown in their original setting. For a witness, this suggests that what counts as the "central" detail of an accident, the cars or the people and setting around them, may differ between cultures. The study asked only about the cars.

**Remembering can be a shared and moral activity.** Qi Wang (2001) compared 256 American and Chinese students. Americans' earliest memories were "lengthy, specific, self-focused, and emotionally elaborate". Chinese students' memories centred "on collective activities, general routines, and emotionally neutral events". In another study, Chinese adults "more frequently drew upon past events to convey moral messages" (Wang and Conway, 2004). The car crash study treats memory as a private record of facts. In some cultures, memory is also something a family or community builds and uses together.

## What this lens values in the study

The study's procedure is simple, cheap and portable. It can be repeated in any language, with any film, which makes it an ideal tool for cross-cultural testing. Its central idea, that words shape memory, also opens the door to the cultural question: if one English verb matters, then the whole language a witness speaks may matter. The paper's subtitle, "the interaction between language and memory", is a cultural lens's starting point.

## What it would question

- Treating 195 students in one American study, described only as "students", as evidence about "a witness" in general.
- The assumption that verbs of collision mean the same thing, and carry the same force, in every language.
- Questions with no agent, when real legal questions are usually about who did what.
- Speed in miles per hour and American cars as a universal test of memory.
- The absence of any information about the students' language, background or driving experience.

## Evidence from this tradition

**Language and witness memory.** Fausey and Boroditsky (2011, English and Spanish) and Fausey, Long, Inamori and Boroditsky (2010, English and Japanese) show that the grammar a language favours for accidents shapes what speakers remember about who caused them. Fausey and Boroditsky (2010) show that the same kind of wording changes blame.

**Autobiographical memory.** Wang (2001) and Wang and Conway (2004) show that what people remember, and why they remember it, differs between American and Chinese participants.

**Attention to context.** Masuda and Nisbett (2001) show that what people describe and recognise in a scene depends partly on culture.

**Witnesses in Ghana.** Anakwah and colleagues (2020) tested 200 <Term>mock witnesses</Term> from rural Ghana, urban Ghana and the Netherlands. Mock witnesses are participants who watch a staged event and are then questioned as if they were real witnesses. Witnesses from the more individualistic culture "reported the most details". All groups reported more correct central details when the crime took place in a setting familiar to them. In a later study comparing Ghana and the United Kingdom, there was no cultural difference in free recall, but "participants from the collectivistic culture endorsed more misleading items in the recognition task" (Anakwah and colleagues, 2024). This is the closest test of the misinformation effect in a Global South culture that this guide found. The authors stress "eliminating leading or suggestive questioning".

**Evidence against a strongly cultural reading.** The ten-country study of co-witness contamination described on the [social lens](/cases/car-crash/lenses/social/) page found the effect in every sample, including 40 students in Bengaluru (Ito and colleagues, 2019). That suggests the basic process of absorbing information after an event is not limited to one culture. The cultural lens should claim that culture changes the size and form of the effect, and what it is about, not that the effect exists only in the West.

<Note title="Converting the speeds">
One mile per hour is about 1.609 km/h. The average estimates in Experiment 1 become: *contacted* 31.8 mph, about 51 km/h; *hit* 34.0 mph, about 55 km/h; *bumped* 38.1 mph, about 61 km/h; *collided* 39.3 mph, about 63 km/h; *smashed* 40.8 mph, about 66 km/h. The 9 mph gap between *contacted* and *smashed* is about 14.5 km/h. These conversions are this guide's.
</Note>

## A study this lens would design

1. Film a minor collision on an Indian road, for example between an auto-rickshaw and a two-wheeler, with no injuries, at a known speed.
2. Ask native speakers of Hindi, Tamil and English to list the verbs they would use for this kind of collision, and to rate each verb for how violent it sounds.
3. Choose a mild and a violent verb in each language, matched as closely as possible on these ratings.
4. Show the film to students and to experienced drivers in three cities. Ask for speed in km/h, using the mild or the violent verb in the participant's first language.
5. Test bilingual participants in both languages, a week apart, to see whether the same person is affected differently in each language.
6. A week later, ask in the same language whether they saw a detail that was not there, such as a fallen side mirror.

**Prediction:** if the effect is universal, violent verbs will raise speed estimates and false reports in every language, by similar amounts. If it depends on language and experience, the size of the effect will vary with how strongly each language marks violence, and drivers, who know real speeds, will be affected less than students.

The difference may come partly from language. Fausey and Boroditsky (2011) found that speakers of different languages describe accidental events differently, some naming a person as the doer more often than others, and that speakers who name the doer more often later remember the doer better. If the Bengali description Rituparna used put less focus on the driver as the person who caused the collision, she may have paid less attention to him, both in describing and in remembering. The car crash study showed that a questioner's verb can change memory. Here, the language of the question, and the language the witness answers in, may each play a part. This is an interpretation for discussion, since no study has tested Bengali.

For India this matters because witnesses are often questioned in one language and recorded in another, and many people are bilingual. A police statement written in English from a witness who spoke Hindi or Bengali passes through a translator's choice of words, and each choice can shift what is remembered and how blame is understood. The cultural lens would recommend questioning witnesses in the language they think in, recording their exact words, and noting where translation was used.
