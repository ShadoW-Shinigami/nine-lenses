The outline asks you to choose two of these roles. This guide covers all seven, so you can see how different the redesigns become when the person's concerns change. Each page has the same order: what this person cares about, what worries them about the original, their redesign, why, and what the redesign gives up.

The original study used university students watching films, not real witnesses. So several roles here look beyond the laboratory, to the places where questions are actually put to witnesses in India: police stations, courts, clinics, classrooms and neighbourhoods after an accident. The parent here is the parent of a child who may one day have to tell the police or a court what they saw. The teacher is someone who runs this study as a classroom demonstration.

## The seven redesigns side by side

| Stakeholder | Their main question | Biggest change to the study | What it costs |
|---|---|---|---|
| Participant | Was I told enough, and was I left with a false belief? | Information sheet, content warning about crash films, right to stop, and a debriefing that corrects the broken glass "memory" | Warned viewers may watch differently, and some may stop |
| Parent | Would my child's memory survive the questions police and courts ask? | A study with children aged 7 to 14, a harmless staged event, open questions against leading ones, an "I don't know" option, a trusted adult present | Far from the events that matter most, such as abuse |
| Teacher | Can I run this in class without misleading or pressuring my students? | Ethics review, a voluntary and anonymous class demonstration with an equal alternative, a class prediction before the results, and data pooled across years | A class-sized study may not "work", which needs explaining |
| Clinician | Do my own questions shape what clients remember, and is this research being used against survivors? | A study of clinicians' question wording with real, personally meaningful but non-traumatic memories, with distress measured | No film, so no known truth to check accuracy against |
| Researcher | Is the verb effect real, and how large is it? | A large, preregistered, multi-site replication in several languages, with pairwise tests, "don't know" answers and coded free accounts | Cost, coordination and a smaller effect by design |
| Policymaker | How should police and courts in India question witnesses? | Trials of audio-video recording and open-question training for witness statements under BNSS s. 180 | Money, training time and new privacy risks |
| Community leader | Will people come forward after an accident, and will gossip change what they remember? | A community study of co-witness discussion in local languages, and guidance co-designed with residents | Less control, and advice that may feel unnatural |

In the table, a <Term>preregistered</Term> study is one whose plan is published before any data are collected. A <Term>co-witness</Term> is another person who saw the same event. BNSS is the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023, India's new law of criminal procedure.

## Choosing your two

The best pairs for the journal are two roles that **disagree**. On these pages the sharpest disagreements are about what it costs to protect participants, about where witnesses should be questioned, and about whether to study films or real memories.

- **Participant and researcher** disagree about how far the study should bend to protect the people in it. The participant wants a content warning, the right to stop without losing credit, and mild films. Staged crashes with no visible people, or minor collisions, would be enough. The researcher wants staged crash films at several known speeds, so the study can measure accuracy as well as the verb effect. The participant page admits what its own protections cost. Warned viewers may watch more carefully, students who stop can leave the groups unequal, and the debrief may spread to people who have not yet taken part. The researcher's design is built to control or detect these problems. Every room contains every condition, and a final check finds out who guessed the purpose.
- **Policymaker and community leader** disagree about where witnesses should be questioned, and about where the danger to memory comes from. The policymaker would test video recording of statements in serious cases and road accidents, in police stations chosen at random, and early statements before a magistrate. For the policymaker, the questions that matter most may come from the first officer at the scene. The community leader points out that bystanders already fear being summoned to a police station. The Good Samaritan guidelines say that a helper who agrees to be a witness should be examined once, at a time and place of their convenience. For the community leader, the strongest suggestions come from other people at the scene, from family, from local news and from social media. Their answer is guidance written with residents, such as noting down what you saw before you talk to others.
- **Clinician and researcher** disagree about what kind of memory is worth studying. The researcher keeps films, because a film gives a known truth to check answers against, and accepts that the study is "still a laboratory". The clinician wants real memories that matter to the person, such as a recent minor road accident with a police or insurance report. The clinician accepts that such a report is itself written from memory, so there is no perfect record of the truth. The clinician also measures distress and whether people felt believed. In therapy, an accurate technique that leaves people feeling disbelieved is not acceptable.

Showing how each would redesign the study, and where their redesigns conflict, demonstrates the perspective-taking the outline describes as "especially powerful".

The superintendent's plan follows from the study. A video record captures the witness's own words at an early point, before later questions, conversations or news reports can change them. It also shows exactly which questions were asked, so a court can see whether a leading question such as "How fast was the truck going when it smashed into the car?" shaped the answer. The new criminal procedure law allows statements to be recorded by audio-video means (BNSS s. 180).

The residents' leader is also right. The Good Samaritan guidelines, which the Supreme Court gave force to in 2016, say that bystanders who help must not be forced to become witnesses. If they choose to be witnesses, they should be examined once, at a time and place of their convenience, by an officer in plain clothes. A compromise is to keep the recording but move it to the witness: a short, single, recorded statement taken at the scene or at the witness's home, with consent, using open questions. That protects the memory and the witness, and it may even save witnesses repeated visits to the station, because one good record is enough. The cost is more equipment, more training, and a rule that some witnesses can still decline to be filmed.
