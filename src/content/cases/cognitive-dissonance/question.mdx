## The research question

The paper opens with its question in one sentence:

> What happens to a person's private opinion if he is forced to do or say something contrary to that opinion? (p. 203)

Earlier studies had shown that a person's private opinion often moves towards what they were made to say. The open question was *why*, and in particular what the size of the reward does. If you pay someone to say something they do not believe, does a bigger payment produce more change in their opinion, or less?

## The two hypotheses

A <Term>hypothesis</Term> is a specific, testable prediction. The paper calls its predictions "derivations" from the theory, because they are worked out logically from it. Its summary states two (pp. 209-210). In plain words:

**1. Saying it moves the opinion.** If a person is persuaded to say or do something that goes against their private opinion, their opinion will tend to change so that it fits better with what they said or did.

**2. Bigger pressure, smaller change.** The more pressure used to get the person to say it, beyond the minimum needed, the weaker this change will be. Pressure here means rewards offered or punishments threatened.

For this experiment, the second hypothesis becomes one concrete prediction: "the larger the reward given to the subject, the smaller will be the subsequent opinion change" (p. 204). So students paid $1 to tell a lie should end up believing it more than students paid $20.

Notice that this is the opposite of what most people expect. It is also the opposite of what reward-based theories of the time predicted, as the [previous page](/cases/cognitive-dissonance/start-here/) explained. A prediction that goes against common sense is called <Term>counter-intuitive</Term>. When a counter-intuitive prediction turns out to be right, it is strong support for the theory that made it, because no other obvious explanation predicted it.

## The theory behind the predictions

The theory is Festinger's <Term>cognitive dissonance</Term> theory, published as a book in 1957. The paper gives a short version in four steps (pp. 203-204). Here it is in plain words.

### Step 1: two thoughts that do not fit

Festinger uses the word <Term>cognition</Term> for any piece of knowledge a person has about themselves or the world: a belief, an opinion, or the knowledge of something they did. Two cognitions are <Term>dissonant</Term> when one does not fit with the other. They are <Term>consonant</Term> when they fit together.

Imagine a student who privately thinks the tasks were boring, but has just told someone they were fun. He now holds two cognitions: "I think the tasks were boring" and "I said they were fun". Normally, a person who believes X says X. So these two cognitions are dissonant. The paper adds a careful phrase here: the two would not fit "at least in our culture" (p. 203). Keep that phrase in mind for Section D.

### Step 2: reasons for the act fit with the act

The student also has cognitions about *why* he said it. "I was paid" and "the experimenter needed help" both fit with "I said they were fun". These reasons are consonant with the act.

### Step 3: the total amount of dissonance

How much discomfort does the student feel? Festinger suggests weighing both kinds of cognition. If D is the sum of the dissonant ones and C is the sum of the consonant ones, the total amount of dissonance depends on D divided by D plus C. More good reasons for the act means a bigger C, so less dissonance. The amount of dissonance is greatest when the reward is "just barely sufficient" to get the person to act (p. 204). Any reward above that only reduces it.

### Step 4: reducing dissonance

Festinger treats dissonance as unpleasant, a state people are motivated to reduce, rather as hunger motivates people to eat. One way to reduce it is to change one of the cognitions. The student cannot undo what he said. But he can change his private opinion: "Actually, the tasks were not that bad." Because the push to reduce dissonance grows with the amount of dissonance, the student with the weakest reason for lying, the one paid $1, should change his opinion the most.

<Note title="The theory names other ways out">
Changing your opinion is not the only way to reduce dissonance. You can also add new consonant cognitions, for example by persuading yourself that the reason you were given was more important than it seemed. The paper tests this too. It asks how scientifically important the students thought the experiment was, because a student who decides the experiment really matters has a better reason for having helped it (p. 208). You will see the result on the [findings page](/cases/cognitive-dissonance/findings/).
</Note>

<Note title="Why the word &quot;forced&quot; misleads">
The paper's title says "forced compliance", but nobody was forced. The experimenter asked politely for a favour and offered money, and three students refused (p. 207). Later research showed that this sense of free choice matters a great deal: when people feel they had no choice, the pattern reverses, and larger payments produce more change. For this reason, later researchers usually call the method the <Term>induced compliance</Term> paradigm. A paradigm, in this sense, is a standard experimental procedure that many studies reuse.
</Note>

## The variables

An experiment changes some things and measures others. The thing the researcher changes is the <Term>independent variable</Term>. The thing measured is the <Term>dependent variable</Term>.

| Variable | Type | Levels or measure in this study |
|---|---|---|
| Payment for telling the waiting woman the tasks were enjoyable | Independent variable, set by the researchers | $1, $20, or not asked at all (Control) |
| How enjoyable the student now says the tasks were | Main dependent variable | A rating from -5 (extremely dull and boring) to +5 (extremely interesting and enjoyable) |
| Desire to take part in a similar experiment | Dependent variable, less direct | A rating from -5 to +5 |
| How scientifically important the experiment was | Dependent variable, a possible second route for reducing dissonance | A rating from 0 to 10 |
| How much the student learned about his own ability | Dependent variable, expected not to change | A rating from 0 to 10 |
| What the student said to the woman | A check on an alternative explanation | Five ratings of the tape recordings, made by two raters |

The Control group is not paid and never tells anyone anything. It shows what students who did the same boring hour, and heard the same false explanation, thought of the tasks. Every result is compared with it.

It was not obvious at the time. Reward-based thinking said that a bigger reward should strengthen whatever it rewards, and Kelman (1953) had reasoned in just this way that a bigger reward should produce more opinion change. His own data hinted at the reverse, but his design could not settle the question. A prediction that a smaller reward produces more change was surprising, which is why a result confirming it was taken as strong support for the theory.

Your friend's explanation is close to step 3 of the theory. A student paid $20 has a large, obvious reason for what he said, so there is little dissonance left to reduce. The theory adds one more step: the $1 student lacks such a reason, so he is pushed to find another way to make his words fit his thoughts, and changing his opinion is the easiest.
</Try>
