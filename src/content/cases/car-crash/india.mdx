This page follows the questions in Section D, part 7, of the outline. It tries to be specific. "India" is not one culture: it includes cities and villages, many languages and religions, castes and classes, and very different kinds of roads, police stations and courts. Treat every statement below as a starting point for your own thinking, not as a fact about all Indians.

## An idea Indian law already knew

The study's starting point, that a question can suggest its own answer, was written into Indian law a century before 1974. The Indian Evidence Act of 1872 defined a <Term>leading question</Term> as "Any question suggesting the answer which the person putting it wishes or expects to receive" (section 141). It said such questions may not be asked in examination-in-chief, the first questioning of a witness by the side that called them, if the other side objects, but may be asked in cross-examination (sections 142 and 143).

The Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam (BSA), which replaced the 1872 Act from 1 July 2024, keeps these rules almost word for word in one section, section 146. Cases registered before that date generally continue under the old Act, so you will meet both sets of section numbers in judgments. This page gives both.

So Loftus and Palmer's question, "About how fast were the cars going when they smashed into each other?", is a leading question in the legal sense. Indian law already limited such questions. What the study added was evidence that a leading question might change more than the answer: it might change what the witness remembers later. Indian law's own history with this idea is more complicated than it looks, and the [decolonial lens](/cases/car-crash/lenses/decolonial/) explores it. The man who drafted the 1872 Act, James Fitzjames Stephen, wrote that it is "difficult" for an untrained person "to distinguish between what they themselves saw and heard and what they were told by others" (Stephen, 1872, pp. 43-44), which is close to Loftus and Palmer's own conclusion.

## What assumptions seem Western?

**One witness, one questionnaire, one language.** Each student in the study answered alone, on a printed form, in English, a few minutes or a week after the event. In India, a witness is usually questioned by a police officer, later perhaps by a magistrate, and years later by lawyers in court. The questions may be asked in one language, written down in another and read out in court in a third. The study tested one suggestive word, once. It did not test a chain of retellings and translations.

**A neutral questioner.** In the study, the "questioner" was a printed sheet from a university researcher. Nothing depended on the answer. In India, the person asking a witness about an accident is often a police officer. The law says that a person examined by the police "shall be bound to answer truly". This is section 180(2) of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS), formerly section 161 of the Code of Criminal Procedure (CrPC). A question from someone with this kind of power may carry more weight than a printed word.

**American roads, American cars, miles per hour.** The students judged sedans on American roads in miles per hour. Indian roads carry a mix of cars, buses, trucks, auto-rickshaws, bicycles, pedestrians and above all two-wheelers, and speed is judged in kilometres per hour. In 2023, two-wheeler riders made up 44.8% of road deaths in India and pedestrians 20.4% (Ministry of Road Transport and Highways, 2025). A "smash" between a bus and a scooter is a different picture from a "smash" between two sedans.

**The witness as a private individual.** The study treated memory as something inside one person's head, to be measured without anyone else present. Many Indian witnesses see an event with others, talk about it with neighbours and family, and give their account in a crowd. The study's design removed exactly these social influences.

## What experiences may be missing?

**An invented scene, to think with.** A minor collision, with a small dent and nobody hurt. Notice how many people are talking at once, and that only one of them is writing. Whose words will end up in the constable's notebook? Each bystander has heard the others' versions before saying anything to him, and the notebook will hold his summary, not their words.
</Figure>

**Many witnesses, talking together.** At a typical Indian road accident, several people gather and discuss what happened before anyone official arrives. Researchers call the effect of this <Term>co-witness contamination</Term>: details one witness hears from another can become part of their own report. A study run in ten countries found this effect in every country, including India, where the sample was 40 students at the National Institute of Mental Health and Neurosciences (NIMHANS) in Bengaluru (Ito and colleagues, 2019). Errors were especially likely when a witness "had not disputed" what the other person said. Whether Indian families and neighbourhoods discuss events more than people elsewhere has not been studied, so treat that as a question, not a finding.

**A statement written by someone else.** When the police question a witness, the officer "may reduce into writing" what the witness says (BNSS section 180(3), formerly CrPC section 161(3)). The witness does not sign it (BNSS section 181, formerly CrPC section 162), and it cannot be used as evidence at trial, only to show that the witness has contradicted themselves. So the first written record of a witness's memory is usually the officer's version, in the officer's words, sometimes in a different language from the one the witness spoke. Since 2024 the law allows the statement to be "recorded by audio-video electronic means" (BNSS section 180(3)). The 1974 study isolated one word chosen by the questioner. The Indian process can give the questioner control over every word.

**Years of waiting and retelling.** In the study, the longest delay was one week. In India, witnesses often testify years after the event. On 8 November 2026, the National Judicial Data Grid showed more than 4 crore (40 million) criminal cases pending in India's district courts, and more than half had been pending for over a year. Over those years, a witness may tell the story many times, to family, to police, to lawyers, to journalists. Each retelling is a chance for information from after the event to enter the memory.

<Note title="The court backlog in numbers">
National Judicial Data Grid, district courts dashboard, accessed 8 November 2026: 4,09,15,273 criminal cases pending (about 40.9 million). Of these, about 59% had been pending for more than a year, about 25.5% for more than five years, and about 9.5% for more than ten years (this guide's calculations from the dashboard). These are the ages of pending cases, not the exact gap between an event and a witness's testimony. But they show that long delays are common. The figures change daily.
</Note>

**Fear of getting involved.** Many Indian bystanders hesitate to help at road accidents, or to become witnesses, because they fear police visits and court appearances. The Supreme Court has addressed this twice. In *SaveLIFE Foundation v. Union of India* (2016), it gave force to government guidelines protecting "Good Samaritans", people who help accident victims. If a Good Samaritan chooses to be a witness, the guidelines say the police should question them at a time and place that suits them, with the officer in plain clothes, and with an interpreter if needed. If they come to the police station, they are to be examined "in a single examination". In 2019, Parliament added section 134A to the Motor Vehicles Act, which protects Good Samaritans from civil or criminal liability for the help they give. In a further judgment in 2026, the Court noted that a bystander often "hesitates: suffers a reactive paralysis, sometimes due to fear of legal proceedings, of getting summoned to the police station as a witness". From a memory point of view, a single, early, recorded interview is also good practice: it limits the repeated questioning that gives misleading information a way in.

**News and social media.** Witnesses may see a case on television or on their phones before they testify. The Law Commission of India's report on *Trial by Media* (2006) noted that "victims and potential witnesses are also interviewed by the news channels" (pp. 82-83). It quoted a New Zealand judge's warning that a witness who sees an accused person's photograph in the news "may quite unconsciously be led into the belief that the accused as photographed is the person they saw" (p. 90). That is post-event information on a national scale.

**Child witnesses.** The study tested young adults. Children are more open to suggestion than adults (Ceci and Bruck, 1993), and many Indian criminal cases depend on them. The Protection of Children from Sexual Offences (POCSO) Act, 2012, builds in protections that match the study's lessons. The child's statement must be recorded "as spoken by the child" (sections 25 and 26), and where possible by audio-video means. In court, lawyers must "communicate the questions to be put to the child to the Special Court which shall in turn put those questions to the child" (section 33(2)). The court must ensure that the child "is not called repeatedly to testify" (section 33(5)).

## Would the findings hold in India?

The answer has three parts.

**The general process very probably occurs.** The Indian evidence is small, but it points the same way as research elsewhere.

- In the ten-country study described above, co-witness contamination appeared in the Indian sample as in every other (Ito and colleagues, 2019).
- At the University of Delhi, Sondhi and Gupta (2007) showed 36 participants a live 20-second event and then gave them misleading information. Participants went on to report suggested details, and "the suggested memories were as confidently held as accurate memories".
- In a study run at Banaras Hindu University, Tiwari (2011) showed 120 participants aged 18 to 25 a video of a murder. A week later, some read a summary containing misleading information. "Participants who were misled retained less number of details", and participants in states of high tension or anger were affected more.
- At Pondicherry University, Srinivasan and Ganth (2017) reported that misleading questions "were able to create false memories", and that changing the adjectives in questions changed people's judgements of how much time had passed. This guide could read only the abstract.

**The specific verb effect has not been tested in India.** This guide found no Indian replication of the speed and verb experiments. Remember too that, outside India, the exact verb effect has not been reliably replicated since 1974, as the [importance page](/cases/car-crash/importance/) explains. A replication in India would need to use Indian languages and verbs, kilometres per hour, and Indian traffic. Hindi, Tamil, Bengali and other languages each have their own range of words for one vehicle striking another, and nobody has tested whether they shift estimates in the same way.

**A Global South comparison suggests culture may change the form of the effect, not whether it exists.** Anakwah and colleagues (2024) tested witnesses in Ghana and the United Kingdom. They found no cultural difference in how much misleading information people repeated when they described the event freely. But when witnesses were asked to recognise details, "participants from the collectivistic culture endorsed more misleading items". A <Term>collectivistic</Term> culture is one in which people see themselves mainly as members of groups such as the family, rather than as separate individuals. This is one study in one country, and India is not Ghana. It suggests a question worth testing: do Indian witnesses accept suggestions more readily when they are asked yes-or-no questions, like the broken glass question, than when they tell the story in their own words?

<SourceCheck title="Source check: an Indian paper on the study">
Tiwari (2011, p. 535) describes this study as "Loftus and Loftus (1975)" and says that the group asked about cars that "smashed into each other" "distinctly remembered having seen the broken glass scattered about the road". Both points are wrong. The study is Loftus and Palmer (1974); Loftus (1975) is a different paper, about barns and stop signs. And only 16 of the 50 *smashed* students, 32%, said they had seen broken glass. The question asked only "yes" or "no", so nobody described glass "scattered about the road". If you use Indian secondary sources, check citations against the original.
</SourceCheck>

**Indian courts already accept that memory is not a recording.** In *Bharwada Bhoginbhai Hirjibhai v. State of Gujarat* (1983), the Supreme Court said that a witness "cannot be expected to possess a photographic memory". It added: "It is not as if a video tape is replayed on the mental screen". And: "It is unrealistic to expect a witness to be a human tape recorder". On time and duration, it said people "make their estimates by guess work on the spur of the moment" (1983 SCR (3) 280, at 286-287). This is close to Loftus and Palmer's starting point, that people are "markedly inaccurate" at judging "time, speed, and distance" (p. 585). Notice, though, how the Court used the idea: to set aside "minor discrepancies" in a witness's account and uphold a conviction. The same judgment also rests on broad claims about how "a girl or a woman in India" would behave, which were cultural assertions, not data. Memory science can be used both to doubt a witness and to defend one. The [critical lens](/cases/car-crash/lenses/critical/) asks who decides which.

## What cultural factors matter?

| Factor | Why it matters for this study |
|---|---|
| Language and translation | A question may be asked in one language, recorded in another and read in court in a third. Each translation can change a verb like "hit" or "smashed". The BNSS sets rules for court languages, translation and reading evidence back to the witness (sections 307 to 314), but no Indian study has tested how translation affects witness memory |
| Who asks the question | A police officer to whom the witness is "bound to answer truly" may carry more authority than a printed questionnaire. One later study found that leading questions mattered less when people thought the questioner was biased (Dodd and Bradshaw, 1980), so trust in the police could make the effect larger or smaller |
| How statements are recorded | The officer writes the statement in their own words, and the witness does not sign it. Audio-video recording, now allowed, would keep the witness's own words |
| Time before trial | Witnesses may testify years later, after many retellings. The study tested a delay of one week |
| Talk among witnesses and families | Co-witness contamination was found in an Indian sample. Crowds at accident scenes, and family discussion afterwards, give it room to work |
| Traffic and speed | Mixed traffic, two-wheelers and kilometres per hour make speed judgements different from the study's sedans in miles per hour |
| Fear of involvement | Bystanders may avoid being witnesses at all, or give hurried accounts, because of fear of police and courts |
| Media coverage | Television and social media can supply post-event information before a witness ever speaks to the police |
| Children and vulnerable witnesses | POCSO and the Supreme Court's 2022 directions on deposition centres try to reduce pressure and repeated questioning |
| Region, class, caste, gender and schooling | Who is believed, who can refuse a police officer, and who is comfortable in a courtroom differ widely across India |

The last-but-one row deserves a word. In *Smruti Tukaram Badade v. State of Maharashtra* (2022), the Supreme Court directed that every district court should have at least one permanent <Term>Vulnerable Witness Deposition Centre</Term>, a separate, protected space where vulnerable witnesses can give evidence. The Court wrote that the barriers to fair testimony "are not only those which exist within the physical spaces of conventional courts but those which operate on the minds and personality of vulnerable witnesses". That is a legal recognition that the setting of questioning shapes what a witness can say.

## What examples support or challenge the findings?

**Supporting: India's road accident data rest on memory.** India's official road accident statistics come from police reports. In 2023, police reported 4,80,583 road accidents, in which 1,72,890 people died (Ministry of Road Transport and Highways, 2025, p. 1). The ministry attributes 68.1% of these deaths to over-speeding (p. 39). But the same report lists weaknesses in the existing system (p. 105). The police officer who goes to the spot "would normally record the data after returning depending on his memory". The data are "often limited to the questions asked" in the official forms. And they are "often affected by the value judgement and biases of the policeman". Judgements of speed made after the event, partly from what witnesses say, are exactly the kind of number Loftus and Palmer showed to be fragile. This link is this guide's inference, not a claim the ministry makes.

<Note title="Road accidents in India, 2023">
Ministry of Road Transport and Highways, *Road Accidents in India 2023* (published 2025): 4,80,583 accidents (about 4.8 lakh), 1,72,890 deaths (about 1.7 lakh) and 4,62,825 injuries. Over-speeding was linked to 68.4% of accidents and 68.1% of deaths. Two-wheeler riders were 44.8% of those killed and pedestrians 20.4%. About two-thirds of victims were aged 18 to 45.
</Note>

**Supporting: Indian law already guards against the study's two routes to distortion.** Suggestive wording and repeated questioning are limited by the POCSO rules, the Good Samaritan guidelines and the new option of audio-video recording of police statements, if police use it. Indian courts, in *Bharwada*, also accept that a witness is not "a human tape recorder".

**Challenging: the specific effect is untested here.** No Indian study has repeated the speed and verb experiments. The only Indian study close to them, Srinivasan and Ganth (2017), is known to this guide only from its abstract. Claims that the verb effect "applies in India" go beyond the evidence.

**Challenging: not every change in testimony is a memory effect.** Indian courts often deal with <Term>hostile witnesses</Term>: witnesses who, in court, go back on what they told the police. The 1974 study offers one possible cause, a memory changed by questions and time. But witnesses may also change their story because of threats, pressure, payment or fear, or because the police statement never recorded their words accurately in the first place. The study cannot tell these causes apart, and Indian research has not measured how often each one happens.

**Challenging: courts may still trust a single confident witness.** In *Vadivelu Thevar v. State of Madras* (1957), the Supreme Court held that "The court is concerned with the quality and not with the quantity of the evidence", and that a conviction can rest on one witness who is "wholly reliable". The study suggests a caution here: a witness can be honest, confident and sincere, and still wrong in detail. Whether a witness is reliable as a person, and whether a particular memory is accurate, are different questions.

There are at least four points. First, the witness may have talked about the crash with other people at the tea stall before the police arrived, so his memory may include details he heard rather than saw: co-witness contamination. Second, the police statement is the sub-inspector's summary, not the witness's own words, and the witness did not sign it (BNSS section 181, formerly CrPC section 162). Third, three years of waiting means many retellings, each a chance for new information to enter the memory. Fourth, the lawyer's question uses "rammed", a violent verb, and it passes through an interpreter, who must choose a Tamil word for it. As in the *smashed* condition, the verb may push the speed estimate upward and may also shape how the witness remembers the crash.

Safeguards follow from each point. Witnesses can be separated and questioned early, before they discuss the event. The police statement can be recorded by audio-video means, as BNSS section 180(3) now allows, so the witness's own words are kept. Delays could be reduced, though that is a problem far beyond psychology. And because the question was asked in cross-examination, the law allows it to be leading (BSA section 146(4), formerly Evidence Act section 143). The judge, and the prosecutor in re-examination, can still help by asking the witness to describe the crash in his own words, and the interpreter can be asked to translate the verb neutrally.
</Try>
