The outline asks for at least three of each. Below are more than three, so you can choose the ones that fit your argument. For each one, the reason is given, because a strength or limitation without a reason earns little credit.

## Strengths

### 1. The key comparison changes only one thing

The $1 and $20 students did the same tasks, heard the same false explanation, were given the same sheet, told the same lie to the same woman, heard the same remark afterwards and answered the same interview. Only the amount of money differed. This makes the comparison between them a clean test of the theory's central prediction, that a larger reward produces less change of opinion. This is high <Term>internal validity</Term>, meaning confidence that the study measured a real cause and effect within its own setting.

### 2. A disguised, blind measure of opinion

The students gave their opinions to an interviewer who seemed to work for an unrelated department survey and who did not know their condition. They had been told in class, weeks before, to be completely honest in these interviews. This reduces two problems: students telling the experimenter what they thought he wanted to hear, and the interviewer's expectations colouring the answers. The friendly remark that "most of our subjects" found the tasks interesting was given to all three groups in the same words, so it could not explain differences between them.

### 3. A counter-intuitive prediction, stated in advance

The theory predicted a result that common sense and reward theories did not: that less money would mean more change. The prediction came from the theory before the experiment, and was not invented afterwards to fit the data. A theory that correctly predicts something surprising earns more support than one that predicts what everyone expected.

### 4. Checks that the effect was specific

The researchers predicted *no* difference on the question about how much students learned about their own abilities, because it had nothing to do with the lie. They found none. This shows that the $1 students were not simply more positive about everything. The effect appeared on the topics the lie touched and not elsewhere.

### 5. A rival explanation tested and ruled out

The authors anticipated the main rival explanation of the time, that the $1 students had argued harder and convinced themselves through rehearsal. They recorded every conversation and had two blind raters score it. The $20 students had argued slightly harder, if anything. Testing your own rivals in this way is good scientific practice.

### 6. High experimental realism, and a method others could reuse

The cover story, the embarrassed request and the doubtful woman made the situation feel real. Students had to decide, in the moment, whether to mislead another student. The procedure was described in enough detail to be repeated. The basic idea, that small inducements produce more attitude change than large ones when people feel they chose, was supported in many later studies using essays and speeches instead of a lie. Most of those studies were small, however, and Limitation 9 explains what a large modern test found.

## Limitations

### 1. Uneven exclusions

Eleven of the 71 students were left out of the analysis: seven from the One Dollar condition, four from Twenty Dollars and none from Control. Some reasons are understandable, such as suspecting the real purpose. But others, such as refusing the money or telling the woman the truth, describe students who resisted the lie. Removing them from the $1 group may have left a group of students who were especially willing to go along with the experimenter. Natalia and Alphonse Chapanis (1964), in a well-known critique of early dissonance research, are usually credited with raising this objection.

### 2. A small sample and weak supporting results

There were only 20 students in each group. The main result reached statistical significance, but the supporting results, on desire to take part again and on scientific importance, did not. The whole case rests on one question, rated once, by 60 people. The paper's claim that the results "strongly corroborate the theory" is stronger than its tables justify.

### 3. Dissonance was never measured

The study assumes the $1 students felt uncomfortable tension, but nobody measured it. Dissonance is inferred from the change in opinion, and the change in opinion is explained by dissonance. Critics call this reasoning <Term>circular</Term>. Later studies, such as Croyle and Cooper (1983), found that people writing counter-attitudinal essays under high choice showed more physical arousal, so something uncomfortable does seem to happen. But reviews of many studies (Kenworthy et al., 2011) and the 2024 multi-lab replication found that discomfort did not explain the attitude change. The 1959 study itself cannot show either way.

### 4. Other explanations fit the same data

The study rules out rehearsal, but not explanations proposed later. Self-perception theory says the $1 students simply inferred their attitude from their own behaviour, with no inner tension. Impression-management theory says they adjusted what they told the interviewer to look consistent, not what they privately thought. And Milton Rosenberg (1965) suggested that $20 was so large it may have seemed suspicious, perhaps a test of whether students could be bribed, so the $20 students may have held back from positive ratings to look honest. The design cannot separate these.

### 5. The experimenter was not blind

The experimenter knew each student's condition, and he ran the step most likely to differ between conditions: the hesitant request, with encouragement if a student hesitated. Without meaning to, he may have been warmer, more embarrassed or more persuasive in one condition. The paper does not report any check on this.

### 6. A narrow sample

All 71 participants were male introductory psychology students at one private American university, taking part to meet a course requirement. The paper does not report their ages, backgrounds or ethnicity. Women, older adults, people without university education and people from other cultures are absent. The paper itself notes that the clash between belief and statement exists "at least in our culture". Section D shows that later cross-cultural studies found dissonance effects that differ between cultures.

### 7. Low mundane realism and a trivial opinion

The students had no stake in whether a laboratory task was boring. Opinions people really care about, such as political, religious or moral views, may behave differently. And a payment of $20, about $240 in today's money, for two minutes' work is unlike most real situations. So the study has little <Term>external validity</Term>, meaning how far results apply outside the study, without further evidence.

### 8. Deception and ethics by today's standards

The study deceived students at every stage: a fake department survey announced in class, a false explanation, a fake absent assistant, a fake waiting participant, and a hidden tape recorder. It asked them to mislead a fellow student. Taking part was a course requirement, which limits how freely they could refuse. Students were debriefed and the money returned, which was good practice for 1959. Under current codes, such as the British Psychological Society's *Code of Human Research Ethics* and the American Psychological Association's ethics code, deception is allowed only when there is no reasonable alternative, and secret recording and course-credit requirements need special justification and alternatives.

### 9. The core effect has not held up in a large modern test

Festinger and Carlsmith had 20 people per group, and most later studies in this tradition were similar in size. In 2024, a registered replication in 39 laboratories tested the modern, choice-based version of the method and found that choice made no difference to attitude change (Vaidis et al., 2024). It was not a test of the $1 and $20 procedure itself, but it means the "less is more" effect should be taught as influential and uncertain, not as an established fact. The [importance page](/cases/cognitive-dissonance/importance/) gives the details.

<Note title="Weighing strengths against limitations">
Notice how the strengths and limitations connect. The deception that gives the study its realism and its disguised measure is also its main ethical problem. The tight focus on one comparison, $1 against $20, gives clean internal validity, but the Control group differs in two ways, and the effect rests on one question. Pointing out connections like these is what turns a list into an evaluation.
</Note>

A strong choice is "a disguised, blind measure of opinion" as the strength and "deception and ethics" as the limitation. The study's best protection against students telling the researchers what they wanted to hear was the fake department interview, which made the measure feel unconnected to the experiment. But that disguise was itself a deception, layered on several others.

So the connected argument is: the design gives a convincing measure of what students said in private, but only by misleading them, and the same method raises the question of whether a study that depends on deceiving people about honesty can be justified today. Another strong pair is "a counter-intuitive prediction" with "dissonance was never measured": the prediction came true, but the process that was supposed to produce it was inferred rather than observed.
</Try>
