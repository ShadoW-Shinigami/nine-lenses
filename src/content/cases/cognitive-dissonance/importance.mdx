The outline asks about three kinds of importance: the contribution to psychology, the influence on theory, and the influence on practice. This page takes them in that order, then looks at what later research did to the study's claims.

## Contribution to psychology

The study made one surprising point so clearly that it became one of the most taught experiments in psychology: **when people say something they do not believe, a small reward can change their private opinion more than a large one.** It turned a piece of common sense upside down. It also showed that attitudes do not only cause behaviour. Behaviour can cause attitudes.

It mattered for three further reasons.

- **It brought the mind back.** In 1959 American psychology was still dominated by behaviourism, which avoided talk of inner states. The study explained its result through an inner state, dissonance, and made a prediction that reward theory could not. It became an early landmark of the move towards cognitive explanations in social psychology.
- **It made "less is more" testable.** The idea that the minimum pressure needed to get someone to act produces the most change in their attitude was new, and it had practical consequences for parenting, teaching and persuasion.
- **It set a style of experiment.** The cover story, the staged request and the disguised measure became a model for social psychology experiments for decades. That style also brought the ethical problems discussed in Sections E and F.

The study was the opening move in a long argument about why the effect happens.

### Four ideas that came from the debate

**1. Choice is essential.** Later studies found that the "less is more" effect appears only when people feel they chose to act. When people are simply told to write an essay against their views, larger payments produce more change, as reward theory predicts (Linder, Cooper & Jones, 1967). The 1959 study had built choice in without saying so: the request was polite, and students could refuse.

**2. Consequences and responsibility matter.** Joel Cooper and Russell Fazio (1984) argued that the clash between belief and statement is not enough on its own. Dissonance arises when a person feels responsible for bringing about an unwanted outcome. In the 1959 study, the outcome was that a fellow student might be misled into a boring experiment. Cooper and Worchel (1970) repeated the procedure and told half the participants that the waiting person had not believed them. Only those who thought they had convinced the listener showed the "less is more" effect.

**3. The self is at stake.** Elliot Aronson, a student of Festinger's, argued that dissonance is strongest when behaviour threatens a person's view of themselves as decent, honest and sensible (Aronson, 1968). The $1 student's problem was more than "I said X but believe not-X". It was "I am an honest person, and I just misled someone for almost nothing." Claude Steele's self-affirmation theory (1988) went a step further: people who are reminded of other values they hold dear feel less need to change their attitudes.

**4. Is there really an inner tension?** Daryl Bem's self-perception theory (1967) said no: people simply infer their attitudes from their behaviour. Studies that measured arousal (Croyle & Cooper, 1983) or let people blame their discomfort on a pill (Zanna & Cooper, 1974) supported the idea that something uncomfortable does happen. Today most researchers accept that both processes exist, in different situations.

## Influence on practice

Dissonance theory moved out of the laboratory into health, therapy and marketing. Most of these uses rely on the later versions of the theory, especially the idea that people want to see themselves as consistent and honest.

**Hypocrisy and health.** Aronson and colleagues reasoned that if people are made to preach something and then reminded that they do not practise it, the clash with their self-image should push them to change their behaviour. In the first study, 80 sexually active young adults at the University of California, Santa Cruz, were split four ways. Half wrote a short speech urging condom use and delivered it to a video camera, believing it would be shown to high school students; the other half wrote the speech but only rehearsed it silently. Separately, half were asked to recall times they had failed to use condoms themselves. Those who did both, the "hypocrisy" group, admitted more past failures (Aronson, Fried & Stone, 1991). In a second study, more students in the hypocrisy condition bought condoms at the end of the session (Stone, Aronson, Crain, Winslow & Fried, 1994). A field study at a campus recreation centre found that students who urged others to save water and were then reminded of their own long showers took shorter showers (Dickerson, Thibodeau, Aronson & Miller, 1992). A meta-analysis of 38 studies found that hypocrisy procedures increase both intentions and behaviour, although simply recalling past failures sometimes worked too (Priolo et al., 2019).

**Preventing eating disorders.** The Body Project, developed by Eric Stice and colleagues, asks young women with body image concerns to argue against the thin ideal in their own words, in writing and in spoken exercises such as role plays. The idea is that voicing the argument creates dissonance with their own beliefs. A meta-analysis of 56 trials with 7,808 participants found improvements in body dissatisfaction and eating disorder symptoms. The effects were small to moderate against no intervention and smaller against other credible programmes (Stice, Marti, Shaw & Rohde, 2019). Two findings fit the theory awkwardly. In one large trial, a non-dissonance healthy-weight programme reduced the risk of eating problems by about as much as the dissonance programme. And in the meta-analysis, effects were larger when participants were *paid*, the opposite of what the $1 and $20 logic predicts.

<Note title="An Indian pilot">
A related dissonance-based body image programme, *Free Being Me*, was tested with 117 girls aged 11 to 14 in India's national guiding association, led by their own Guide leaders over five weekly sessions. Most girls found it comfortable and enjoyable, and their body esteem improved slightly. The study had no control group, so the authors call for a proper trial (Paraskeva et al., 2024).
</Note>

**Therapy.** Motivational interviewing, widely used for addiction, helps clients voice their own reasons for change rather than lecturing them. Its founders write that the value of "hearing oneself argue for change" was linked to Festinger's dissonance theory and to Bem's self-perception theory (Miller & Rose, 2009). Joel Cooper (1980) found that people afraid of snakes improved more when they had chosen to undergo an effortful treatment, whether it was a recognised therapy or a set of physical exercises with no therapeutic purpose. This is effort justification applied to therapy.

**Smoking, in India and elsewhere.** Smokers often hold "self-exempting" beliefs such as "I don't smoke enough for it to harm me", which reduce the clash between smoking and knowing it is dangerous (Peretti-Watel, Halfen & Grémy, 2007). An Indian study followed 1,112 smokers of cigarettes and bidis from the Tobacco Control Policy survey across two waves. Those who had quit by the second survey believed less in smoking's supposed benefits, such as calming stress, than those still smoking, and the authors concluded that Indian smokers "exhibit similar patterns of dissonance reduction" to smokers in high-income countries (Sidhu et al., 2022).

**Marketing and everyday life.** "Post-purchase dissonance", the doubt after an expensive purchase, is a standard idea in consumer research, and companies send reassuring messages after a sale to reduce it. Carol Tavris and Elliot Aronson's popular book *Mistakes Were Made (But Not by Me)* (2007) used dissonance to explain why people, from politicians to doctors, justify their errors instead of admitting them.

## What later research did to the claims

The study's influence is not in doubt. Its central claim is now much less certain than textbooks suggest.

**The reward version was quickly complicated.** Within ten years, studies showed that the "less is more" effect appears only under certain conditions: when people feel free to refuse, when the act is public and face to face, and when it has a consequence they feel responsible for (Linder, Cooper & Jones, 1967; Carlsmith, Collins & Helmreich, 1966; Cooper & Worchel, 1970). Under other conditions, larger rewards produced more change. Researchers then stopped varying the reward and started varying the sense of choice. This became the standard <Term>induced compliance</Term> method: people write an essay against their own view, with either high or low choice.

**The largest test found no choice effect.** In 2024, a team of 90 researchers led by David Vaidis and Willem Sleegers published a <Term>registered replication</Term>: a repeat of a classic study whose methods and analyses were peer-reviewed and fixed before any data were collected, so the results could not be adjusted afterwards. Thirty-nine laboratories in 19 countries, using 14 languages, recruited 4,898 university students. Students wrote an essay in favour of an unpopular university policy, usually higher fees, under high or low choice. A third group wrote a neutral essay. No Indian laboratory took part.

The results were clear:

- The choice manipulation worked: high-choice students felt freer to refuse.
- But high-choice students did **not** change their attitudes more than low-choice students. The difference was almost exactly zero.
- Writing an essay against one's view, with or without choice, did shift attitudes a little towards the essay's position, compared with writing a neutral essay.
- Students who wrote against their view felt more uncomfortable and conflicted, but these feelings did not explain the attitude shift.

The authors concluded that the results "call into question whether the induced-compliance paradigm provides robust evidence for cognitive dissonance" (Vaidis et al., 2024).

<Note title="The 2024 numbers">
Here d is a standardised measure of the difference between two groups; 0.2 is conventionally called small and 0.8 large. High versus low choice: d = -0.03 on attitude after the essay, and d = 0.02 on attitude change, neither significant. Counter-attitudinal essay versus neutral essay: d = 0.26 to 0.31, a small effect. The manipulation check, how free students felt to refuse, differed strongly (d = 0.77). About 3,800 students from 38 labs were analysed. By comparison, the original choice study being repeated, Croyle and Cooper (1983, Experiment 1), had 10 people per group and reported d = 2.40, which the 2024 authors call "disproportionately large".
</Note>

**Defenders and critics responded.** Eddie and Cindy Harmon-Jones (2024) argued that the "low-choice" students still felt fairly free, so the test may not have created a real contrast. The replication team agreed that this gives some reason for caution. But they also audited 104 earlier induced-compliance essay studies and found that the typical study had only about 13 to 17 people per condition, that none had been preregistered, and that almost none had enough participants to detect a realistic effect reliably (Sleegers et al., 2024). Small, unregistered studies are more likely to produce chance findings that look real. This is a central concern of psychology's <Term>replication crisis</Term>, the discovery in the 2010s that many published findings do not hold up when repeated.

**What about the $1 and $20 version itself?** The 2024 study tested the choice version, not the reward version. We found no modern, preregistered repeat of the $1 and $20 procedure. So the accurate statement is: the method that grew out of Festinger and Carlsmith, in its most common modern form, did not show its key effect in the largest and most careful test ever run. Do not write that "the $1/$20 study failed to replicate", because no one has properly tried.

So the study's importance today is mainly historical and theoretical. It challenged reward theory, started one of the largest research programmes in social psychology, and gave the world a useful idea about self-justification. Whether the specific "less is more" effect is real, and how large it is, remains an open question.
