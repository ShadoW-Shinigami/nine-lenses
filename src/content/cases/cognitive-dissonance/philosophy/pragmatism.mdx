## The tradition in brief

Pragmatism is the one philosophical tradition on these pages that grew up alongside American psychology. Charles Sanders Peirce (1839 to 1914) first defined it; William James (1842 to 1910), who also wrote the founding textbook of American psychology, developed and spread it; John Dewey (1859 to 1952) and George Herbert Mead (1863 to 1931) formed a second generation at Chicago. They disagreed with each other about truth, and Peirce renamed his own view "pragmaticism" to keep it apart from James's.

Three ideas run through this page.

**A belief is a habit of action.** For Peirce, "the whole function of thought is to produce habits of action" (EP 1:131), and the test of what a person believes is what they are ready to do.

**Doubt hurts and belief soothes.** Eighty years before Festinger, Peirce described an unpleasant inner state that drives mental work until it is removed, and he said plainly that what ends the work is firmness of belief, not truth.

**The self is social, and we learn what we think by seeing what we do.** For James and Mead a person is a set of selves kept up before different audiences, and the "I" that acts is known only after the act.

## What pragmatists say about belief, doubt and consistency

Each idea below starts with the pragmatists' own words, then explains them in plain English, then says why they matter for this study.

**Doubt is an irritation, and belief is the calm that ends it.** "Doubt is an uneasy and dissatisfied state from which we struggle to free ourselves and pass into the state of belief; while the latter is a calm and satisfactory state which we do not wish to avoid, or to change to a belief in anything else. On the contrary, we cling tenaciously, not merely to believing, but to believing just what we do believe" (Peirce, EP 1:114). "The irritation of doubt causes a struggle to attain a state of belief", and "as soon as a firm belief is reached we are entirely satisfied, whether the belief be true or false" (EP 1:114 to 115). Doubt "stimulates us to action until it is destroyed". Peirce even named the trigger: "The feeling which gives rise to any method of fixing belief is a dissatisfaction at two repugnant propositions" (EP 1:120). And doubts "most frequently" arise "from some indecision, however momentary, in our action" (EP 1:128).

In plain words: Peirce is describing what it feels like to be unsure. Doubt is uncomfortable, and the discomfort pushes you to do something about it, which he calls inquiry. Belief is the calm that returns when the question is settled, and once calm, we hold on to the belief we have, "tenaciously". Two of his remarks go beyond the obvious. First, the calm comes from firmness, not from truth: a false belief, firmly held, feels exactly as good as a true one. Second, doubt usually starts with a hesitation in action, a moment when you do not know what to do, so the practical world is where belief is made and unmade. His phrase about "two repugnant propositions" names the trigger: two claims that will not sit together.

Why it matters here: Peirce describes, eighty years early, an unpleasant inner state produced by a clash and relieved by settling on a belief. He also warns that the relief proves nothing about the belief.

**A belief is a habit.** "The essence of belief is the establishment of a habit, and different beliefs are distinguished by the different modes of action to which they give rise" (EP 1:129 to 130). "There is no distinction of meaning so fine as to consist in anything but a possible difference of practice" (EP 1:131).

In plain words: Peirce's test for whether someone really believes something is not what they say but what they are prepared to do. To believe the ice is thin is to be ready to walk around the lake. Two beliefs that would lead to exactly the same actions are, for him, the same belief in different words. This is the heart of the <Term>pragmatic maxim</Term>: the meaning of an idea is the difference it makes in practice.

Why it matters here: the test of whether the $1 students "really" believed the tasks were enjoyable is not the number they gave the interviewer but whether they would want to do a similar experiment again, and the study's question on that point gave a weak result.

**The method of tenacity, and why it fails.** Peirce asked why we should not settle every question by "taking any answer to a question which we may fancy, and constantly reiterating it to ourselves, dwelling on all which may conduce to that belief, and learning to turn with contempt and hatred from anything which might disturb it" (EP 1:115). People do this: "Oh, I could not believe so-and-so, because I should be wretched if I did." The ostrich "hides the danger, and then calmly says there is no danger" (EP 1:116). He called this the <Term>method of tenacity</Term>, and he said it cannot last, because "The social impulse is against it. The man who adopts it will find that other men think differently from him" (EP 1:116). The alternative is inquiry, and its first duty is "integrity of belief": "to avoid looking into the support of any belief from a fear that it may turn out rotten is quite as immoral as it is disadvantageous" (EP 1:123).

In plain words: Peirce asks a provocative question. If what we want is the calm of belief, why not just pick an answer we like and hold on to it, repeating it to ourselves and refusing to look at anything that might unsettle it? He admits that this works for a while and gives great peace of mind; the ostrich with its head in the sand is, for the moment, calm. He names it the method of tenacity, holding on by sheer grip. His reason for rejecting it is social rather than logical. We cannot help meeting people who think differently, and sooner or later it occurs to us that their view might be as good as ours. Other people are what keep self-serving beliefs from surviving. The honest alternative, inquiry, requires "integrity of belief": being willing to look at the evidence for a belief even when you are afraid of what you will find.

Why it matters here: the $1 students' settling on "it was interesting" is tenacity in Peirce's sense, and the design removed the one thing Peirce relies on to break tenacity, a dissenting voice that is not scripted to agree.

**We have no window on our own minds.** "We have no power of Introspection, but all knowledge of the internal world is derived by hypothetical reasoning from our knowledge of external facts" (EP 1:30).

In plain words: Peirce denied that we can simply look inside ourselves and read off what we think or feel. Knowledge of our own minds, he argued, is reached the same way as knowledge of other people's: by inference from outward facts, including our own behaviour. You learn that you are anxious by noticing that you cannot sit still.

Why it matters here: this is, in outline, the claim that Daryl Bem later made against Festinger, that the students worked out their attitudes from what they had done. Self-perception theory has a pragmatist ancestor.

**New beliefs are grafted on with a minimum of disturbance.** "The individual has a stock of old opinions already, but he meets a new experience that puts them to a strain ... The result is an inward trouble to which his mind till then had been a stranger, and from which he seeks to escape by modifying his previous mass of opinions. He saves as much of it as he can, for in this matter of belief we are all extreme conservatives." The new idea that wins is the one "he can graft upon the ancient stock with a minimum of disturbance of the latter" (*Pragmatism*, lecture 2). And "by far the most usual way of handling phenomena so novel that they would make for a serious rearrangement of our preconception is to ignore them altogether, or to abuse those who bear witness for them."

In plain words: James describes what happens when a new fact bumps into an old set of beliefs. The clash produces "inward trouble", and the person looks for the smallest change that will make the trouble go away, keeping as much of the old stock as possible, because "in this matter of belief we are all extreme conservatives". Grafting is the gardener's technique of joining a new shoot to an old tree so that it grows as part of it; a new belief is accepted when it can be grafted on with the least damage. And the most common response of all, James says, is to ignore the new fact or discredit the person who reported it.

Why it matters here: this is a 1907 description of what the $1 students did, and it predicts the difference between the groups without any talk of a drive. The $20 students had a ready graft, "I was well paid"; the $1 students had to cut into the old stock.

**Truth is what works, with safeguards.** James's famous claim is that "Truth happens to an idea. It becomes true, is made true by events" (lecture 6). But the truth is "the expedient in the way of our thinking ... expedient in the long run and on the whole of course; for what meets expediently all the experience in sight won't necessarily meet all farther experiences equally satisfactorily. Experience, as we know, has ways of boiling over, and making us correct our present formulas." And in lecture 2: "the greatest enemy of any one of our truths may be the rest of our truths". He also limited the right to believe ahead of evidence to cases "where faith in a fact can help create the fact" ("The Will to Believe"), and the *Principles* add the other side: "Nature cannot change the past to suit our thinking" (volume 2, chapter 21).

In plain words: James's slogan is often misread as "truth is whatever works for you". His own text blocks that reading three times. An idea becomes true by being verified, by leading us successfully through experience; the test is "the long run and on the whole", not the comfort of the moment; and any belief must fit with "the rest of our truths", which are its greatest enemy if it does not. He also set strict limits on believing ahead of the evidence. It is allowed only where believing helps make the thing true, as when confidence that a friendship will work helps it work. It is not allowed about the past, because "Nature cannot change the past to suit our thinking".

Why it matters here: whether an hour of turning pegs was enjoyable is a fact about the past. On James's own rules, the $1 students' new belief was adopted for comfort, leads nowhere, and clashes with their memory, so it has not earned the name true.

**Many social selves, and an "I" known only afterwards.** "Properly speaking, a man has as many social selves as there are individuals who recognize him and carry an image of him in their mind", James wrote, and "he generally shows a different side of himself to each of these different groups" (*Principles*, volume 1, chapter 10). The 1934 Mead text: "We are one thing to one man and another thing to another" (p. 142); "The 'I' is the response of the organism to the attitudes of the others; the 'me' is the organized set of attitudes of others which one himself assumes" (p. 175); "It is as we act that we are aware of ourselves" (p. 174); "We are finding out what we are going to say, what we are going to do, by saying and doing" (p. 140). Dewey described what happens after an act that cannot be undone: "Self-deception originates in looking at an outcome in one direction only"; "Satisfied revenge may feel like justice vindicated" (*Human Nature and Conduct*, pp. 252 to 253).

In plain words: James says that a person has as many social selves as there are groups whose opinion he cares about, and shows a different side to each: the polite son at home, the show-off among friends. Mead builds a whole theory from this. The "me" is the self as seen through other people's eyes, the organised set of attitudes we have taken over from them. The "I" is the part of us that acts, and it is known only after it has acted; we often find out what we think by hearing ourselves say it. Dewey adds what happens after an act that cannot be undone. The mind looks at the result from one side only, and renames it, so that revenge feels like justice.

Why it matters here: the lie to the woman and the rating to the interviewer were two social selves speaking to two audiences, not a behaviour and a private truth. And a student asked afterwards what he thinks is, in Mead's terms, finding out by saying.

## What pragmatists say about knowledge and truth

The ideas above describe how beliefs get settled. The pragmatists also had a theory of knowledge, or <Term>epistemology</Term>: an account of what makes a belief true, what counts as evidence, and how a person ought to form beliefs. That theory decides how they judge the $1 student's new opinion. It also shows that pragmatism is not the doctrine that "whatever works for you is true", which is the charge Rand makes on the previous page.

**Truth is what inquiry would finally agree on, and even a past feeling is a fact.** "The opinion which is fated to be ultimately agreed to by all who investigate, is what we mean by the truth, and the object represented in this opinion is the real" (Peirce, "How to Make Our Ideas Clear", EP 1:139). "We may define the real as that whose characters are independent of what anybody may think them to be" (EP 1:137). Peirce applied this even to states of mind: "A dream has a real existence as a mental phenomenon, if somebody has really dreamt it; that he dreamt so and so, does not depend on what anybody thinks was dreamt, but is completely independent of all opinion on the subject" (EP 1:137). And a person who picks beliefs to suit himself "can use the word truth only to emphasize the expression of his determination to hold on to his choice" (EP 1:137).

In plain words: Peirce is a <Term>realist</Term>, someone who holds that facts do not depend on what anyone thinks of them. Truth is not what you or your group believe now. It is what careful inquiry, carried on long enough by many people, would end up agreeing on. The dream example matters most here. What you felt at a given time is a fact about you, and it stays a fact whatever you later say about it. A person who chooses beliefs to suit himself can still use the word "true", but for him it only means "I am sticking to this".

Why it matters here: the interviewer asked how enjoyable the tasks had been. That is a question about the student's experience during the hour, and on Peirce's view the answer is fixed: whether he was bored then "does not depend on what anybody thinks" now. So, for the pragmatists, the $1 student's rating did not become true just because it made him feel settled. It is a claim about the past that can be false, and the control group's -0.45 is the nearest public evidence of what the hour was like. James makes the same cut in the *Principles*: "I will to get up early to-morrow morning; I believe that I got up late yesterday morning" (volume 2, chapter 21). The past is something we believe, not something we can will.

**Testimony can outweigh our own experience, and no single mind is the judge.** On how a child learns, Peirce wrote that "testimony is even a stronger mark of fact than the facts themselves", and added: "this remains so through life; testimony will convince a man that he himself is mad" ("Questions Concerning Certain Faculties Claimed for Man", 1868, EP 1:19 to 20). He denied that private certainty settles anything. The rule "Whatever I am clearly convinced of, is true" makes "single individuals absolute judges of truth", which "is most pernicious"; instead, "If disciplined and candid minds carefully examine a theory and refuse to accept it, this ought to create doubts in the mind of the author of the theory himself" ("Some Consequences of Four Incapacities", 1868, EP 1:29). He also described a kind of thinker whom facts do not move but contradictions do: "show him that an opinion he is defending is inconsistent with what he has laid down elsewhere, and he will be very apt to retract it" ("How to Make Our Ideas Clear", EP 1:138).

In plain words: Peirce saw that what other people tell us can outweigh what we have seen for ourselves, at every age. He did not think this was simply foolish, because most of what anyone knows comes from others. But he held that feeling sure proves nothing, and that one person's private certainty is a poor test of truth. The proper check is a community of careful inquirers who are free to disagree. His last passage describes a thinker for whom consistency matters more than evidence. Peirce called this the <Term>a priori method</Term>: believing what seems "agreeable to reason", by which he meant what we find ourselves inclined to believe ("The Fixation of Belief", EP 1:119). He counted it a better method than tenacity, and still a failure.

Why it matters here: Peirce's a priori thinker is a close portrait of the person dissonance theory describes. On the paper's account, the $1 student was not moved by the fact of a dull hour, but he was moved by an inconsistency between what he had said and what he thought. Peirce's point about testimony explains why the researchers expected their scripted remark about other students' enjoyment to help self-persuasion, although the control group shows that the remark did nothing by itself. And Peirce would add that the room held no one who could disagree. The only dissenting voice, the waiting woman who said her friend had found it boring, was scripted to stop disagreeing at once.

**Belief is moved by more than evidence, but James's licence to believe is narrow.** James's essay "The Will to Believe" (1897) answers the mathematician W. K. Clifford, whom he quotes: "It is wrong always, everywhere, and for every one, to believe anything upon insufficient evidence". James replies that belief is in fact moved by "fear and hope, prejudice and passion, imitation and partisanship, the circumpressure of our caste and set" (section 3). His own thesis is limited: "Our passional nature not only lawfully may, but must, decide an option between propositions, whenever it is a genuine option that cannot by its nature be decided on intellectual grounds" (section 4). A genuine option is one that is "of the forced, living, and momentous kind" (section 1).

In plain words: Clifford said it is always wrong to believe anything without enough evidence. James answers in two ways. As a description, belief is driven by hope, fear, imitation and the pressure of the people around us. By "caste and set" James means one's social class and circle, not caste in the Indian sense. As a rule, James allows feeling to decide a question only in a narrow case, which he calls a <Term>genuine option</Term>. The choice must be forced (you cannot avoid choosing), living (both answers are real possibilities for you) and momentous (much depends on it), and it must be impossible to settle by evidence. His examples are religious faith and trust between people.

Why it matters here: James quotes Clifford condemning belief held "for the solace and private pleasure of the believer" (section 2), and that is what the $1 belief provided. James's licence does not cover it. Whether the hour had been enjoyable was not a genuine option: the student could settle it on the evidence he already had, an hour of his own experience. So the $1 belief fails James's first test. The next idea gives the second.

**Feeling sure proves nothing; a belief is judged by where it leads.** James named two duties that can pull apart: "Believe truth! Shun error! – these, we see, are two materially different laws" (section 7). He was a <Term>fallibilist</Term>, someone who holds that any belief might turn out wrong: "To know is one thing, and to know for certain that we know is another" (section 5), and the mind "may have no infallible signal for knowing whether it be truth or no" (section 6). So a belief is judged by its results, not its origin: "Not where it comes from but what it leads to is to decide" (section 6).

In plain words: there are two duties, to find truth and to avoid error, and a person can lean towards either. But we have no inner signal that tells us we are right, so the feeling of certainty is only a feeling. What counts is whether further experience keeps confirming a belief.

Why it matters here: James does not condemn the $1 belief for its origin in discomfort; he asks where it leads. A belief that some dull pegs were enjoyable leads to nothing that could confirm it. Nobody will test it, and it contradicts an hour of memory, so it fails James's second test as well.

**Belief is a feeling, rationality feels like ease, and education means being able to hold back.** In the *Principles* James wrote that "belief, or the sense of reality, is a sort of feeling more allied to the emotions than to anything else", and that what marks belief is "the cessation of theoretic agitation, through the advent of an idea which is inwardly stable, and fills the mind solidly to the exclusion of contradictory ideas" (volume 2, chapter 21). In "The Sentiment of Rationality" (1879) he said the mark of rationality is "A strong feeling of ease, peace, rest", and warned: "As soon, in short, as we are enabled from any cause whatever to think with perfect fluency, the thing we think of seems to us pro tanto rational" (*The Will to Believe and Other Essays*, 1897, pp. 63 to 64). His answer was self-command: "The greatest proof that a man is sui compos is his ability to suspend belief in presence of an emotionally exciting idea. To give this power is the highest result of education" (*Principles*, volume 2, chapter 21).

In plain words: James the psychologist says that belief is felt. It is the calm that arrives when one idea fills the mind and pushes out the ideas that contradict it. The sense that something makes sense is also felt, as ease and fluency. The Latin phrase *pro tanto* means "to that extent": anything we can think about smoothly seems, to that extent, reasonable. The warning is in the words "from any cause whatever". A convenient story can produce the feeling as well as a true one. *Sui compos* means "in command of oneself". James thought the mark of an educated mind was the power to hold back belief when an idea is emotionally attractive.

Why it matters here: "the cessation of theoretic agitation" through an idea that excludes "contradictory ideas" resembles what Festinger later called dissonance reduction, though James was describing belief in general, not a clash between an act and an opinion. James's essay explains why the $1 student's new view felt fine: once "the tasks were interesting" removed the clash, he could think about the hour fluently again, and fluency feels rational. His remedy names what the study did not look for. No student was asked to hold back belief, and the students who might have done so were the ones excluded, as the readings below show. James also described a rule of habit that resembles the 1959 result: "ACT as if the thing in question were real, and keep acting as if it were real, and it will infallibly end by growing into such a connection with our life that it will become real" (volume 2, chapter 21). But he described a slow process of repeated action, and he applied it to moral and religious habits. His own distinction between what we will and what we believe suggests that it cannot reach the past.

**Truth runs on credit, backed by first-hand checks.** "Truth lives, in fact, for the most part on a credit system. Our thoughts and beliefs 'pass,' so long as nothing challenges them, just as bank-notes pass so long as nobody refuses them. But this all points to direct face-to-face verifications somewhere, without which the fabric of truth collapses like a financial system with no cash-basis whatever" (James, *Pragmatism*, lecture 6).

In plain words: most of what we believe we take on trust from others, as we accept paper money without checking it. That works only because somebody, somewhere, has checked the thing directly. A system of beliefs with no direct checks behind it collapses like a bank with no cash.

Why it matters here: the experimenter's remark that "Most of our subjects tell us afterward that they found it quite interesting" (p. 206) was, in James's image, a bank-note that nobody had checked. It was scripted, and no student ever met those other subjects. Meanwhile each student held the cash himself: an hour of direct experience with the spools and pegs. Every group received the note, and the control and $20 groups kept trusting their cash, so the note cannot explain the result alone. But a $1 student who let it count against his own hour made what James would call a bad trade, because the one person in a position to verify the claim was the student himself.

**Good thinking means staying uneasy long enough to find a real link.** Dewey's *How We Think* (1910) describes reflection as starting in "a state of perplexity, hesitation, doubt" and continuing in "an act of search or investigation" (chapter 1). He named the easy exit: "The easiest way is to accept any suggestion that seems plausible and thereby bring to an end the condition of mental uneasiness" (chapter 1). Against it: "Reflective thinking, in short, means judgment suspended during further inquiry; and suspense is likely to be somewhat painful" (chapter 1). He separated reflection from a looser kind of thought held together only by "emotional congruity", ideas "fitting agreeably into a single picture or story", as in a daydream or a tale (chapter 1). When two things clash, "The object of thinking is to introduce congruity between the two. The given conditions cannot themselves be altered; time will not go backward", and the task is "the discovery of intervening terms" that link them (chapter 6). In *Human Nature and Conduct* he added: "The belief that we can know ourselves immediately is as disastrous to moral science as the corresponding idea regarding knowledge of nature was to physical science" (p. 253), and "There is every reason for examination of the objective factors of what is enjoyed before we translate enjoyment into a belief in excellence" (p. 254).

In plain words: Dewey describes the dissonance situation from the side of good thinking. A puzzle makes us uneasy. The easy way out is to grab the first plausible story that makes the unease stop. The hard way is to stay uneasy while we look for evidence, and Dewey admits that this hurts. Thought that is held together only because it "fits agreeably", like a story, is not aimed at finding out what is so. Applying this to belief is the page's step, not Dewey's. When two facts clash, the honest way to make them fit is to find a real link between them, an <Term>intervening term</Term>, and to leave the facts that cannot change as they are. His example is a man late for an appointment: the clock cannot go back, so he must find a faster route. Dewey also denied that we know our own minds directly, and asked people to check what they actually enjoyed before deciding that a thing was good.

Why it matters here: Dewey's vocabulary fits the $1 and $20 difference closely, though his own example is about keeping an appointment, so this is an analogy. The clash was between "I said it was fun" and "it was dull". The lie could not be undone: time will not go backward. The honest repair was an intervening term that links the two. The $20 student had a true one, the payment. The $1 student had none, so he changed one of the givens, his verdict on the past hour, which is exactly what Dewey says thinking must not do. What dissonance theory calls reduction, Dewey calls the failure to keep judgement suspended. His last sentence reads almost as a question for the interviewer: before the student turned "I enjoyed it" into a rating of the tasks, did anyone ask him to look at what he had actually enjoyed?

## Reading the study through this tradition

**The drive that was never measured, and Peirce's irritation.** Festinger treated dissonance as a drive and never measured it; the paper infers it from the ratings. Peirce also posited a felt state that drives mental work and did not measure it either. But he adds two things the study needs. Doubts arise "from some indecision, however momentary, in our action", which fits the finding that the act came first and the belief followed. And "as soon as a firm belief is reached we are entirely satisfied, whether the belief be true or false", which means the student's relief says nothing about whether the tasks were enjoyable. The lens agrees that inconsistency is felt and motivates. It insists that the motive explains the settling of the opinion, not its truth.

**The $1 students and the method of tenacity.** On the way to the interview every student was told that "Most of our subjects tell us afterward that they found it quite interesting" (p. 206). The $1 student, with no good reason for what he had just done, had material to dwell on, and he dwelt on it: his group rated the tasks well above the control group. That is tenacity: "dwelling on all which may conduce to that belief". Peirce says tenacity fails because other people disagree with us. The design removed that correction. The one person who told the student otherwise, the waiting woman whose friend had found it boring, was scripted to be talked round: after his reply she "listened quietly, accepting and agreeing to everything the S told her". The experiment built a small hermitage in which tenacity could hold its ground.

**One dollar or twenty: the cheapest repair.** James's account predicts the direction of the result without any talk of a drive. The $20 student can marry "I said it was fun" to "it was dull" through "I was well paid". Nothing in his stock of opinions has to move, and his rating stays near the control value. The $1 student has no such bridge, so the smallest available change is to the opinion about the tasks. James would also notice the students who refused to lie. They avoided the strain altogether, and his picture of believers as "extreme conservatives" would predict that many people prefer to avoid an awkward situation rather than live with it. The design kept only those who did not.

**Is the new belief true? Not for James.** A loose pragmatist might say the $1 student's belief worked for him and so was true. James forbids this. Truth is "expedient in the long run and on the whole", experience "has ways of boiling over", and "the greatest enemy of any one of our truths may be the rest of our truths". A belief that the pegs were fun has no future in which to be verified, was adopted to escape a clash with "I am honest", and contradicts an hour of memory. Nor is it a case for the will to believe, which applies only where faith can help create the fact. "Nature cannot change the past to suit our thinking." The hour was over. The option that was genuinely forced and live, in James's sense, was the request itself, made by an embarrassed experimenter to a student fulfilling a course requirement. James would put the moral weight there, in the decision to lie, not in the later rating.

**Two social selves, not behaviour and inner state.** The paper treats what the student said to the woman as behaviour and what he told the interviewer as a reading of his private opinion. James and Mead would deny the contrast. The student spoke to a young woman as a helpful fellow student and to "the department" as an honest evaluator. Each was a self before an audience, and neither was a window onto a third, private self. The 1934 text describes the student's situation exactly: "I have done certain things that seem to commit me to a certain course of conduct ... Perhaps if he does so act it will place him in a false position with another group" (p. 176). The paper's own qualifier, that the two cognitions clash "at least in our culture" (p. 203), is Mead's generalized other at work. The discomfort comes from a community attitude towards lying that the student has taken into himself.

**Bem, Peirce and the "I" that is known afterwards.** Daryl Bem later argued that the $1 student simply inferred his attitude from his behaviour, as an observer would. Peirce said something close in 1868: all knowledge of the inner world "is derived by hypothetical reasoning from our knowledge of external facts". The 1934 Mead text says "exactly how we will act never gets into experience until after the action takes place" (pp. 177 to 178). Dewey adds the after-the-fact renaming that looks at "an outcome in one direction only". So the lens treats the argument between dissonance and self-perception as less of a choice than psychologists made it. A student knows his own attitude by inference from his act, as Peirce, Mead and Bem say, and the inference is pushed by an irritation that is real, as Peirce and Festinger say. What the lens rejects is the paper's picture of a fixed inner opinion that an interview can read off as a number.

**The experimenters spent the trust that inquiry runs on.** Peirce held that the problem of fixing belief is social: "how to fix belief, not in the individual merely, but in the community" (EP 1:117). James wrote that a college or any "social organism" exists "because each member proceeds to his own duty with a trust that the other members will simultaneously do theirs" ("The Will to Believe", section 9). An experiment that lies to its participants at five points spends that trust, and Dewey's "Conduct is always shared" (*Human Nature and Conduct*, p. 17) means the experimenters were participants in the lie, not observers of it.

**The five suspicious students.** Five students were set aside because, when asked at the end of the interview, they said they suspected that the payment to lie was the real purpose of the study, three from the $1 group and two from the $20 group (p. 207). In Dewey's terms these five showed signs of reflective thinking: some "perplexity, hesitation, doubt" about the situation, and a guess about what was going on. The paper tells us nothing about how much the other students reflected. But the design needed students who did not see through the setup, so the result describes belief change in students who did not voice a suspicion. A pragmatist would credit the paper for listing the exclusions openly (p. 207), because a laboratory finding is only as good as its report of what was left out.

**Felt consistency is not rational consistency.** Peirce's a priori thinker gives up an opinion when shown an inconsistency but not when shown a fact. James says that fluency "from any cause whatever" feels rational. Dewey asks for judgement suspended until evidence decides. Each separates the feeling that one's ideas fit from the work of making them fit the facts, a point the [hub page](/cases/cognitive-dissonance/philosophy/) follows across all five traditions.

**What a pragmatist would have measured.** James locates belief in "the cessation of theoretic agitation", which implies two things to observe: agitation before and calm after. Neither was recorded. Peirce's rule says that a belief's meaning lies in its practical effects (EP 1:132), so a pragmatist would test the new belief by later action, not by one number. Would the student do the tasks again, unpaid, or recommend them to a friend? The fourth interview question, about taking part in a similar experiment, came closest, and its result was weak. And James's own "act as if" process is gradual and repeated, so one act followed by a rating a few minutes later is a weak test of it.

## Where it agrees, and where it clashes

The pragmatists and the paper agree that inconsistency is felt and moves people, that doing changes believing, and that a theory earns its keep by predicting something surprising and testing it. Peirce's "dissatisfaction at two repugnant propositions" is the nearest thing in philosophy to Festinger's starting point. James's "minimum of disturbance" predicts the $1 and $20 difference. The lens would also value the tape-recorded check against the rehearsal explanation, which is the kind of self-correction Peirce's method of science requires.

They clash on three points. The settling of the student's opinion does not make it true, and James would refuse the word. The paper's contrast between the lie as behaviour and the rating as inner state dissolves once both are seen as social selves before audiences. And the design removed the social correction that, for Peirce, is what keeps tenacity from winning: the one dissenting voice in the room was scripted to agree.

## Questions it would put to the researchers

- What did the student doubt, and when? Was it "a real and living doubt" (EP 1:115), or a question put to him by an interviewer?
- What rule of action follows from the new belief? Would he do the tasks again, unpaid? Your fourth question comes closest, and the result there was weak.
- Which audience was each statement for, and why is the interviewer's number a window while the conversation is a behaviour?
- What would count as the belief "working" over "the long run and on the whole"?
- Where is the drive, what does it feel like, and why not ask? "Only a hitch in its workings occasions emotion and provokes thought" (Dewey, *Human Nature and Conduct*, p. 178). What was the hitch?
- Were the eleven students who refused, told the truth or saw through the story doing something more usual than the twenty who complied?
- What did five points of deception do to the community you depend on for honest answers?
- What did the student believe the tasks were, as distinct from how he now feels about them, and what later action would show the difference?
- Who checked the claim that "most of our subjects" found the tasks interesting? What happens to a belief that rests on a note nobody has verified?
- Five students suspended judgement and inquired. Does your result describe minds in general, or minds that did not stop to reflect?

## Cautions

- **"Truth is whatever works for you" is a misreading of James.** His own safeguards are quoted above, and he rejected the charge that his view was "a doctrine of caprice" (*Pragmatism*, lecture 7). Do not write that James would call the $1 student's belief true.
- **The pragmatists disagree about truth.** Peirce held that truth is the opinion inquiry would finally settle on, resting on "real things, whose characters are entirely independent of our opinions about them" (EP 1:120). Say "the pragmatists" only for what they share: meaning and belief are tied to action and consequences.
- **Peirce's doubt is not cognitive dissonance.** His doubt is the unsettled state before a question is answered; dissonance is a clash between things already believed. For Peirce the irritation leads to inquiry. What Festinger's students did is closer to the method of tenacity, which Peirce describes as a failure. Present it as a parallel and a contrast, not an anticipation.
- **Mead's book is a posthumous compilation** from students' notes, and James's social self (1890) and Mead's (1920s) are not the same theory. Pair them, but do not merge them.
- **James quotes Clifford in his own wording.** James has "for every one"; Clifford's essay reads "for anyone". Quote James's version only when quoting James.
- **James's "act as if" is about moral and religious habit.** The passage ends with the claim that people to whom "'God' and 'Duty' are now mere names can make them much more than that". Applying it to a paid lie is a reading.
- **Dewey's "time will not go backward" is about keeping an appointment.** Its use for the $1 student is an analogy.
- **"Do not block the way of inquiry"**, Peirce's famous rule, is from a later lecture (1898) that is not in the volume used here. Cite it through the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy if you use it.
- **Do not claim influence.** Nothing shows that Festinger or Bem read the pragmatists. The historical links on the other pages are Kurt Lewin and the behaviourists.
