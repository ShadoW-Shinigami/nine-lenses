## The tradition in brief

Immanuel Kant (1724 to 1804) wrote the most influential modern account of morality as a matter of reason and duty. Four of his ideas matter here.

**Duty, not reward.** An act has moral worth only when it is done from duty, for the sake of the moral law itself, and not from inclination, fear or advantage. The rule a person actually acts on is their <Term>maxim</Term>. Kant's test for a maxim is whether you could will that everyone act on it: "I ought never to act except in such a way that I could also will that my maxim should become a universal law" (*Groundwork*, 4:402).

**Truthfulness is a duty to oneself.** Lying is, for Kant, not mainly a wrong against the listener. It is a wrong the liar does to their own standing as a person.

**Persons are ends.** "So act that you use humanity, whether in your own person or in the person of any other, always at the same time as an end, never merely as a means" (*Groundwork*, 4:429). Kant's own test for "merely as a means" is whether the other person could agree to how they are being treated.

**Two standpoints, and the limits of self-knowledge.** A human being can be studied as part of nature, where every event has a cause, and respected as a free agent answerable for what they do. Introspection does not escape the first standpoint: "the human being cannot claim to cognize what he is in himself through the cognizance he has by inner sensation" (*Groundwork*, 4:451).

## What Kant says about lying, self-deception and reward

**Lying is the greatest violation of a duty to oneself.** "The greatest violation of a human being's duty to himself regarded merely as a moral being (the humanity in his own person) is the contrary of truthfulness, lying" (*Metaphysics of Morals*, 6:429). The wrong does not depend on harm: lying "need not be harmful to others in order to be repudiated" (6:430). It does not even depend on anyone being deceived: "A human being who does not himself believe what he tells another (even if the other is a merely ideal person) has even less worth than if he were a mere thing" (6:429). A person may not use himself "as a mere means (a speaking machine)" (6:430).

**The inner lie.** Kant saw the puzzle that dissonance theory later took up. "It is easy to show that the human being is actually guilty of many inner lies, but it seems more difficult to explain how they are possible; for a lie requires a second person whom one intends to deceive, whereas to deceive oneself on purpose seems to contain a contradiction" (6:430). His answer is that self-love supplies the motive and the mind lets itself be persuaded. Someone "from self-love, takes a wish for the deed because he has a really good end in mind", as "a lover's wish to find only good qualities in his beloved blinds him to her obvious faults" (6:430 to 431). "By an external lie a human being makes himself an object of contempt in the eyes of others; by an internal lie he does what is still worse: he makes himself contemptible in his own eyes" (6:429). From this "rotten spot" the "ill of untruthfulness spreads into his relations with other human beings" (6:430 to 431). In the *Groundwork* he had already warned that "we like to flatter ourselves by falsely attributing to ourselves a nobler motive, whereas in fact we can never, even by the most strenuous self-examination, get entirely behind our covert incentives" (4:407). Everywhere "we come upon the dear self, which is always turning up".

**Acting for a price.** Kant's shopkeeper does not overcharge an inexperienced customer, but only because "his advantage required it", so his honesty has no moral worth (*Groundwork*, 4:397). More sharply: unless duty is "exalted above everything else in actions", a person "is for sale and can be bought for a price that the seductive inclinations offer him" (*Metaphysics of Morals*, 6:483).

**Conscience as an inner court.** "Every human being has a conscience and finds himself observed, threatened, and, in general, kept in awe (respect coupled with fear) by an internal judge". "It follows him like his shadow when he plans to escape. He can indeed stun himself or put himself to sleep by pleasures and distractions, but he cannot help coming to himself or waking up from time to time" (6:438). The court has a prosecutor and a defence. A wrongdoer may paint his act "as something in which he was carried away by the stream of natural necessity", yet "the advocate who speaks in his favor can by no means reduce to silence the prosecutor within him" (*Critique of Practical Reason*, 5:98).

**Acting "as if" can become real, within limits.** In the *Anthropology* Kant made a claim that sounds like this study's finding. "The more civilized human beings are, the more they are actors. They adopt the illusion of affection, of respect for others, of modesty, and of unselfishness without deceiving anyone at all, because it is understood by everyone that nothing is meant sincerely by this." And: "when human beings play these roles, eventually the virtues, whose illusion they have merely affected for a considerable length of time, will gradually really be aroused and merge into the disposition" (7:151). He approved of this, with a condition. The pretence must be transparent and aimed at the good. "It is only the illusion of good in ourselves that must be wiped out without exemption" (7:153).

**Character, and its one proof.** To have character is to bind oneself "to definite practical principles" by one's own reason (*Anthropology*, 7:292). "The imitator (in moral matters) is without character" (7:293). And "the only proof within a human being's consciousness that he has character is that he has made truthfulness his supreme maxim, in the heart of his confessions to himself as well as in his behavior toward everyone else" (7:295). Kant illustrated the point with a man ordered, on pain of death, to give false testimony against an honest person. Whether he would resist, "He would perhaps not venture to assert", but that he could resist "he must admit without hesitation", because "he can do something because he is aware that he ought to do it" (*Critique of Practical Reason*, 5:30).

## Reading the study through this tradition

**The lie to the waiting woman.** The students told a young woman, hired to play a participant, that the tasks were interesting, using words from a sheet headed "For Group B". The design's safeguard, that nobody was really deceived, is exactly what Kant's ethics of lying sets aside. The wrong is in the speaker's use of his own speech against his own belief, and it stands "even if the other is a merely ideal person". Reading lines from a sheet makes the student, in Kant's phrase, a speaking machine. Festinger and Kant agree that the lie is the act that matters. For Festinger it is a lever. For Kant it is a self-inflicted wound.

**One dollar or twenty: no grading of the wrong, but a different aftermath.** Both paid groups sold a declaration, and Kant does not rank the wrong by the price. What differs is what happens afterwards. The $20 student has a motive he can admit, like the prudent shopkeeper: no moral worth, but no inner lie either. The $1 student has a motive too small to admit, and so "takes a wish for the deed", re-describing a boring hour as enjoyable to keep his picture of himself. Kant's remark that we never get behind our covert incentives predicts that the student will not experience this as lying to himself. Kant and Festinger both expect the small inducement to produce the most inner work. They name the result differently. Festinger calls it consistency restored. Kant calls it the "rotten spot" from which further untruthfulness spreads.

**The course requirement and the professor's request.** The students were required to serve as participants, and the request came from an embarrassed experimenter speaking for a professor. Kant's essay on enlightenment describes a society of guardians who tell people "Do not argue but drill!" and finds that "It is so comfortable to be a minor!" (8:35, 37). He allows that institutions need people to act "merely passively" in their offices. He never allows that this licenses untruth: even a statement one "is compelled to make by an unjust constraint" must be truthful (8:426). Later research found that the dissonance effect depends on students feeling free to refuse. Kant would say that the morally interesting moment was always the choice, and that "feeling free" and being answerable are not the same thing.

**The students who refused or told the truth: the gallows case made real.** Of the 11 students set aside, three refused the money and the job, two told the woman the truth, and one wanted her number so that he could explain. For Kant these six are the most important people in the experiment. They show that the request could be refused, which is what makes the compliers answerable for complying: "he can do something because he is aware that he ought to do it". The paper reports them frankly and then removes them, because they did not produce the behaviour. Kant would say that the analysis thereby removed the evidence of freedom and kept only the mechanism.

**The experimenters' deception: persons treated merely as means.** A false announcement to a whole class, a false title on a notice board, a false explanation, a fake absent assistant, a hidden recorder. Kant's test for using someone merely as a means is whether they "cannot possibly agree" to the treatment (*Groundwork*, 4:429 to 430). A cover story is built so that they cannot. The experimenters told more lies, more carefully, than any student did, and did so inside a course whose teachers are, on Kant's account, the "experimental (technical) means" of moral education through their own example (*Metaphysics of Morals*, 6:479). Kant was not against experiments. He praised experimental schools and warned that "the results of an experiment are often entirely different from what we expected" (*On Education*, section 20). His experiments were to be run in the open. The careful debriefing and the returned money are, to borrow a phrase Kant used of the virtues in social circulation, "small change" (*Anthropology*, 7:152), not a cure. The comparison is this page's, not Kant's.

**The unmeasured drive, and another name for it.** Dissonance was never measured; it was inferred from the ratings. Kant holds that self-knowledge through inner sense gives only appearances, so neither the student's report nor Daryl Bem's later idea, that the student inferred his attitude from his own behaviour like an outside observer, reaches the real cause. Both describe the student as a clockwork of representations. Kant also offers a rival name for the unpleasant state the theory posits: conscience. The discomfort after acting against one's belief is the inner prosecutor. Excuses are the defence counsel. And Kant predicts that the defence never fully wins: the prosecutor "follows him like his shadow". The relief Festinger measured would be, for Kant, the court being talked round, not a verdict changed. The guide presents this as a competing interpretation, not a refutation.

## Where it agrees, and where it clashes

Kant and the paper agree that the lie is the act that matters, that a small inducement does more inner work than a large one, and that behaviour can change what a person comes to believe. The *Anthropology* says as much about role-play growing into disposition.

They clash on the names. What Festinger calls dissonance, Kant calls conscience. What Festinger calls reduction of dissonance, Kant calls an inner lie. What the paper calls a consonant cognition, the $20 payment, Kant calls a price. And where the paper treats the students who refused as lost data, Kant treats them as the proof that everyone else could have refused.

## Questions it would put to the researchers

- Was the student's lie wrong? Your paper has no word for it.
- You offered money to make a person say what he did not believe. Did you consider that you were buying a declaration, and what that makes of the seller?
- What did the six who refused or confessed show you, and why did you remove them?
- Could any participant have agreed to the procedure had he known it? If not, in what sense was he a participant rather than an instrument?
- You call the unpleasant state "dissonance" and never measure it. Why is it not conscience?
- You taught a class of students, in a psychology course, that the department lies to them for good reasons. What example did you set?
- Your theory says the $1 student restored consistency. By your own account he now believes something false about his afternoon. Is that a restoration or a loss?

## Cautions

- **How absolute is Kant on lying?** In "On a supposed right to lie" he refused any exception, even to a murderer asking where a friend is hiding, because one excused lie undoes the trust that makes speech work. But that essay concerns duties of right, the duties that law can demand of everyone, rather than duties of virtue, which concern a person's own motives, as Kant's own footnote says (8:426), and he treated polite formulas such as "your obedient servant" as deceiving nobody (6:431). Scholars disagree about how strict his position is. The safe statement is that Kant held truthfulness to be an unconditional duty and refused the murderer exception.
- **Kant did not "anticipate cognitive dissonance".** He described self-deception and conscience, which is different. He also did not deny that behaviour has causes; he allowed "psychological causality" and wrote an empirical *Anthropology*. His claim is that causal explanation is one standpoint and cannot settle questions of worth.
- **"The more civilized human beings are, the more they are actors" is partly approval**, not a complaint. Do not quote it as Kant's attack on hypocrisy.
- **The lectures on education were compiled from Kant's notes** by Rink in 1803. Cite them as lectures.
- **Kant is not hostile to feeling.** He said that beneficence from sympathy "deserves praise and encouragement but not esteem" (4:398). Whether that is fair to feeling is contested; do not write that a good person, for Kant, acts coldly.

The dissonance account says that the second reviewer had too little justification for praising a weak paper, felt an unpleasant tension, and reduced it by coming to believe the praise. The first had a good enough reason and felt no tension.

Kant agrees about the direction of events and disagrees about the names. Both reviewers made a declaration they did not believe, which is a lie, and a violation of a duty to oneself; the board seat does not grade the wrong. The first reviewer can admit his motive, like the shopkeeper who is honest for profit, so he committed no second wrong, though his report has no worth. The second reviewer has "taken a wish for the deed": he now believes something false about the paper to protect his picture of himself as an honest judge. That is the inner lie, the "rotten spot" from which further untruthfulness spreads, and what the theory calls consistency restored, Kant calls a loss.

His advice to the second reviewer follows from the first duty to oneself, to "know your heart": admit that he softened a report to please an editor; treat the discomfort as the prosecutor in the court of conscience rather than as a problem to be solved; and let action follow the admission, by writing to the editor to restore his judgement or by declining to review for that editor again. The concepts in play are duty against inclination, the inner lie, and conscience as an inner court.
