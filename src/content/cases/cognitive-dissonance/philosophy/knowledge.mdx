## What this page is for

Each tradition page has a section on <Term>epistemology</Term>, the part of philosophy that asks what knowledge is, what makes a belief justified, and how a person ought to form beliefs. Some questions that dissonance theory raises do not belong to any one tradition. They are live debates in philosophy today, argued by people who are not Aristotelians, Kantians or existentialists. This page sets out eight of them.

Dissonance theory is, among other things, a theory of how beliefs change. Festinger defined dissonance as "the existence of nonfitting relations among cognitions" and proposed it as "a motivating factor in its own right" (*A Theory of Cognitive Dissonance*, 1957, p. 3). A theory of belief change cannot avoid epistemology. Every time it says a belief "changed", a philosopher can ask: was the new belief justified? Did the person choose it? Did he know why it changed? Was the change rational? And can the theory itself be tested?

Each section below gives the idea in plain words, the sources, and what it means for the $1 and $20 study. Quotations from philosophy encyclopedias are paraphrased; quotations from books and papers were checked against the texts.

## 1. Coherence is not the same as truth

Philosophers have two old pictures of how beliefs are justified. On <Term>foundationalism</Term>, some beliefs are basic, such as what you can see in front of you, and the rest are justified by resting on them. On <Term>coherentism</Term>, no belief is basic; a belief is justified by how well it fits with all the others. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy (SEP) entry "Coherentist Theories of Epistemic Justification" records the main objection: a set of beliefs can fit together perfectly and still be cut off from the world, and two incompatible sets can be equally coherent. So coherence alone does not guarantee truth.

The American philosopher W. V. O. Quine gave the most famous picture of beliefs as a connected system. Our beliefs form "a man-made fabric which impinges on experience only along the edges". When experience clashes with the system, logic does not tell us which belief to give up: "Any statement can be held true come what may, if we make drastic enough adjustments elsewhere in the system", and "no statement is immune to revision". In practice we follow "our natural tendency to disturb the total system as little as possible" ("Two Dogmas of Empiricism", section 6).

In plain words: when a new fact clashes with what you believe, you have a choice about what to change, and people usually change whatever disturbs the rest of their beliefs least. Quine thought this was mostly sensible, but he also valued beliefs that can be tested and refuted.

Why it matters here: dissonance theory describes a mind that seeks coherence in exactly Quine's way, and it adds a rule about which belief gives way. In Harmon-Jones and Mills's summary of Festinger, "Resistance to change is based on the responsiveness of the cognition to reality and on the extent to which the cognition is consonant with many other cognitions" (2019, p. 4). In the $1 condition the student had three candidates. "I said it was fun" was fixed: he had said it, to another person. "I did it for one dollar" was fixed too; the authors note that "it is undoubtedly difficult to convince oneself that one dollar is more than it really is" (p. 208). The least anchored was his private verdict on the hour, so that moved. And the experimenter had supplied a ready, socially backed reason to move it: the remark that "Most of our subjects tell us afterward that they found it quite interesting", added, the paper says, "to make it easier for anyone who wanted to persuade himself" (p. 206). The result was a more coherent set of beliefs ("I said it was interesting; it was fairly interesting; I am honest") that was less accurate about the hour. That is the coherentist's problem on a small scale. The SEP entry also gives coherence some credit: noticing that one's beliefs do not fit is often a good reason to reconsider something. The students were right to feel that something did not fit. The question is which belief they revised.

## 2. Can we choose what we believe?

<Term>Doxastic voluntarism</Term> (from Greek *doxa*, belief) is the view that we can believe things at will, as we can raise a hand. Most philosophers reject it. The IEP entry "Doxastic Voluntarism" summarises Bernard Williams's famous argument of 1970: belief aims at truth, so if I knew I had acquired a belief just by deciding to, whether or not it was true, I could not regard it as a belief about how things are. Many philosophers think Williams's argument does not prove that choosing a belief is impossible, but they agree that we have no direct control of this kind. What we do have is <Term>indirect control</Term>: we choose actions, such as where we look and whom we listen to, that later change what we believe.

The two classic positions come from the seventeenth century. Descartes held that error comes from the will running ahead of the understanding: "Whence then come my errors? They come from the sole fact that since the will is much wider in its range and compass than the understanding, I do not restrain it within the same bounds" (Meditation IV, Haldane and Ross, pp. 175 to 176). His rule: "the knowledge of the understanding should always precede the determination of the will" (p. 176). Pascal described indirect control. He told an unbeliever who "cannot believe" to follow those who began "by acting as if they believed, taking the holy water, having masses said, etc. Even this will naturally make you believe, and deaden your acuteness" (*Pensées* 233, Trotter's translation).

In plain words: you cannot simply decide to believe that it is raining. But you can do things that will, over time, change what you believe. Descartes thought we go wrong when we agree to something before we clearly understand it. Pascal thought that acting as if you believe can bring belief, at some cost to your sharpness.

Why it matters here: nobody in the study tried to change a belief. The students chose to say something, for $1 or $20, and their belief moved afterwards, apparently without their noticing. That is indirect influence of Pascal's kind, but unplanned. Pascal's unbeliever wants the belief and uses action as a tool; the $1 student wanted nothing of the kind. The $20 condition shows how much depends on explanation: the same act, with a good reason attached, left the belief where it was. And there is an ethical consequence that the [ethics page](/cases/cognitive-dissonance/ethics/) and the [power page](/cases/cognitive-dissonance/power/) take further. If belief can be moved indirectly through acts, then whoever arranges the acts, an experimenter, an employer, a recruiter, has power over belief without ever making an argument.

## 3. The ethics of belief: is it wrong to believe without evidence?

In 1877 the English mathematician W. K. Clifford argued that believing is a moral matter. His example is a shipowner about to send an old emigrant ship to sea. Doubts about her safety "preyed upon his mind, and made him unhappy". He "succeeded in overcoming these melancholy reflections" and "acquired a sincere and comfortable conviction that his vessel was thoroughly safe and seaworthy". The ship sank. Clifford's verdict: "he had no right to believe on such evidence as was before him. He had acquired his belief not by honestly earning it in patient investigation, but by stifling his doubts." Even if the ship had arrived safely, he would be no less guilty, because the question is "not what it was, but how he got it". Clifford's summary: "it is wrong always, everywhere, and for anyone, to believe anything upon insufficient evidence" ("The Ethics of Belief", section 1). The modern descendant of his view is called <Term>evidentialism</Term>: a belief is justified only if it fits the evidence the person has.

In plain words: Clifford thought that how you come to believe something is as important as whether it turns out true. A belief adopted to stop feeling uncomfortable, rather than earned by looking at the evidence, is wrong even if it happens to be right, because it trains you to be <Term>credulous</Term>, too ready to believe things. William James's reply, that in a narrow set of cases a belief may be held ahead of the evidence, is on the [pragmatism page](/cases/cognitive-dissonance/philosophy/pragmatism/).

Why it matters here: Clifford's shipowner resembles a dissonance case, although Clifford was writing about the duty to inquire, not about a drive. He held two clashing thoughts, "my ship may be unsafe" and "I am sending families to sea". The clash made him "unhappy". He reduced it by adding comforting thoughts and dismissing the doubts, and he ended with a "sincere and comfortable conviction". Clifford says sincerity does not excuse the route. Applied to the $1 student, evidentialism gives a clear verdict. His evidence about the tasks, an hour of dull peg-turning, did not change. What changed was his situation: he had lied for a dollar. So nothing in his evidence supports the new rating. There is one important difference. Clifford's shipowner "knowingly and willingly worked himself into that frame of mind". Whether the $1 student did anything knowingly is the question of the next section, and responsibility for belief change that a person does not notice is the hardest case for any ethics of belief.

## 4. Do we know our own minds?

Philosophers long assumed that each of us has <Term>privileged access</Term> to our own mind: I know what I believe and why, in a way others cannot. In 1977 the psychologists Richard Nisbett and Timothy Wilson challenged this in a famous paper, "Telling more than we can know". Much of their evidence came from dissonance studies, and from what researchers had noticed but never published. Elliot Aronson told them: "We occasionally asked our subjects why they had responded as they did. The results were very disappointing." Philip Zimbardo told them: "I don't remember any subject who ever described anything like the process of dissonance reduction that we knew to have occurred." Nisbett and Wilson concluded that the participants' explanations "are so removed from the processes that investigators presume to have occurred as to give grounds for considerable doubt that there is direct access to these processes" (1977, p. 238).

In plain words: people often cannot report the real causes of their own judgements. When asked why, they give reasons drawn from everyday ideas about what causes what, the same reasons an outsider would guess. Daryl Bem's self-perception theory, on the [behavioural lens page](/cases/cognitive-dissonance/lenses/behavioural/), goes further and says that people often learn their own attitudes by watching their own behaviour. The philosopher Peter Carruthers has generalised the idea, as the SEP entry "Self-Knowledge" explains: we know our own attitudes using the same skill we use to work out what other people think and want, by interpreting what we see them do.

Why it matters here: the study never asked. Each student "was first encouraged to talk about the matter and was then asked to rate" (p. 206), but only the ratings are reported, and nobody was asked whether his opinion had changed or why. The later testimony from Aronson and Zimbardo suggests that the $1 students would not have said "because I was paid only a dollar". If asked, a $1 student might sincerely have said "it was quite interesting, and others say so too", repeating the experimenter's line with no idea that the dollar mattered. There are two pictures of the +1.35. On Festinger's, a real attitude changed and was reported accurately. On Bem's and Carruthers's, there was no hidden attitude; the rating was an inference from behaviour, made in the interview. Both agree that the student was sincere and did not know the cause. The exclusions matter here too. The five students who suspected the real purpose were the ones with most insight into what was being done to them, and they were removed. The study therefore measured belief change in people who did not see the mechanism, which is necessary for the test, and also means it cannot tell us what happens when people do see it.

## 5. Self-deception without a plan

Self-deception looks impossible if we model it on lying to someone else. The SEP entry "Self-Deception" (Ian Deweese-Boyd, 2023) names two puzzles. The <Term>static paradox</Term>: how can one person believe something and its opposite at the same time? The <Term>dynamic paradox</Term>: how can I set out to deceive myself without my plan revealing itself to me? One family of answers keeps the lying model and divides the person into parts or stages. Another, led by the philosopher Alfred Mele (*Self-Deception Unmasked*, 2001), drops it. On Mele's <Term>deflationary</Term> view, self-deception needs no intention and no pair of contradictory beliefs. It is simply a false belief produced by treating the evidence in a way that is biased by what one wants.

In plain words: Mele says that self-deception is not a lie told to oneself. It is ordinary belief-forming gone wrong in a motivated way: noticing the evidence you like, discounting the evidence you dislike, asking for less proof for the conclusion you want. A related idea in the same SEP entry is that people demand less evidence for a belief when being wrong about it would cost them little.

Why it matters here: the $1 student fits Mele's conditions well. His new belief, that the tasks were fairly interesting, was probably false, measured against the control group's -0.45. The motive was to ease the clash between what he had said and what he had experienced. His evidence, the hour itself, supported the old view better than the new. And the biased treatment produced the belief, which is what the contrast between $1 and $20 is meant to show. Neither paradox arises on Mele's view: the student need not have held "dull" and "interesting" at once, and he never intended to deceive himself. The low-cost point fits as well. Being wrong about whether an hour of peg-turning was interesting cost the student nothing, so little was needed to shift him. There are two gaps: the study never measured the motive, and +1.35 is a modest shift, not a reversal. Compare Sartre's bad faith on the [existentialism page](/cases/cognitive-dissonance/philosophy/existentialism/), which is the strongest view on the other side: a self-deception that is the person's own project.

## 6. Is rationalising rational?

"Rationalising" usually means making up good-sounding reasons after the fact, and it is usually counted as a failure of reason. Several recent views say it has a function. The psychologist Fiery Cushman argues: "Rationalization occurs when a person has performed an action and then concocts the beliefs and desires that would have made it rational. Then, people often adjust their own beliefs and desires to match the concocted ones." He calls this "a useful fiction ... fiction, because it imputes reason to non-rational psychological processes; useful, because it can improve subsequent reasoning" ("Rationalization is rational", *Behavioral and Brain Sciences*, 2020, abstract). Hugo Mercier and Dan Sperber argue that human reasoning evolved to win arguments, not to find truth: "Our hypothesis is that the function of reasoning is argumentative", and "Skilled arguers, however, are not after the truth but after arguments supporting their views" (2011, abstract). Ziva Kunda's account of <Term>motivated reasoning</Term> adds a limit: people "are more likely to arrive at conclusions that they want to arrive at, but their ability to do so is constrained by their ability to construct seemingly reasonable justifications for these conclusions" (1990, p. 480).

In plain words: on Cushman's view, much of what we do comes from habit, instinct or social rules. Afterwards we work out reasons that would have made the act sensible, and we adopt them. Usually this helps, because the habit or rule often carried good information, and adopting the reasons lets us plan with it. Mercier and Sperber say that reasoning is built for persuading others, so it naturally collects arguments for one side. Kunda says we can believe what we want only as far as we can make a believable case for it.

These views have critics. The philosopher Jason D'Cruz replied that Cushman "downplays rationalization's signature liability: It exposes a person to the hazards of delusion and self-sabotage" (2020). Tom Stafford argued that the effects are "small rather than gross", and calculated that the 1959 gap between the $1 and $20 groups was "a mean shift of 1.40 on an 11-point scale (i.e., 13% change between groups ...)" (2020). And the philosopher Eric Mandelbaum argued that we "do not acquire and update information in a Bayesian way", meaning by rational weighing of probabilities, and that belief change serves "a psychological immune system", which protects the self rather than tracks the truth (2019, abstract).

Why it matters here: Cushman's account says when rationalising should go wrong, and the $1 condition is such a case. Rationalising is useful when the act carried information about what is worth doing. The $1 student's speech to the woman carried information about the experimenter's request and the awkwardness of refusing, not about the tasks. Reading "I must have found it interesting" off that act imported an error. The $20 student had an obvious outside cause, so there was nothing to read off. (This application is the page's, not Cushman's.) Kunda's limit fits the size of the effect: the $1 students moved to +1.35, not to +5, because they could build a believable case for "somewhat interesting" but not for "extremely interesting". And this section sets up a direct clash with the [Rand page](/cases/cognitive-dissonance/philosophy/rand/). For Rand, rationalisation is a betrayal of reason. For Cushman, it is reason doing a rough but useful job. The study alone cannot decide between them, because it measured no later behaviour that the new belief helped or hurt.

## 7. Virtues and vices of the mind

<Term>Virtue epistemology</Term> asks not "is this belief justified?" but "what kind of thinker forms beliefs well?". It models intellectual virtues, such as open-mindedness, intellectual courage and humility, on Aristotle's virtues of character (SEP, "Virtue Epistemology"). The philosopher Quassim Cassam studies the opposite. <Term>Epistemic</Term> means "to do with knowledge". Epistemic vices are "blameworthy or otherwise reprehensible character traits, attitudes, or ways of thinking that systematically obstruct the gaining, keeping, or sharing of knowledge"; and "many epistemic vices are stealthy, in the sense that they block their own detection by active critical reflection" (*Vices of the Mind*, 2019, publisher's summary). A further position, <Term>epistemic situationism</Term>, uses psychology experiments to argue that most people's thinking habits shift so much with small features of the situation that they cannot count as stable virtues (SEP, "Virtue Epistemology", on Mark Alfano).

In plain words: some ways of thinking reliably lead us towards the truth, and some reliably lead us away. The worst of the bad ones are "stealthy": they hide themselves, so you cannot catch them just by reflecting harder. Situationists add a doubt: perhaps very few people have good thinking habits that hold steady when circumstances change.

Why it matters here: dissonance reduction is a good example of a stealthy vice. It hides its own working, as the Aronson and Zimbardo testimony shows, so a person cannot fix it by reflection alone; Cassam stresses the role of other people and outside checks. Note that it is not quite wishful thinking. The $1 student did not wish the tasks had been interesting before he lied; the wish, to have acted sensibly, arose from the act. The situationist reading is this: the gap between +1.35 and -0.05 came from the size of a payment, which has no bearing on whether the tasks were interesting. A virtue epistemologist would answer that one rating from twenty students per group cannot show that nobody has the virtue, and would point to the students who were set aside, discussed in section 4 and on the tradition pages, whose refusals look like intellectual honesty and courage. And intellectual humility gives the practical lesson of the whole study: hold a belief more loosely when you notice that you have just acted on its behalf. "I may think it was interesting because I said so" is exactly the self-suspicion dissonance research teaches.

## 8. Do scientists reduce dissonance too?

The philosophy of science gives two angles on dissonance, and both matter for a course on paradigms.

**Can the theory be tested?** Karl Popper held that a scientific theory must forbid some outcomes; a theory that can explain any result explains nothing (SEP, "Karl Popper", section 2). The $1 and $20 study made a risky prediction in his sense. Reinforcement thinking of the 1950s predicted more liking with more pay; dissonance theory predicted the reverse; and the result came out the theory's way. The opposite result was possible and would have counted against the theory. But the theory's later history shows the flexibility Popper feared. As results came in that did not fit, conditions were added: choice, commitment, foreseeable harm, personal responsibility, a threat to the self-concept. In 1978 Anthony Greenwald and David Ronis wrote that "Cognitive dissonance theory has shown a history of adapting its theoretical statement to be consistent with the body of empirical data it has spawned", that "The continuing process of adjusting a theoretical statement to maintain its currency with empirical data is scientifically questionable" ("maintain its currency" here means "keep it up to date"), and, pointedly, that "the behavior of the theorists doing the revising is a near-perfect illustration of dissonance reduction of the sort intended in the original statement of the theory" (p. 55). After the large 2024 replication described on the [importance page](/cases/cognitive-dissonance/importance/), the replication team warned that explanations added after the fact "risk making a theory unfalsifiable, particularly in the case of an underspecified theory, such as cognitive-dissonance theory" (Sleegers and colleagues, 2024, p. 6). An <Term>unfalsifiable</Term> theory is one that no possible result could show to be wrong.

Imre Lakatos offered a fairer test than Popper's (SEP, "Imre Lakatos"). Every research programme, he said, protects a "hard core" of central claims with a "protective belt" of extra assumptions that absorb the damage from awkward results. That is acceptable as long as the programme keeps predicting new facts, which Lakatos called a progressive programme. It becomes a problem when the belt only explains results after the event, which he called a degenerating programme. For dissonance theory, the hard core is that inconsistency motivates change. The belt includes choice, responsibility and unmeasured routes such as deciding that the issue does not matter. Students can ask, on Lakatos's test, whether each revision predicted something new. Elliot Aronson's self-concept version led to the "hypocrisy" studies, in which people who publicly urged others to act safely and were then reminded of their own lapses changed their behaviour, which was a new prediction; others are harder to defend.

**Do scientists ignore awkward data?** Thomas Kuhn's *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions* (1962) gave this course its key word. In "normal science", Kuhn argued, scientists work within a <Term>paradigm</Term>, a shared set of theories, methods and model examples, and they tend to set aside or explain away <Term>anomalies</Term>, results that do not fit; only a pile-up of them brings a crisis and a change of paradigm (SEP, "Thomas Kuhn"). Kuhn drew on a 1949 psychology experiment by Jerome Bruner and Leo Postman. They flashed playing cards at people, including trick cards such as a red six of spades. Most people first "saw" a normal card: "27 out of our 28 subjects showed dominance responses to the trick cards", a "'perceptual denial' of the incongruous elements" (pp. 213 to 214). When the trick finally broke through, one said: "I'm not even sure now what a spade looks like! My God!" (p. 218).

In plain words: Popper and Lakatos ask whether a theory can lose. Kuhn and the card experiment ask whether the people holding it will notice when it does. Bruner and Postman's "perceptual denial" is a cousin of dissonance reduction: an awkward fact is first resolved by seeing it as fitting.

Why it matters here: Festinger's own two classic studies sit on both sides of this point. In *When Prophecy Fails* (1956), Festinger, Henry Riecken and Stanley Schachter reported that members of a group whose prophecy of a flood had failed became more committed, not less. Archival research published in 2025 disputes that account, as the [ethics page](/cases/cognitive-dissonance/ethics/) explains, so present it as "as reported in 1956". And Greenwald and Ronis suggested that dissonance theorists treated their own theory's awkward results in a similar way. For a paradigms course this makes a useful chain of ideas: a psychology experiment on incongruity, borrowed by a philosopher to explain how science works, beside a psychology theory of incongruity that later faced the same charge. The question to take to [Section B](/cases/cognitive-dissonance/paradigm/) is whether dissonance theory has had its crisis yet.

## Cautions

- **Encyclopedia summaries are paraphrased.** Where this page reports what the SEP or IEP says about Williams, Mele, Popper, Lakatos or Kuhn, the words are the page's, not the entry's. Check the entry before quoting it.
- **Parallels are not anticipations.** Clifford's shipowner, Pascal's holy water and Descartes's runaway will are not early versions of dissonance theory. None of the philosophers here discusses the 1959 study, and the applications are the page's.
- **Nisbett and Wilson's quotations from Aronson and Zimbardo are personal communications** reported in their paper, not published findings. Attribute them as "quoted in Nisbett and Wilson (1977, p. 238)".
- **Clifford's line is often misquoted.** The 1879 text reads "for anyone, to believe anything upon insufficient evidence". James's quotation of it reads "for every one".
- **"Web of belief" is the title of a later book** by Quine and J. S. Ullian (1970). The 1951 essay speaks of a "fabric" and a "field of force".
- **The 2024 replication tested the choice-based essay version**, not the $1 and $20 procedure. Do not write that the 1959 study "failed to replicate".
- ***When Prophecy Fails* is disputed.** Present its findings as reported in 1956, and note the 2025 archival challenge.
- **Kuhn did not write about dissonance theory.** The link between his account of anomalies and Festinger's theory is the page's.

Popper would ask whether the new condition was added only to save the theory. If the extra condition forbids nothing new and simply makes the awkward result fit, it is what he calls an ad hoc rescue, and it makes the theory harder to test. Lakatos would be more patient. The test is whether the revised theory now predicts something new that the student can check. If it does, and the prediction comes true, the change is progressive. If the new condition only explains the result after the event, the programme is degenerating.

The coherence point explains why the supervisor is suspicious. The student had two years invested in the theory, and Festinger's rule says the belief least anchored to reality gives way first. Her investment and her public commitment are fixed; her reading of one new result is the easiest thing to change. A more coherent set of beliefs ("my theory is right, and the new result supports it") is not thereby more true.
