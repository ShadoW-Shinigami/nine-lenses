## What you care about

Imagine you are a clinical psychologist in Chennai. Some of your clients have survived road accidents, assault or childhood abuse. Your daily work is talking with people about their memories. You care about two things that can pull against each other. You do not want your own questions to plant ideas in a client's mind. And you do not want clients who tell you about real harm to be disbelieved, by you, by their families or by a court.

This study speaks to both concerns. It shows how much a single word can shape what someone reports. It also began a line of research that was later used, in the 1990s "memory wars", to argue that many memories of abuse recovered in therapy were false.

## What worries you about the original study

- **The memories were not personal.** Students watched short films of strangers' crashes, with nothing at stake for them (pp. 586-587). Trauma researchers have questioned whether "laboratory studies of ordinary events are relevant to the understanding of traumatic memories" (van der Kolk and Fisler, 1995, abstract).
- **The false detail was trivial.** Broken glass in a film is a small, plausible detail that fits the scene. Believing you saw it costs nothing. A memory of abuse involves your family, your body and your sense of who you are.
- **The question came from a stranger with no relationship to the witness.** In therapy, questions come from a trusted person, over many sessions. That could make suggestion stronger than in the laboratory, not weaker.
- **The research has been used in ways the study cannot support.** A finding about broken glass has been cited to cast doubt on memories of abuse. The [ethics page](/cases/car-crash/ethics/) describes the False Memory Syndrome Foundation, founded in 1992 to support parents accused by their adult children, which drew on research on false memory.

## Your redesign

1. **Study memories that matter to the person, without creating new ones.** Recruit adults who were recently in a minor road accident with an official record, such as a police or insurance report, so that some details can be checked. Exclude anyone with severe injuries or current post-traumatic stress, and give everyone information about support services.
2. **Use clinicians as interviewers.** Trained clinicians interview each person about the accident. Half use only open questions ("Tell me what happened"). Half also ask questions that suggest a detail, using a verb such as *smashed* for a collision the record describes as minor.
3. **Record and compare.** Every interview is recorded. The person's account is compared with the official record, and accounts are compared again a month later.
4. **Measure how the person feels.** Ratings of distress, and of feeling believed, are taken after each interview, because a technique that is accurate but leaves people feeling disbelieved is not acceptable in therapy.
5. **Never implant a false memory of harm.** No participant is told that something happened to them that did not happen.

## Why

Clinicians have good reason to take suggestion seriously. A working group of the Royal College of Psychiatrists reached a firm conclusion. It said that "when memories are 'recovered' after long periods of amnesia, particularly when extraordinary means were used to secure the recovery of memory, there is a high probability that the memories are false". The same group warned that the issue "should not be allowed to confuse the recognition and treatment of sexually abused children" (Brandon, Boakes, Glaser and Green, 1998). Both halves matter.

Beliefs about memory still differ sharply between groups. In one survey, 82% of hypnotherapists and 69% of psychoanalysts agreed that traumatic memories are often repressed, against far fewer research psychologists. The authors described "a substantial gap between the memory beliefs of clinical-psychology researchers and those of practitioners" (Patihis, Ho, Tingen, Lilienfeld and Loftus, 2014). A clinician should know where the evidence is strong and where it is weak.

The evidence cuts against strong claims on both sides. A systematic review looked at studies that tried to implant false childhood memories. On average, 47% of participants showed some recollection of the suggested event, "but only in 15% are these experiences likely to be rated as full memories" (Brewin and Andrews, 2017). False memories can be created, but less easily than some accounts suggest. On the other side, Jennifer Freyd's <Term>betrayal trauma theory</Term> offers a reason why some real abuse is forgotten (Freyd, 1994). The theory says that a child abused by a caregiver may lose access to the memories in order to keep the attachment the child depends on.

In India, courts already accept that truthful witnesses fill gaps. The Supreme Court has said that a witness "cannot be expected to possess a photographic memory" (*Bharwada Bhoginbhai Hirjibhai v. State of Gujarat*, 1983). It added that a truthful witness may "fill up details from imagination on the spur of the moment". The Court called this "a sort of a psychological defence mechanism". A clinician can help courts and families see that a mistaken detail does not mean the whole account is false.

## What your redesign gives up

- **No perfect record of the truth.** A police or insurance report is itself written from memory, often by someone who arrived after the event. The film in 1974 gave a certainty that real accidents never can.
- **Risk to participants.** Talking about a real accident can bring back distress, even if it was minor. Screening and support reduce the risk but do not remove it.
- **Still not trauma.** A minor road accident is closer to real life than a film, but it is still far from childhood abuse. The study cannot settle the questions that divided the memory wars.
- **Clinicians asked to use leading questions.** Some clinicians may refuse to ask suggestive questions, even for research, because it goes against their training.
