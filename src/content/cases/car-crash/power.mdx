This page follows Section D, part 8, of the outline. Questions of power ask who had the authority to design the study, define its terms and tell its story, and who did not.

## Who is speaking?

**A young professor, an undergraduate and a government grant.** Elizabeth Loftus was an assistant professor in her first year at the University of Washington. John Palmer was an undergraduate working as a research technician. The research was funded by the United States Department of Transportation (p. 585). This is not the picture of a powerful establishment. But the study speaks with the authority of the laboratory: its claims are made in the language of experiments and statistics, published in a leading journal, and later taught to millions of students.

**Researchers who controlled the wording.** The whole study turns on one fact: the researchers chose the words, and the students did not know. The researchers decided which verb each student read, what counted as an answer (a number or a tick), and which answers mattered. The students' own words, their free accounts of each accident, were collected and never reported (pp. 586-587). In this study, power lies in the question, which is exactly the study's point about witnesses.

**A speaker who became a public expert.** Loftus went on to become one of the best-known expert witnesses on memory in the United States. She has testified about 300 times, almost always for the defence (as reported by Reuters, 2021). She has also written books for a wide readership, including *Eyewitness Testimony* (1979) and, with Katherine Ketcham, *Witness for the Defense* (1991). So the voice that tells the story of this research is also a voice that has argued about it in court, in cases where the stakes were very high. Her supporters see this as science serving justice. Her critics, as the [ethics page](/cases/car-crash/ethics/) explains, see the same work used to cast doubt on victims.

**The vocabulary is the researchers'.** The paper calls participants "subjects" and says they were "interrogated" about speed (p. 586), as was usual in 1974. The word "interrogated" is revealing. It is the language of police and suspects, and the study reproduces, in miniature, a questioner who controls the conversation and a witness who must answer.

## Who is being studied?

**People described only as "students".** The paper says nothing else about its 195 participants: no ages, no sexes, no ethnic backgrounds, no driving experience, no college, no way they were recruited (pp. 586-587). Other studies from the same laboratory at the time used University of Washington students, so it is likely, but not stated, that these were young American undergraduates. In the 1970s, most American psychology studies used students of this kind, because they were convenient and available. The social psychologist David Sears (1986) called this the problem of a science built on "college sophomores in the laboratory".

**Witnesses with nothing at stake.** The students watched other people's accidents on a screen. Nobody they knew was involved. Their answers had no consequences for anyone. Real witnesses are often frightened, sometimes injured, sometimes friends or relatives of the people involved, and they know their words may decide a case. The study speaks about "a witness" (p. 586), but the people it studied were in a very different position from the witnesses it describes.

**Treated as sources of numbers and ticks.** The students' speed estimates were averaged, and their "yes" or "no" answers were counted. Nobody asked them how sure they were, why they answered as they did, or whether they thought the question had suggested an answer. Their own experience of remembering is not part of the study.

## Whose voices are absent?

| Absent voice | What it might have added |
|---|---|
| The students' own accounts | The free descriptions written after each film could have shown how students described the crashes before any leading question, and whether anyone mentioned glass unprompted |
| Real witnesses to real accidents | How fear, injury, personal involvement and repeated questioning by police change what people remember |
| People accused on the basis of witness evidence | The study's findings are often used to protect the accused. Yet their experience of being wrongly identified, or wrongly cleared, is absent from the research itself |
| Victims and survivors | When this research was later used in court and in the "memory wars", people who reported abuse were on the other side of the argument, but their voices are not part of the experimental literature |
| Police officers and lawyers | The people who actually ask witnesses questions. Their reasons for leading questions, and the pressures they work under, are not studied |
| Children, older people and people with little schooling | Groups who are often witnesses, and who may respond to suggestion differently |
| People who speak other languages | Whether a verb like *smashed* works the same way in Hindi, Tamil or Spanish, and what happens when questions are translated. The [cultural lens](/cases/car-crash/lenses/cultural/) takes this up |
| People outside the United States | Everyone in the study judged speed in miles per hour on American roads. Driving, policing and courts are very different elsewhere |

## Why this matters for the study's claims

These points are about evidence as well as fairness. They limit what the study can claim.

- **A study that controls the questioner cannot study the questioner.** Real leading questions come from people, often people with power over the witness, such as a police officer. One later study reported that who asks matters: leading questions seemed to distort memory less when people believed the questioner was biased (Dodd and Bradshaw, 1980). A printed questionnaire removes this social side completely.
- **A study of low-stakes students cannot show how large the effect is for real witnesses.** The direction of the effect may hold, but its size could be smaller, because real witnesses care about accuracy, or larger, because real questioning is longer and repeated.
- **A study that does not describe its participants cannot show that the process is universal.** The paper speaks of "a witness" in general, but readers cannot tell whom the results describe.
- **A study whose findings are used in court carries the power of the people who use it.** Laboratory results about broken glass in a film were later used to argue about real memories of real crimes. Who gets to decide which memories are trusted, and whose interests that serves, is a question of power. The [critical lens](/cases/car-crash/lenses/critical/) page asks it directly.

The student is right that the study involved little harm and no obvious coercion, and the [ethics page](/cases/car-crash/ethics/) agrees. But power is not only about harm. In this study the researchers chose the words, decided which answers counted, and kept the purpose hidden. The students' own accounts were collected and never reported. This is a quiet form of power: the power to frame a question and define the answer. It is also exactly what the study shows about witnesses, who answer questions framed by someone else.

Power also matters for what the study can claim. The participants are described only as "students", so we cannot tell whom the results describe. And the findings were later used in courtrooms, where they shaped which memories were believed. A power-and-representation analysis asks who frames the questions, whose answers count, and who benefits from the conclusions, even in a study that harmed nobody.
