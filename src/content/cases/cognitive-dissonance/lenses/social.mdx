## The lens in brief

Social psychology studies how other people shape what we think, feel and do. Kurt Lewin, one of its founders, summed up its approach in a formula: behaviour is a function of the person and the environment, and the environment that matters most is usually other people. Leon Festinger was Lewin's student, so this lens is the study's home discipline. <Term>Compliance</Term> means doing what someone asks, whether or not you agree. <Term>Self-presentation</Term> means managing the impression you give to others.

The paper's explanation, however, is private: two thoughts inside one head that do not fit. The social lens asks how much of the effect came from the social situation the researchers built.

## How this lens reads the study

**A polite request is a strong force.** The experimenter did not order anyone to lie. He hesitated, seemed embarrassed, said the professor in charge had suggested this student, and offered reassurance if the student hesitated: "It will only take a few minutes" (p. 205). Only three students refused (p. 207). A social psychologist sees this as the first finding of the study, even though the paper treats it as background. A few years later, Stanley Milgram's obedience studies at Yale (1963) showed, in a very different way, how hard it is to say no to a person in charge of the situation.

**A public act, to a real person.** The lie was told face to face to a fellow student who would believe it and then do the boring tasks herself. She had just said that a friend advised her to "try to get out of it" (p. 206), so the student was actively talking her into something. He also agreed to be on call in future, and gave his phone number. Social psychologists call this <Term>commitment</Term>: a public, identifiable act that is hard to take back. A private thought can be revised quietly. A statement made to someone who trusted you cannot.

**Other people's opinions were built into the procedure.** Festinger's own <Term>social comparison theory</Term> (1954) says that when people are unsure what to think, they compare their views with other people's. The procedure contained two such comparisons. The woman reported that her friend found the tasks boring. Then, on the way to the interview, the experimenter told every student that "most of our subjects tell us afterward that they found it quite interesting" (p. 206). This remark went to all three conditions, so it cannot explain the differences between them. But it shows that the researchers knew opinions are anchored in what others say.

**The interview was a social encounter too.** In 1971 James Tedeschi, Barry Schlenker and Thomas Bonoma argued that dissonance experiments show <Term>impression management</Term>, not private attitude change. People want to appear consistent, because others distrust a person who says one thing and thinks another. On this reading, the $1 students reported liking the tasks to look honest, not because their private view had changed. The 1959 design answers part of this: the interviewer was blind and seemed to belong to an unrelated survey. But the interview came minutes later, in the same building, and a student who suspected a link had good social reasons to stay consistent.

**A $20 bribe looks suspicious.** Milton Rosenberg (1965) suggested a different social process. People in experiments often feel that psychologists are judging them, which he called <Term>evaluation apprehension</Term>. An unusually large payment might feel like a test of whether the student could be bought, so a student paid $20 might prove he had not been, by calling the tasks dull. This would also produce the 1959 result, without any dissonance.

## What this lens values in the study

It shows that a very small social pressure, a polite request from a man in charge, can lead people to mislead a peer and then change what they say they think. It is also a classic of experimental craft: the cover story, the embarrassed request and the doubtful woman made the situation feel real (see experimental realism on the [design page](/cases/cognitive-dissonance/design/)).

## What it would question

- Treating compliance with the request as background, rather than as something to explain.
- Studying each student alone, cut off from the friends and groups through whom people usually deal with doubt.
- Whether the rating measured a private opinion or a public presentation.
- The social meaning of the money, never asked about: a fair wage, a favour returned or a bribe?
- The relationship in the room, a young man persuading an unknown young woman, which the paper does not discuss.

## Evidence from this tradition

**Social support, and a disputed classic.** In *When Prophecy Fails* (1956), Festinger, Henry Riecken and Stanley Schachter described a small group whose leader predicted a great flood for 21 December 1954. The researchers joined the group secretly, posing as believers. The book reports that when the date passed, members who were together kept their faith and began to seek new converts, while members who waited alone tended to give up. The authors concluded that support from others is a powerful way to reduce dissonance. This classic is now disputed. Using newly opened archives, Kelly (2025) argues that the group sought publicity before the failed prophecy, abandoned its beliefs afterwards, and was influenced by the observers themselves. Baggio and Amouroux (2026) accept that the book misrepresents events but dispute that this was deliberate. Treat the case as an illustration of the idea, not as strong evidence.

**Face to face, or anonymous?** Carlsmith, Collins and Helmreich (1966) repeated the logic of the 1959 study with male high school students and three payments. When students told a female accomplice face to face that a dull task was interesting, the smaller payment produced more attitude change, as in 1959. When they wrote an anonymous essay instead, the larger payment produced more change, as reward theory predicts. A public act to a real person seems to be part of what produced the original result.

**Dissonance through other people.** Michael Norton, Benoît Monin, Joel Cooper and Michael Hogg (2003) found that students changed their attitudes on a fee increase simply after learning that a fellow member of their own university group had agreed to argue for it. They called this <Term>vicarious dissonance</Term>. The effect was strongest in students who identified strongly with their group. Dissonance, on this evidence, can be a group experience, not only a private one.

**Evidence against a purely social reading.** If the result were only self-presentation, there would be no reason for inner discomfort. But Zanna and Cooper (1974) found that students who could blame their discomfort on a pill did not change their attitudes, and Croyle and Cooper (1983) recorded increased physical arousal after people freely wrote essays against their own views. Something happens inside the person, whatever the social situation adds.

## A study this lens would design

1. Keep the boring tasks and the $1 and $20 payments.
2. Vary how public the act is: some students tell the waiting student face to face and give their name, others leave an anonymous written note for her.
3. Vary social support: some students are asked to help alongside a second "participant", actually a confederate, who agrees cheerfully; others are asked alone.
4. Measure the attitude twice: once in a face-to-face interview, and once on an anonymous questionnaire sealed in an envelope.
5. Ask each student afterwards whether he saw the payment as a favour, a wage or a bribe.

**Prediction:** if self-presentation drives the effect, it will appear in the interview but not on the anonymous questionnaire. If private dissonance drives it, it will appear on both, and will shrink when another person shared the lie.

Riya told the first-years something she did not believe, alone, with no outside reason except a polite request. The clash between "I said it was useful" and "I thought it was useless" stayed with her personally, and changing her view of the workshop was the easiest way to reduce it.

Arjun had four others saying the same thing, so he had a ready explanation: "We were all asked to say it." Their identical behaviour gives him consonant thoughts and spreads the responsibility. Dissonance theory treats support from others as a strong way to reduce dissonance, so he has less need to change his opinion. Whether an act is done alone or in a group can matter as much as the size of any reward.
