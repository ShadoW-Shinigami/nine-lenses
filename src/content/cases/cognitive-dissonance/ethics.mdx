A design critique asks whether a study could answer its question. An ethics critique asks whether it should have been run as it was. In this study the two are hard to separate, because the layers of deception that gave the researchers a clean, disguised measure of opinion are also the study's main ethical problem. This page takes the ethics on their own, so you can use them in Section E and in your own reflection.

## Two yardsticks

<Term>Research ethics</Term> are the rules for how researchers must treat the people who take part in their studies. Judging a study from 1959 needs two yardsticks.

The first is the standard of its own time. In 1959 no law and no university committee had to approve a psychology experiment. The American Psychological Association's code of 1953 was a general professional code, and the association's first principles written for research came only in 1973. Judging the study by this yardstick is fair to the researchers.

The second is the standard of today. A student in India who proposed this study now would need approval from an <Term>ethics committee</Term>, a group that reviews a study's risks and protections before it begins, applying the Indian Council of Medical Research guidelines. A journal would expect the study to meet the American Psychological Association or British Psychological Society codes. This yardstick tells you what you would have to change.

## What the paper reports, and what it does not

The 1959 paper has no ethics section, but it describes its own deceptions in unusual detail, because the deceptions were the method. Each row gives the paper's own words.

| Question | What the paper says | Where |
|---|---|---|
| How students came to take part | Students in the introductory course "are required to spend a certain number of hours as subjects (Ss) in experiments". They chose this one from a notice board, where it was listed as "Measures of Performance". No alternative to taking part is mentioned. | p. 204 |
| A lie told to the whole class | Weeks earlier, the course instructor told the class that the department was running a study "to evaluate these experiments", that some students would be interviewed afterwards, and "urged" them to be completely honest in those interviews. This was false. It was arranged so that the final interview would seem unconnected to the experiment. | p. 204 |
| A false account of the study | After the boring tasks, the experimenter gave an "unhesitant and assured false explanation of the experiment", describing a hired student who told the next participant the tasks were fun. | p. 205 |
| The request to lie | The experimenter, acting embarrassed and unsure, said the hired student had failed to come and asked the participant to take his place for $1 or $20. If the student hesitated, the experimenter pressed: "It will only take a few minutes". | p. 205 |
| The person lied to | "The girl, an undergraduate hired for this role", listened, then said a friend had found the experiment boring. The paper never names her or reports her experience. | p. 206 |
| Hidden recording | "The discussion between the S and the girl was recorded on a hidden tape recorder." | p. 206 |
| Students who refused | Three students "refused to take the money and refused to be hired". Two told the woman the truth. One "demanded her phone number saying he would call her and explain things". All were "run through the total experiment anyhow". | p. 207 |
| Debriefing | After the interview, "The true purpose of the experiment was then explained to the S in detail, and the reasons for each of the various steps in the experiment were explained carefully in relation to the true purpose." The students met the woman again. All paid students were asked to return the money, and "All Ss, without exception, were quite willing to return the money." | pp. 206 to 207 |
| Not reported | Whether students were told about the recording or asked to allow its use; whether anyone could decline the interview or withdraw their data; how the students felt on learning they had been fooled, and whether anyone helped them with that; how the woman found being lied to by dozens of men in turn. | |

Three things stand out.

**The deception had many layers, and one of them involved a teacher.** A cover story inside an experiment is common. Here the course instructor also misled an entire class weeks in advance, so that students would trust an interview that was itself part of the study. Students who were later deceived in the laboratory had first been deceived in their classroom, and told to be honest inside a situation built on a falsehood.

**The study asked people to act against their own values.** The students were misled, and they were also induced, by a polite request from an authority figure, to deceive a fellow student. Six of the 71 resisted, by refusing, by telling her the truth, or by wanting to put it right. The man who demanded the woman's phone number was troubled by what he had done before he knew the truth. Learning at the debriefing that you had lied to a woman for a dollar, and that she knew, is a different kind of experience from learning that a word list was not really a memory test.

**The debriefing was careful, and that was good practice for 1959.** The researchers explained every step, let the students meet the woman, recovered the money, and debriefed the eleven excluded students as well. The paper says the explanation was given "in detail" and "carefully". That is more than most studies of the period reported.

## The standards of the time

The American Psychological Association published its first code, *Ethical Standards of Psychologists*, in 1953. It was built from real cases of ethical difficulty submitted by members (Pope and Vetter, 1992). It was a general professional code, enforced through a complaints committee, with no legal force and no requirement for written consent, independent review or debriefing.

Deception was the normal tool of American social psychology in the 1950s. Solomon Asch's conformity studies used confederates who gave wrong answers, and Festinger's own earlier work used staged groups. A review of the leading journal found that by 1969 two thirds of its studies used deception (Sieber, Iannuzzo and Rodriguez, 1995), and a later history attributed the rise between the 1950s and 1970s partly to "the influence of cognitive dissonance theory" (Nicks, Korn and Mainieri, 1997). Course requirements that obliged students to serve as participants were standard at American universities.

The argument about deception began a few years after this study. Stanley Milgram's obedience experiments were published in 1963. Diana Baumrind's 1964 criticism of them, in the association's own journal, argued that "loss of dignity, self-esteem and trust in rational authority" are the harms most likely to last. Herbert Kelman argued in 1967 that the "widespread use" of deception had "serious" implications, for the possibility of harm, for the relationship between experimenter and participant, and for the quality of the data. Kelman's own 1953 study is the one that Festinger and Carlsmith set out to improve on, and the lie-to-a-peer design is exactly the kind of procedure he later questioned. Independent review of research arrived with the Public Health Service rule of 1966, the association's research principles of 1973, federal law in 1974 and the Belmont Report in 1979.

By this yardstick the study was ordinary, and better than ordinary in one respect: it debriefed fully and reported doing so. The instructor's lie to the class and the hidden recording were features a careful reader might have questioned even then.

## What the codes require today

Three codes matter for a student in India. The ICMR's *National Ethical Guidelines for Biomedical and Health Research Involving Human Participants* (2017) are the rules an Indian ethics committee applies, and they cover social and behavioural research. The American Psychological Association's code (2017 amendments) and the British Psychological Society's *Code of Human Research Ethics* (2021) are the codes most journals and textbooks refer to. On the points that matter here they agree.

| Requirement today | What the codes say | The 1959 study |
|---|---|---|
| Review before the study starts | ICMR section 4.0: all research with human participants, including behavioural research, must be reviewed and approved by an ethics committee before it is conducted. | None reported; none was required. |
| Consent that warns of withheld details | <Term>Informed consent</Term> means agreeing to take part after being told what will happen, including risks, and knowing one can refuse. Where details are withheld, BPS 4.1 says "it should be made clear that this is the case in the participant information". ICMR Box 9.5 calls this "authorized deception". | Students consented only to "Measures of Performance". |
| Deception only when justified | APA 8.07: deception is allowed only when justified by the study's "significant prospective scientific, educational, or applied value" and no non-deceptive method would work; never when it is expected to cause "physical pain or severe emotional distress"; and it must be explained "as early as is feasible", with the chance to withdraw one's data. BPS 7: "If the reaction of participants when deception is revealed later in their participation is likely to lead to discomfort, anger or objections from the participants then the deception is inappropriate." | Active deception at five points, plus a sixth that is unusual: the participant was recruited to deceive a third person. The core deception would probably be allowed with justification. The instructor's lie to the class would not. |
| No pressure through grades | APA 8.04(b): when research participation is a course requirement, students must be "given the choice of equitable alternative activities". BPS 8.1 asks that the requirement be stated in course handbooks so that students can choose another course. ICMR Box 6.2 lists students among groups "under a hierarchical system" who are vulnerable to pressure, and 6.9.2 asks for "extra efforts" to respect their autonomy. | Participation was required for the course, with no alternative reported, and the request invoked a professor by name. |
| Fair inducements | APA 8.06(a): avoid "excessive or inappropriate" inducements. BPS 4.12: incentives "should be at the same level for all participants". | The payment was unequal, was paid for an act the student believed dishonest, and was then taken back. |
| Recording with consent | APA 8.03: consent is needed before recording a participant's voice, except where the design involves deception and "consent for the use of the recording is obtained during debriefing". | The recorder was hidden, and the paper does not report consent at debriefing. |
| Debriefing | <Term>Debriefing</Term> means explaining the study afterwards, correcting false beliefs and reducing any harm (APA 8.08; BPS 10; ICMR 9.2.9). | Done, and done well, as far as the paper reports. |
| Protecting those who help run the study | Codes and committees also consider risks to confederates and staff. | The woman's experience is not reported. |
| Privacy | Data must not identify participants. | No student is named. |

Judged by today's codes, the study has two clear failures: the deception of a whole class by their instructor, and the hidden recording without reported consent. It has two features that would need justification but might be allowed: inducing students to lie to a peer, and paying them unequally for it. It has one clear strength: a full debriefing. The core idea, deceiving participants about the purpose of a study for a few minutes and then explaining everything, is something ethics committees approve every week, when the value is clear and the debriefing is careful.

## What critics and defenders have said

**Critics.** No journal commentary devoted to the ethics of this particular study was found, as distinct from the general debate about deception. The general critics apply directly. Kelman's 1967 objection to routine deception on grounds of harm, trust and data quality was aimed at designs like this one. Baumrind's point about "trust in rational authority" fits a study in which the trusted instructor's announcement was part of the trick. Richard Gross's textbook account notes that the researchers "deliberately created an illusion of freedom" and that "an authority figure (the experimenter) asked a favour of the participant", and then leaves the ethics to the reader as an exercise. Hock's only ethical remark is that the true purpose "cannot be revealed to the participants because this could bias their responses and invalidate the results", which is the defence, not the charge.

**Defenders.** The debriefing was full and detailed, with the confederate present for the paid groups, and it was given to all 71 students including the excluded, which was ahead of common practice in 1957. Every student returned the money "without exception", which the authors offer as evidence that nobody felt cheated once informed. Refusal was possible and happened, since 3 of the 51 students asked to lie refused, and the design relied on a sense of free choice, so open coercion would have defeated it. There was no physical risk, and the lie concerned the dullness of a laboratory task, not anything of personal consequence. Later research found that participants in deception studies generally "did not mind being deceived" and reported more enjoyment and educational benefit than participants in other studies, with objections concentrated in studies that risk harm or probe private behaviour (Christensen, 1988).

## The ethics of how the findings were used

Dissonance theory describes a lever. If people change their beliefs to match what they have done, then anyone who can get a person to act can move their beliefs, as long as the person feels the act was their own choice and the reward was small. The [Why it matters](/cases/cognitive-dissonance/importance/) page lists the uses. Several raise ethical questions.

**Persuasion without awareness.** The foot-in-the-door technique, in which a small request prepares the way for a large one (Freedman and Fraser, 1966), and the low-ball technique, in which a commitment is secured before the cost is revealed (Cialdini and colleagues, 1978), both draw on commitment and consistency, and both are taught to salespeople. Effort justification, the finding that a severe initiation makes a group seem more attractive (Aronson and Mills, 1959), explains why costly or humiliating entry binds people to groups. The person being moved does not know that the lever is being pulled. The study itself is a small example: students were led, by a scripted request, to adopt an opinion they did not hold. At the far end of the same line, Edgar Schein's 1956 account of Korean War prisoners induced to write mildly pro-Communist statements for small rewards was later read by dissonance theorists as induced compliance under duress.

**Uses for good.** The Body Project, which asks young women to argue against the thin ideal in their own words, has reduced eating disorder symptoms in controlled trials, and the "hypocrisy" method, in which people publicly advocate safe behaviour and then recall their own lapses, has increased condom use. Both are described on the importance page. The ethical difference lies in whether the person knows and agrees to the aim.

**Festinger's other study.** Three years earlier, Festinger and two colleagues had joined a small group that expected the end of the world, pretending to be believers, and reported what happened when the prophecy failed (*When Prophecy Fails*, 1956). The members never knew they were being studied. Later scholars have noted that "so many" of the group's followers "were actually social scientists engaged in covert participant observation" that the findings may have been shaped by the observers themselves (Dawson, 1999), and a 2025 study of newly opened archives argues that the book misrepresents what happened and that the observers interfered in the group's affairs (Kelly, 2025), a charge the book's defenders accept in part. The two studies together show a research culture in which deception was a method, not a problem.

## Would it be approved today?

Probably yes, in a modified form. Deception studies of induced compliance are still run. The 2024 replication across 39 laboratories in 19 countries, described on the importance page, used a cover story, took written consent to the counter-attitudinal task, told participants that taking part was "completely up to you", stopped the study if a participant refused twice, and ended with a step-by-step debriefing (Vaidis and colleagues, 2024). Its ethics approvals are published alongside its data. A committee would want, at least:

1. A consent form that says some details will be withheld until the end, and that the student can withdraw at any time, including after the debriefing, with their data removed.
2. An alternative way to meet the course requirement, so that no one has to take part.
3. No deception of the class by the instructor. The "department survey" would have to be set up some other way, or dropped.
4. Equal, modest payment to everyone, not taken back, with justification varied through choice rather than money, as modern versions do.
5. Consent for the recording asked for at the debriefing, with the tape erased if refused.
6. A debriefing that attends to feelings as well as facts: that most people agree to such requests, that agreeing says more about the situation than the person, and that the woman was part of the team.
7. A gentler version of the request to mislead a peer, or its replacement by an essay or a recorded statement, with briefing and support for anyone playing the confederate.

With these changes the study would lose a little realism and gain a great deal of trust. Whether it would still find the "less is more" effect is a separate question, which the replication evidence on the importance page addresses.

<Note title="Was it unethical? A measured answer">
By the standard of its time the study was ordinary, and its debriefing was better than ordinary. By today's standard, its central deception could probably be justified, but two features could not: a teacher misleading a whole class in the service of an experiment, and a hidden recording with no reported consent. A good evaluation names the strength and both failures, and notices that the study's ethical problem and its methodological strength are the same thing: the disguise that made the opinion measure trustworthy.
</Note>
