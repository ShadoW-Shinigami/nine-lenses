## The participants

The participants were 71 male students taking the introductory psychology course at Stanford University in California (p. 204). The course required students to spend a certain number of hours as participants in experiments. Students chose an experiment by signing their names on a sheet on a notice board. This one was listed as a two-hour experiment on "Measures of Performance".

The paper tells us nothing else about the students. It does not give their ages, their year of study, their ethnicity, their family background or their nationality. It also does not say in which year the experiment was run, only that the paper was received by the journal in November 1957.

Several other people helped run the study: the course instructor, the experimenter, the interviewer, the "girl", who was an undergraduate hired to play a waiting participant, and two raters who later scored tape recordings. The paper does not name any of them or say who played which role. It only shows that the experimenter was a man.

## A cover story planted in advance

The study began before any student arrived. In the first week of the course, when the instructor explained the requirement to take part in experiments, he also told the class about a study the psychology department was running. Because students had to take part in experiments, he said, the department wanted to evaluate those experiments so it could improve them. A sample of students would be interviewed after taking part, and they were urged to be completely honest (p. 204).

This department study did not exist in the way the students thought. It was part of the experiment. It gave the researchers a way to ask about the boring tasks in an interview that seemed to have nothing to do with the experimenter, where, in the paper's words, "we could reasonably expect frank and honest expressions of opinion" (p. 204).

A false explanation like this, designed to hide a study's real purpose, is called a <Term>cover story</Term>. Giving participants false information on purpose is <Term>deception</Term>. This study used several layers of it.

## How the groups were formed

In an experiment, each of the groups being compared is called a <Term>condition</Term>. There were three:

- **Control.** Did the boring tasks and heard the false explanation, but was never asked to say anything to anyone.
- **One Dollar.** Did the same, then was paid $1 to tell a waiting student that the tasks were enjoyable.
- **Twenty Dollars.** Exactly the same, but paid $20.

The group that is not given the experimental treatment is the <Term>control group</Term>. It shows what students thought of the tasks anyway, so the other two conditions can be compared with it.

The paper does not say how students were placed in conditions. It does not use the words "random" or "at random". Many textbooks, including Gross, say the students were randomly allocated. That may well be true, but it is not in the paper.

<SourceCheck title="Source check: random assignment">
Hock says "the participants were randomly assigned to one of three conditions", and Gross says the same. The 1959 paper does not describe how students were assigned. This matters in Section E: <Term>random assignment</Term>, meaning that chance alone decides who goes into which condition, is what allows an experiment to claim that the conditions caused the difference. You can say the study was designed as an experiment with a control group. You cannot quote the paper as saying assignment was random.
</SourceCheck>

### Not everyone's answers were used

The data of 11 students were set aside, leaving 20 in each condition (p. 207). The paper lists the reasons. All 11 were in the two paid conditions.

<Participants />

All 11 went through the whole procedure anyway and were told the truth afterwards. Hock and many other textbooks go straight from "71 male students" to "three groups of 20" without mentioning these exclusions. Notice that the exclusions were uneven: seven from the One Dollar condition, four from Twenty Dollars, none from Control. Later critics asked whether removing these particular students could have changed the results. You will meet that argument in [Section E](/cases/cognitive-dissonance/strengths-limitations/).

## The procedure

Each student went through the procedure alone. The diagram shows the steps. Steps 1 to 3 and 5 to 7 were the same for everyone. Only step 4 differed.

### Steps 1 and 2: arrival and the boring hour

<Figure src="images/cases/cognitive-dissonance/tasks.webp" alt="A young man with a crew-cut sits at a wooden table in a plain 1950s room, resting his cheek on one hand and putting a wooden spool onto a shallow tray with the other. Loose spools stand on the table. A square board of wooden pegs waits on a side table. A young man in a white shirt and tie sits nearby with a stopwatch and a clipboard." tag="reconstruction" wide>
**Step 2, the boring tasks.** The student works with one hand, as instructed, while the experimenter times him and takes notes. The peg board at the side is the second task, used in the second half hour; the paper's board held 48 square pegs, more than the illustration shows. This is an illustration based on the paper's description, not a photograph of the study.
</Figure>

Each student first waited a few minutes in the department secretary's office. The experimenter collected him and, on the way into the laboratory, mentioned "offhand and conversationally" that the introductory psychology people might want to interview him when he was done (p. 204). He claimed to know little about it.

Then, with no explanation, the student was given the first task. He had to put 12 spools onto a tray, empty the tray, fill it again, and so on, using one hand and working at his own speed. After half an hour the experimenter took the tray away and gave him a board holding 48 square pegs. His job was to turn each peg a quarter turn clockwise, then another quarter turn, and so on, again with one hand, for another half hour.

All this time the experimenter sat with a stopwatch, "busily making notations on a sheet of paper" (p. 204). This was acting. It made the student believe that his speed on the tasks was the point of the experiment. The real purpose of the hour, the paper says, was to give every student "an experience about which he would have a somewhat negative opinion" (p. 205). From the researchers' point of view, "the experiment had hardly started".

### Step 3: the false explanation

When the hour was up, the experimenter set the stopwatch back to zero, put it away, pushed his chair back and lit a cigarette, all signals that the experiment was over (p. 205). He then "explained" the study. There were two groups, he said. The student had been in the group that got no introduction. In the other group, a student hired by the department met each new participant in the waiting room, pretended he had just finished, and told them what it was like.

The experimenter showed the student a sheet headed "For Group B". On it was written:

<Quote source="Festinger and Carlsmith (1959), p. 205">
It was very enjoyable, I had a lot of fun, I enjoyed myself, it was very interesting, it was intriguing, it was exciting.
</Quote>

The point of the study, he said, was to compare how the two groups performed. None of this was true. Up to this point, every student in all three conditions had been treated in exactly the same way.

<SourceCheck title="Source check: what the Control group heard">
Hock describes the Control students as being taken straight to the interview after the tasks, while "the rest of the participants" heard the false explanation. That is wrong. The paper says the procedure was identical for all three conditions up to and including the false explanation, the "For Group B" sheet included (p. 205). The Control students differed only in not being asked to tell anyone the tasks were fun. This matters, because the Control group can only serve as a fair baseline if it heard the same story. Hock's version of the "For Group B" sheet also leaves out one phrase, "it was very interesting".
</SourceCheck>

### Step 4 for the Control group: waiting

The experimenter asked Control students to wait in the secretary's office in case the interviewer wanted to talk to them. He left them there for four minutes, came back and took them to the interview (p. 205).

### Step 4 for the paid groups: the request

<Figure src="images/cases/cognitive-dissonance/request.webp" alt="The same room after the tasks. The experimenter leans towards the student, rubbing the back of his neck with an awkward expression and holding out a single handwritten sheet. A cigarette rests in an ashtray. The student listens with a puzzled face. The spools and peg board have been pushed aside." tag="reconstruction" wide>
**Step 4a, the request.** The experimenter acts unsure of himself, as if he has never had to ask this before. The sheet is the "For Group B" list of things to say. In the real procedure, the money was paid only after the student agreed.
</Figure>

For students in the paid conditions, the experimenter's manner changed. After his smooth, confident explanation, he became hesitant and a little embarrassed (p. 205). The student who usually did the Group B job had phoned to say he could not come, he said, and another participant was already waiting. The professor in charge had suggested that this student might do it instead. If he would do it now, and agree to be on call if the regular student was ever absent again, the experimenter could pay him a dollar, or in the other condition twenty dollars.

If a student hesitated, the experimenter added things like "It will only take a few minutes" or "The regular person is pretty reliable; this is the first time he has missed" (p. 205). Once the student agreed, he was asked to read the "For Group B" sheet again. He was paid, and signed a handwritten receipt.

<Note title="How much was $20 then?">
The amounts were chosen to be very different. The experiment was run before November 1957, probably in 1956-57. Using the United States consumer price index, $1 in 1957 had about the buying power of $12 today, and $20 about the buying power of $240. A student was being offered either the price of a cheap meal or a sizeable sum for two minutes' work. (Calculated from the US Bureau of Labor Statistics CPI, September 2026.)
</Note>

### Step 4 for the paid groups: the lie

<Figure src="images/cases/cognitive-dissonance/lie.webp" alt="A 1950s department office with a typewriter on an empty secretary's desk. The young man sits turned towards a young woman in a cardigan and full skirt, smiling and gesturing as he talks. She listens politely with a slightly doubtful look. Low down, behind the half-open door of a cabinet, the reels of a tape recorder can just be seen." tag="reconstruction" wide>
**Step 4b, the lie.** The waiting "participant" was an undergraduate hired to play the part. Look for the tape recorder in the cabinet: the conversation was recorded without the student knowing.
</Figure>

The experimenter told the student, "I think the next one is a girl", took him to the secretary's office, introduced the two, and left (pp. 205-206). The secretary had gone out.

The young woman said little at first. When the student said something positive about the experiment, she said she was surprised, because a friend who had done it the week before had told her it was boring and that she should try to get out of it. Most students answered with something like "Oh, no, it's really very interesting. I'm sure you'll enjoy it." After that she listened quietly and agreed with everything (p. 206). A hidden tape recorder recorded the conversation.

After two minutes the experimenter came back, sent the woman into the laboratory, thanked the student, and wrote down his phone number, to keep up the story that he might be called again.

<SourceCheck title="Source check: who the student was talking to">
Hock describes the paid students talking to an "incoming participant" and never says that she was an undergraduate hired to play the part, or that she told each student her friend had found the experiment boring. A reader of Hock could think real students were misled. In the paper, the waiting "participant" was "an undergraduate hired for this role", a <Term>confederate</Term>: a person who works for the researchers while pretending to be a participant. Her doubtful remark was part of the script (p. 206). Hock also says the usual assistant had "called in sick". The paper says only that "something or other came up for him" (p. 205), and that the money was for doing the job now and being "on call" in future.
</SourceCheck>

### Step 5: a friendly remark, for everyone

From here on, all three conditions were treated the same. As the experimenter walked each student to the interviewer's office, he said: "Thanks very much for working on those tasks for us. I hope you did enjoy it. Most of our subjects tell us afterward that they found it quite interesting" (p. 206).

This was deliberate. The remark gave every student, in every condition, an easy way to persuade himself that the tasks had been enjoyable, if he wanted to. Because all three conditions heard it, it cannot explain differences between them.

### Step 6: the interview

<Figure src="images/cases/cognitive-dissonance/interview.webp" alt="A small 1950s office. An older man with glasses and a tweed jacket sits behind a desk with a pencil and a question sheet. Across the desk the same young student leans back with a hand on his chin, thinking before he answers." tag="reconstruction" wide>
**Step 6, the interview.** The student believes he is helping the department evaluate its experiments. The interviewer does not know which condition the student was in.
</Figure>

The experimenter asked the interviewer whether he wanted to talk to this student. He said yes, and the experimenter left. The interviewer "was always kept in complete ignorance of which condition the S was in" (p. 206). When the person collecting the data does not know which condition a participant is in, they are said to be <Term>blind</Term> to the condition. This protects against the interviewer's expectations influencing the answers.

## What was measured

The interview had four questions. For each, the student was first encouraged to talk, and then asked to give a number on an 11-point scale (p. 206).

| Question in the interview | Scale | Why it was asked |
|---|---|---|
| Were the tasks interesting and enjoyable? | -5 (extremely dull and boring) to +5 (extremely interesting and enjoyable), 0 neutral | The main measure. It is the opinion the paid students had just contradicted |
| Did the experiment give you an opportunity to learn about your own ability to perform these tasks? | 0 (learned nothing) to 10 (learned a great deal) | A check. It had nothing to do with the lie, so no differences were expected |
| Would you say the experiment was measuring anything important, with scientific value? | 0 (no value) to 10 (a great deal of value) | A possible second way to reduce dissonance: "I lied, but for an important cause" |
| Would you have any desire to participate in another similar experiment? | -5 (definitely dislike to participate) to +5 (definitely like to participate) | Related to enjoyment, but affected by other things too, so a weaker test |

### Step 7: suspicion questions and the truth

At the end of the interview, the interviewer asked the student what he thought the experiment was about, and then directly whether he was suspicious of anything (p. 206). The interviewer then took him back to the laboratory, where the experimenter, and for paid students the young woman, were waiting. The true purpose of the study and the reason for each step were explained "in detail". Paid students were asked to give the money back. "All Ss, without exception, were quite willing to return the money" (p. 207).

Explaining a study's real purpose to participants afterwards is called <Term>debriefing</Term>.

### The tape recordings

The researchers expected one objection. Perhaps the $1 students had worked harder at persuading the woman, and convinced themselves in the process. To test this, the tape recordings were typed out and scored by two raters on five dimensions, such as how positive the student's statements were and how persuasive he sounded. You will see the scores on the [findings page](/cases/cognitive-dissonance/findings/).

<Note title="Were the raters blind, and did they agree?">
The ratings were "done in ignorance of which condition each S was in" (p. 209). Agreement between the two raters, measured as the correlation between their scores, ranged from .61 to .88 across the five dimensions, with an average of .71. A correlation of 1.0 would mean perfect agreement. This is <Term>inter-rater reliability</Term>. Values around .7 are acceptable but not high.
</Note>

<Try
  scenario="A student planning a replication says: 'The experimenter's remark on the way to the interview, that most students found it interesting, should be cut. It is a leading comment and it will bias the results.'"
  question="Would cutting the remark make the comparison between conditions fairer? Explain why Festinger and Carlsmith included it."
>
Cutting it would not make the comparison fairer, because the remark was said in exactly the same words to all three conditions. Anything said to every group equally cannot explain a difference between groups. That is the logic of keeping all conditions the same except the independent variable.

The researchers included it on purpose, to make it easier for any student who wanted to persuade himself that the tasks were enjoyable. The theory predicts that only students with strong dissonance will take up that offer. So the remark makes the predicted difference easier to detect without favouring one condition. A fair criticism is a different one: the remark may raise everyone's ratings a little, so the absolute numbers should not be read as what students would say with no prompting at all.
</Try>
