A design critique asks whether a study could answer its question. An ethics critique asks whether it should have been run as it was. The two are linked, because the features that gave this study its clean result, the staged aggression, the frustration step and the hidden observers, are the same features that raise ethical questions. This page takes the ethics on their own, so you can use them in Section E and in your own reflection.

## Two yardsticks

<Term>Research ethics</Term> are the rules for how researchers must treat the people who take part in their studies. Judging a study from 1961 needs two yardsticks.

The first is the standard of its own time. In 1961 there was no law, and no university committee, that had to approve a psychology experiment before it started. The American Psychological Association had published a code of ethics in 1953, but it was a general professional code, and the association's first principles written for research came only in 1973. Judging the study by this yardstick is fair to the researchers, who worked within the normal practice of their field.

The second is the standard of today. A student in India who proposed this study now would need approval from an <Term>ethics committee</Term>, a group that reviews a study's risks and protections before it begins. The committee would apply the Indian Council of Medical Research guidelines, and a journal would expect the study to meet the American Psychological Association or British Psychological Society codes. This yardstick tells you what you would have to change.

Use both. A study can be ordinary for its time and unacceptable today, and saying so is not an attack on the researchers.

## What the paper reports, and what it does not

The 1961 paper has no section on ethics. None was expected. What it does contain are facts about the procedure from which you can judge. Each row gives the paper's own words.

| Question | What the paper says | Where |
|---|---|---|
| Who agreed to the study | The authors thank the director and head teacher of the Stanford University Nursery School "for their assistance throughout this study". Parents are not mentioned anywhere. | p. 575, note 2 |
| What the children were told | Nothing is reported. The experimenter invited the model to "come and join in the game". The test room was so separate from the school that "many subjects were under the impression that they were no longer on the nursery school grounds". | p. 576 |
| Upsetting the children on purpose | All 72 children were "subjected to mild aggression arousal". After about two minutes with attractive toys, the experimenter said these were "her very best toys", that "she did not let just anyone play with them", and that she had "decided to reserve these toys for the other children". | pp. 576 to 577 |
| What the children watched | Twenty-four children watched an adult lay the doll on its side, sit on it, punch it "repeatedly in the nose", hit it on the head with a mallet, throw it in the air and kick it "about the room", saying "Sock him in the nose" and "Kick him", for about nine minutes. | p. 576 |
| Watching the children | Judges scored each child "through a one-way mirror in an adjoining observation room". | p. 577 |
| Leaving | The experimenter stayed in the test room, because otherwise "a number of the children would either refuse to remain alone or would leave before the termination of the session". | p. 577 |
| Afterwards | No debriefing, reassurance, return of the "best toys", or follow-up is reported. | nothing reported |
| Possible harm | The authors write that watching adult aggression "may serve to weaken inhibitory responses and thereby to increase the probability of aggressive reactions to subsequent frustrations". | p. 580 |

Three things stand out.

**Nobody's consent is reported.** The nursery school's staff helped, and their permission seems to have stood in for the parents'. The paper may simply not describe a consent step that did happen, so say "the paper reports no consent", not "there was no consent". But the silence is itself evidence of the time: consent was not something authors were expected to report.

**The children were upset on purpose, and the wish to leave was treated as a problem to manage.** The "best toys" story was not true. It was an active falsehood, told to every child, designed to produce a negative feeling. The sentence about children who "would leave before the termination of the session" shows that the researchers expected some children to want out, and arranged for an adult to stay so that they would not go. A modern code treats the wish to leave as a right, not an obstacle.

**The study's own theory implies that it could leave a mark.** If watching an adult attack the doll teaches a child new aggressive acts and weakens the child's inhibitions, as the authors argue, then the aggressive condition may have taught 24 three-to-five-year-olds something their parents did not want them to learn. The paper reports nothing done to undo it.

## The standards of the time

The first modern research ethics code, the Nuremberg Code of 1947, was written by the judges at the trial of Nazi doctors. Its first principle is that "the voluntary consent of the human subject is absolutely essential", and it names "fraud" and "deceit" as incompatible with consent. But it was written for medical experiments, and psychologists of the 1950s did not treat it as a rule for their laboratories.

The American Psychological Association published its first code, *Ethical Standards of Psychologists*, in 1953. It was built from real cases of ethical difficulty submitted by members (Pope and Vetter, 1992). It was a general professional code, enforced through a complaints committee, with no legal force and no requirement that a study be reviewed before it began.

Independent review arrived later. In 1966 the United States Public Health Service began requiring committee review of the research it funded, and clarified that this included behavioural research. Bandura's study was paid for by a Public Health Service grant. Five years later it would have needed review; in 1960 it did not. The association's principles for research followed in 1973, federal law required review boards from 1974, and the Belmont Report of 1979 set out the three principles, respect for persons, beneficence and justice, that most codes now share.

So in 1961 the only safeguards were the researchers' own judgement and the goodwill of the nursery school. Deception and hidden observation were ordinary in the experimental psychology of the period. By 1969 two thirds of the studies in the leading social psychology journal used deception (Sieber et al., 1995). University nursery schools were routine research sites: the paper says the potato-print activity was chosen because earlier "studies in the nursery school" had shown that children liked it (p. 576), which shows a standing research relationship between the laboratory and the school.

By this yardstick the study was ordinary. Two features might still have troubled a careful reader in 1961: deliberately upsetting very young children, and the possibility, which the authors themselves raise, of teaching them aggression. Hock's preface to his book makes the fair point. Such studies "were carried out long before formal ethical guidelines existed and the research could not be replicated under today's ethical principles", but "the lack of guidelines, however, does not excuse past researchers for abuses", and the judgement "must now be made by each of us individually".

## What the codes require today

Three codes matter for a student in India. The Indian Council of Medical Research's *National Ethical Guidelines for Biomedical and Health Research Involving Human Participants* (2017) are the rules an Indian ethics committee applies, and they cover social and behavioural research. The American Psychological Association's *Ethical Principles of Psychologists and Code of Conduct* (2017 amendments) and the British Psychological Society's *Code of Human Research Ethics* (2021) are the codes most journals and textbooks refer to. On the points that matter here they agree.

| Requirement today | What the codes say | The 1961 study |
|---|---|---|
| Review before the study starts | ICMR section 4.0: all research with human participants, including behavioural research, must be reviewed and approved by an ethics committee before it is conducted. | None reported; none was required. |
| Parents' informed consent | <Term>Informed consent</Term> means agreeing to take part after being told what will happen, including risks, and knowing one can refuse. For children, a parent or guardian consents (APA 3.10(b); BPS 4.5; ICMR 6.5.4). The BPS adds that children "are used to being in inferior power relations with adults", so the researcher must make a special effort to show that saying no has no consequences. | Not reported. |
| The child's own assent | <Term>Assent</Term> is the child's own agreement, in words the child understands. APA 3.10(b): provide an appropriate explanation, seek assent, and consider the person's preferences and best interests. ICMR asks for assent from children aged 7 to 18 and parental consent for all; below 7 no assent is documented, but "if the child objects, this wish has to be respected". BPS 4.7: signs of lack of assent, such as "becoming withdrawn and quiet" or "looking towards exits", count as much as a signature. | Not reported. The children were taken to a "game", and some would "refuse to remain alone or would leave". |
| Deception only when justified | APA 8.07(a): only when justified by the study's "significant prospective scientific, educational, or applied value" and no non-deceptive method is feasible. BPS 7 separates "withholding some of the details" from "deliberately falsely informing the participants". ICMR 9.2.9: minimal risk, no alternative, and "an adequate plan for debriefing the participants". | Two deceptions. The "game" framing is a mild withholding. The "best toys" story is an active falsehood designed to upset a small child. |
| The right to withdraw | Participants must be told they can stop at any time without penalty (APA 8.02; BPS 4). ICMR 1.1.2: the right "to withdraw from research at any time, is paramount". A child who tries to leave is withdrawing. | The design kept children in the room. |
| Minimising and undoing harm | APA 8.08(c): when procedures have harmed a participant, take "reasonable steps to minimize the harm". BPS 10: after a procedure that induced a negative mood, "it would be ethical to induce a happy mood state before the participant leaves the experimental setting". | Nothing reported. |
| Debriefing | <Term>Debriefing</Term> means explaining the study afterwards and correcting any false beliefs (APA 8.08(a)). For small children this would mean a calm play period, the toys given back, a plain message that the grown-up was only pretending, and information for parents and teachers. | Not reported. |
| Hidden observation | BPS 7: unless those observed consent, "observational research is only acceptable in public situations where those observed would expect to be observed by strangers". | A one-way mirror in a private room. |
| Privacy | Data must not identify participants. | No child is named. The study is fine on this point. |

Judged by today's codes, the study fails on consent, assent, deliberate distress, the right to withdraw, and aftercare. It is adequate on privacy. The deception itself was small, and modern committees approve deception in child research when it is justified and undone afterwards. The problem is less the single cover story than the absence of every protection around it.

## What critics and defenders have said

**Critics.** Published criticism of the study's ethics is thinner than its fame suggests, because most critics have attacked its validity instead. Hock's only critical note is that hitting an inflated doll is not the same as attacking a person. The psychoanalytic writers Evangelia Galanaki and Konstantinos Malafantis (2022) argue that the Bandura experiments "raise the ethical issue of children's exposure to violence, with unknown short- and long-term consequences", and that the children "experienced an indirect attack with a mild traumatic character" when adults intruded on their play. One caution: the same authors write that the children came "with their caretakers' consent". Nothing in the 1961 paper supports that, so do not repeat it as fact. The [humanistic](/cases/bobo-doll/lenses/humanistic/) and [critical](/cases/bobo-doll/lenses/critical/) lens pages of this guide add further objections, but those are interpretations rather than published commentary.

**Defenders.** The case for the defence rests on the paper's own details. The aggression lasted about nine minutes and was aimed at a toy made to be hit. The frustration was, in the authors' word, "mild", lasted about two minutes, and the child was at once offered other toys. The school's director and head teacher helped "throughout this study", and a teacher who knew each child took part in the ratings, so the children were inside a familiar institution with familiar adults nearby. The experimenter stayed in the test room because some children would not stay alone, which shows attention to their comfort as well as to the data. The children's recorded remarks, such as "He's a good fighter like Daddy", read as play and judgement rather than fear. By 1963 the same research group was sending questionnaires to parents, so a channel to families existed. And no publication has documented lasting harm to any of the 72 children, though that is absence of evidence and nothing more.

## The ethics of how the findings were used

The study became a weapon in the argument about violence on television, and later about video games. The [Why it matters](/cases/bobo-doll/importance/) page tells that story. Two ethical questions follow from it.

**Knowledge creates duties.** If children copy what adults do, then every adult who is watched by a child is a model, whether they mean to be or not. Parents, teachers and broadcasters cannot claim that what children see does not matter. Bandura himself used this knowledge to help: his group treated children's fear of dogs by letting them watch another child play with one (Bandura, Grusec and Menlove, 1967), and television serials built on his theory, described on the importance page, have modelled healthier behaviour in several countries, including India.

**Knowledge can also shift blame, and travel further than it should.** A finding that children learn aggression from models can be used to blame parents or television for a child's violence, while the conditions that produce violent homes and neighbourhoods stay out of view. The [critical lens](/cases/bobo-doll/lenses/critical/) page develops this point. The laboratory measure of aggression that this study invented, acts against a toy minutes after watching, also became the ancestor of the measures that the United States Supreme Court found "not compelling" when it struck down a law against selling violent video games to children in 2011. None of the Court's opinions mentioned the Bobo studies, but the gap between a count of punches at a doll and a claim about harm from media is where that case was lost. The ethical use of the study is to add to what we understand about children, not to narrow it or stretch it.

## Would it be approved today?

Not in its 1961 form. An ethics committee would want, at least:

1. Written, opt-in consent from parents that describes the aggressive model, the toy episode and the observation in plain words.
2. An explanation suited to the child, attention to signs of dissent, and the session ending if a child wants to leave.
3. The frustration step dropped, or redesigned as a mild disappointment that parents are told about in advance and that is put right afterwards.
4. Filmed models rather than a live adult, which Bandura's own 1963 study showed to work as well, and a comparison condition that models calm or kind behaviour.
5. A kind ending: play with the withheld toys, a plain message that it was pretend, and a check on the child's mood before the return to class.
6. A note to parents and teachers about what to watch for, with a named contact, and a follow-up a week later.
7. Observers who do not know which condition a child was in.

Several of these are the changes proposed on the [stakeholder pages](/cases/bobo-doll/stakeholders/). A modern study of imitation in preschool children would be expected to obtain parental consent as a matter of course. With these changes, a study of observational learning could still be run. The question is not whether children can be studied, but how.

<Note title="Was it unethical? A measured answer">
By the standard of its time, the study was ordinary. It broke no rule that psychologists of the time treated as binding on laboratory studies, and the procedures were typical of child research in American university nursery schools. By today's standard it would not be approved. The strongest ethical charge against it is also its main scientific claim: if watching an adult attack the doll teaches aggression, then the researchers deliberately taught it to 24 small children, without their parents' reported consent, and did not report trying to undo it. A good evaluation says both things.
</Note>

Today's codes, including the ICMR guidelines an Indian committee would apply, require parents' informed consent, the child's assent, a real right to withdraw, no deliberate distress without strong justification, and a debriefing that undoes any harm.
