The outline asks you to interpret the study through at least three other perspectives. This guide covers all nine, so you can see the whole range before choosing. Each lens page follows the same order: the lens in brief, how it reads the study, what it values, what it questions, evidence from that tradition, and a study it would design instead.

If the perspectives are new to you, read the short course-wide introduction on [the nine lenses](/lenses/) page first.

## The nine readings side by side

Use this table to compare, not to replace the lens pages. Each row is one lens.

| Lens | What it sees in the study | Its sharpest question | The study it would run instead |
|---|---|---|---|
| Behavioural | Money that reinforced the lie, not the rating; students reading their own behaviour as an observer would | Is an inner drive needed at all, if observers can predict the result? | A version that measures what students do afterwards and runs observer simulations alongside |
| Cognitive | A mind weighing reasons and explaining its own act, and perhaps misremembering its earlier view | What did the students think, and did they know they had changed? | Asking students to write down their thoughts, testing what they remember of their earlier view, and measuring quick reactions as well as stated opinions |
| Psychoanalytic | Rationalisation protecting the student from guilt about deceiving a young woman for almost nothing | What unconscious feeling does the new opinion keep out of awareness? | Long open interviews and measures of defensive style, with the $1 condition only |
| Biological | A drive that was described but never measured in the body | Can the tension be seen in arousal and brain activity, and does it cause the change? | The same conditions with heart rate, skin conductance and discomfort ratings throughout |
| Humanistic | A threat to the student's picture of himself as honest, and a freedom that was manufactured | Under what conditions can people face what they did instead of defending against it? | Informed consent to some withheld details, empathic listening, and participants as co-interpreters |
| Social | A chain of social encounters: a polite request, a public lie, a remark about what others thought | Was it private dissonance or public self-presentation? | Varying public versus anonymous acts, social support, and how the attitude is collected |
| Cultural | A rule, "say what you believe", that the authors themselves limited to "our culture" | Who experiences an act as their own choice, and when? | A multi-site study varying who asks, who is affected and who will know |
| Critical | A study about lying built from lies told by people with power over the students | Whose interests does an individual, mental explanation serve? | Discourse analysis of how people account for real institutional pressure in their lives |
| Decolonial | Indian rumours and an Indian researcher at the start, and a universal law at the end | Whose knowledge becomes theory, and whose becomes data? | A study built from Indian concepts of duty, context and sincerity, with local researchers |

## How the lenses connect

The lenses are not nine separate verdicts. Some agree with each other and some disagree. Noticing which is which will make your journal stronger.

- **Behavioural and cognitive tell the same story with and without a mind.** Bem's self-perception theory and Kelley's attribution rules make nearly the same prediction: a $20 student explains his statement by the money, a $1 student by his attitude. The behavioural lens says this is inference from behaviour; the cognitive lens says it is information processing. Neither needs an unpleasant drive. The biological lens is the one that tests whether a drive exists.
- **Psychoanalytic and humanistic agree that the self is being protected.** Both read the $1 students' new opinion as a defence. Psychoanalysis calls it rationalisation and looks for unconscious guilt. The humanistic lens calls it distortion of experience to protect the self-concept, and asks how acceptance could make defence unnecessary. Aronson's self-concept version of dissonance theory sits between them.
- **Social and critical both look outside the head.** The social lens points to the request, the audience and the interview as social situations. The critical lens goes further and asks who designed those situations and for whom. Both challenge the paper's purely private explanation.
- **Cultural and decolonial ask different questions about the same phrase.** "At least in our culture" leads the cultural lens to test the finding in other places. It leads the decolonial lens to ask why the finding was taught as universal, and why the Indian research at its origin was forgotten.
- **The replication evidence cuts across all of them.** The 2024 multi-lab study, described on the [importance page](/cases/cognitive-dissonance/importance/), found that writing against one's view shifts attitudes a little, but that choice made no difference. Every lens now has to explain a smaller and less certain effect than the 1959 paper reported.

These three lenses stay close to the study's own world. Dissonance theory is a cognitive theory, the study is a social psychology experiment, and the main rival account, self-perception, is behavioural. Using them mostly restates the 1950s and 1960s debate inside experimental social psychology. The analysis will show less range.

A stronger set includes at least one lens that questions the study's assumptions from outside. For example: biological, to test whether the drive the theory depends on actually exists; humanistic or psychoanalytic, to ask what it meant for a student to deceive someone and protect his self-image; and cultural or decolonial, to take seriously the paper's own phrase "at least in our culture" and the theory's Indian origins. This shows the reader that you understand how paradigms disagree about what counts as evidence.
