## The lens in brief

The biological perspective explains behaviour and experience through the body: the brain, the nervous system, hormones and genes. For memory, it asks what physically changes in the brain when something is learned, stored and recalled.

Three ideas matter here. <Term>Encoding</Term> is the process of taking in information so that it can be stored. <Term>Consolidation</Term> is the gradual process, over hours and longer, by which a new memory becomes stable in the brain. <Term>Reconsolidation</Term> is the idea that when a stored memory is recalled, it can become unstable again for a while, and must be stabilised once more. During that window, new information may enter it. The <Term>hippocampus</Term>, a curved structure deep in each side of the brain, is central to forming memories of events. The <Term>prefrontal cortex</Term>, at the front of the brain, is involved in checking and monitoring what is retrieved.

The study is far from this lens. It measured no brain activity, no stress and no physical state. But it asks a question that brain research later took up: how can a memory change after it has been formed?

## How this lens reads the study

**The verb arrived while the memory was still forming.** In both experiments, the speed question came straight after each film (pp. 586-587). A memory only minutes old has not yet been consolidated. A biological psychologist would say that the word *smashed* entered at the point when the brain was still building a stable record of the crash. A week later, in Experiment 2, the broken glass question asked students to retrieve a memory that had been consolidated with that word already in it. On this reading, the one-week delay was not a weakness of the design. It was what allowed the change to settle into the memory.

**True and false memories look alike in the brain.** Sixteen *smashed* students said they had seen broken glass (p. 587). As far as anyone can tell, they believed it. Brain imaging suggests why this is possible. Roberto Cabeza and colleagues (2001) found that the hippocampus was "similarly activated for True and False items". Daniel Schacter and Scott Slotnick (2004) concluded that sensory activity is greater for true than for false recognition, and that the prefrontal cortex helps with "retrieval monitoring that can limit the rate of false recognition". So a false detail can feel like a real memory, and the main difference may be faint traces of what was actually seen.

**Calm students, not frightened witnesses.** The students watched films of strangers' accidents in a classroom. Their bodies were probably calm. Real witnesses to a crash are often frightened, and fear releases stress hormones that affect memory. A <Term>meta-analysis</Term> is a study that combines the results of many earlier studies. One meta-analysis found that "high levels of stress negatively impact both types of eyewitness memory", both identifying people and recalling events (Deffenbacher, Bornstein, Penrod and McGorty, 2004). The 1974 study cannot say whether stressed witnesses would be more or less open to a leading word.

**Four seconds of crash.** In Experiment 2 the accident lasted 4 seconds (p. 587). Attention can take in only so much in that time. Loftus, Loftus and Messo (1987) later tracked the eye movements of people watching a scene in which a man held a weapon. The viewers made "more eye fixations on the weapon than on the check" that appeared in the comparison version. They also remembered other details less well. This is <Term>weapon focus</Term>. A brief, dramatic event leaves many details weakly encoded, and weakly encoded details are the easiest to change.

**Nobody's brain state was known.** The paper reports nothing about the students: no ages, no sleep, no health (pp. 586-587). Later research shows these matter. Older adults in one study were more likely to say they had seen information that was only suggested, and "more confident in their false memories" (Mitchell, Johnson and Mather, 2003). A later review found that the age difference was smaller and less certain in studies that included control groups (Wylie and colleagues, 2014). People who were sleep deprived while they watched an event later produced more false memories in a misinformation task, but sleep loss after the event did not have this effect (Frenda, Patihis, Loftus, Lewis and Fenn, 2014). Any of these could have varied, unmeasured, among the 9 students in each verb condition.

## What this lens values in the study

It shows a clear behavioural effect that brain research can then try to explain. Its method, a known event followed by misleading information and a test, was later adapted for brain imaging, because a study of false memory in the brain needs to know which memories are false. Its finding that a memory can change after the event also fits what biology later found about consolidation and reconsolidation.

## What it would question

- The absence of any physical measure: no stress, arousal, attention or sleep.
- Treating all students as the same, when age, sleep and health affect how easily memories change.
- Whether calm students watching films can stand in for frightened witnesses in real accidents.
- Whether "memory change" is the right description without any evidence of what changed in the brain.
- Whether the effect would last beyond a week, which depends on how the changed memory was consolidated.

## Evidence from this tradition

**Reconsolidation.** Karim Nader, Glenn Schafe and Joseph LeDoux (2000) studied fear memories in rats. After a fear memory was recalled, the <Term>amygdala</Term>, a small structure deep in the brain involved in fear, needed to make new proteins to store the memory again. In other words, a recalled memory has to be rebuilt. Almut Hupbach and colleagues (2007) found a human version. People who learned a list, received a reminder of it, and then learned a second list later "incorrectly intermixed items from the second list when recalling List 1". The effect appeared only after a delay. The authors concluded that "reconsolidation is also a constructive process, one that supports the incorporation of new information in memory". Chan and LaPaglia (2013) used a misinformation-style procedure in six experiments and found that recalling a memory and then learning new information soon after could impair the original, in a way that "cannot be attributed solely to source confusion".

**The brain during misinformation.** Okado and Stark (2005) scanned people during a misinformation procedure. In parts of the hippocampus and the nearby cortex, stronger activity while watching the original event predicted later true memories, and stronger activity while reading the misinformation predicted later false ones. In a follow-up with Loftus, "true and false memories showed similar brain activation, but could be distinguished by this reactivation", and only "in the early regions of the sensory cortex" (Stark, Okado and Loftus, 2010). The brain keeps a faint trace of where a memory came from, but the difference is small.

**Stress and attention.** Deffenbacher and colleagues (2004) combined 27 tests of identification and 36 of recall, and found that high stress harmed both. Loftus, Loftus and Messo (1987) gave "the first direct empirical support for weapon focus" by tracking eye movements.

**An Indian study of sleep.** At the Indian Institute of Technology Guwahati, Verma and Kashyap (2019) found that sleep deprivation after learning "led to higher false memory" in 39 male volunteers, using a word-list task. This was not a misinformation study, but it adds Indian evidence that the body's state affects false memory.

**The limits of brain evidence.** The differences between true and false memories in imaging studies are found by averaging across groups of people. They do not give a reliable test for one person's memory (Schacter and Slotnick, 2004; Stark, Okado and Loftus, 2010). Evidence for reconsolidation in human memory of events is also less certain than in animals: Chan and LaPaglia describe it as "far less compelling".

<Note title="Brain tests of memory in Indian courts">
India has a history of interest in brain-based tests of what a person knows. In *Selvi v. State of Karnataka* (2010), the Supreme Court held that "no individual should be forcibly subjected to any of the techniques in question", namely narcoanalysis, polygraph tests and a brain-based test called the Brain Electrical Activation Profile. It added that even with consent, "the test results by themselves cannot be admitted as evidence because the subject does not exercise conscious control over the responses" (para. 223). A 2024 Indian study claimed that another brain-based method, which its authors call neuro signature profiling, could determine the "veracity of eyewitness recall" (Das and Kacker, 2024). That claim goes far beyond the imaging research above, which finds that true and false memories largely overlap. Treat it with strong caution.
</Note>

## A study this lens would design

1. Recruit adults of three age groups, 18 to 25, 40 to 55 and over 65, and record their sleep the night before.
2. While each person watches an accident film, track their eye movements and measure stress by heart rate and saliva cortisol, a stress hormone.
3. Show half the participants a film designed to be more frightening, with loud sound and a closer view, and half a calmer version.
4. Ask the speed question with *smashed* or *hit*. Scan a subset of participants with brain imaging while they read the question.
5. A week later, ask about broken glass and other details, with "yes", "no" and "don't know" options.

**Prediction:** false reports of glass will be more common in people who were short of sleep before the film and in people whose eyes spent little time on the area of the crash. They may also be more common in older adults. Stress will have mixed effects. The brain scans will show group-level differences between later true and false memories, but not a test that works for any one person.

The psychologist should advise against relying on the test. Imaging research finds that true and false memories produce very similar brain activity. The differences, faint traces of sensory reactivation, appear only when results are averaged over groups of people, and do not give a reliable test for one person (Schacter and Slotnick, 2004; Stark, Okado and Loftus, 2010). A witness who has absorbed a detail from a question or a conversation may believe it completely, so their brain response would not reveal the error. In law, the Supreme Court held in *Selvi v. State of Karnataka* (2010) that no one can be forced to take such tests, and that results cannot be admitted as evidence on their own.

Instead, the psychologist would recommend good interviewing. Each witness should be asked separately, as early as possible, to describe what they saw in their own words, without leading questions. The officer should record whether either witness has heard the other's account, or anyone else's. The biological lens adds one more step: note conditions that affect encoding, such as how long each witness could see the car, the lighting, and how frightened they were. Those facts help judge which account rests on a better view.
