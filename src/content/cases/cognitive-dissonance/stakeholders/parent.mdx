## What you care about

Imagine your son or daughter is an undergraduate in a psychology department in India, and the department asks students to take part in studies like this one. Your child is an adult, and the decision is legally theirs. But you still care. You want them to be safe, respected and honest. You want to know that a teacher's request is not an order in disguise. You may also worry about privacy, about money, and, if your child is a young woman, about being alone in a room with a stranger who is trying to persuade her of something.

## What worries you about the original study

- **The teachers were part of the trick.** The course instructor's announcement in class, about a department study of experiments, was used to set up the cover story (p. 204). If teachers can be part of a deception, how will your child know what to believe?
- **Your child could be asked to lie.** The experiment's key step was persuading a student to tell another student something false. Many families teach children that lying to someone who trusts you is wrong. Being asked to do it by a teacher figure sends a confusing message.
- **A recording was made in secret.** The conversation was taped without the student's knowledge (p. 206). You would want to know who keeps such recordings and for how long.
- **Saying no was not easy.** The study was part of a course requirement, and the request came from a confident adult who became embarrassed and kept adding reasons when a student hesitated (p. 205).
- **Money muddles the choice.** Twenty dollars was a large sum in the 1950s. A large payment can push a young person, especially one with little money, to agree to something they would otherwise refuse.
- **Gender and privacy.** The original was all men talking to one young woman, who is called "the girl" throughout. If a replication paired your daughter with a male stranger in an empty office, or the reverse, you would want to know who was present and why.

## Your redesign

1. **Give students a genuine choice.** Taking part should be one option among others, such as a short essay, with no effect on marks.
2. **Tell students, and through them families, what kind of study it is.** A plain-language sheet says: "Some details will be explained afterwards. You will not be harmed or embarrassed. You can stop at any time."
3. **No secret recording.** Either tell students before the conversation that it will be recorded, or do not record it.
4. **Keep payments modest and fair.** Pay for time, at a rate that would not tempt a student to do something against their values.
5. **Think about gender and setting.** Hold conversations in a room with an open door or a visible staff member nearby, and let students choose not to take part in a one-to-one conversation with someone of another gender.
6. **Name a person to contact.** Give students a faculty member outside the research team, and the ethics committee's contact, for questions or complaints.

## Why

Indian guidance supports several of these points. The ICMR's *National Ethical Guidelines* (2017) are written for health research, but their section on social and behavioural research is a useful reference. They list students among groups who may be vulnerable because they are "under a hierarchical system" (Box 6.2). They require that payments not become an "undue inducement" (Section 2.5.5). They also note that "power differences between participants and researchers in India make it difficult for people to explicitly refuse to participate", and ask researchers to watch for quiet signs of refusal such as silence or restlessness (Box 9.4).

The same guidelines describe <Term>relational autonomy</Term>: people's decisions are shaped by their families and social position, not made in isolation (Box 9.4). That is a reason to make information easy for students to share with their families, while still treating the student as the one who decides.

## What your redesign gives up

- **Fewer, different volunteers.** With a real alternative available, some students will choose the essay, and those who stay may be more cooperative than average.
- **A weaker disguise.** Telling students in advance that something is hidden, and that conversations are recorded, makes them more watchful. The result may shrink or disappear.
- **Less control.** An open door and a visible staff member change the feel of the conversation. A student who knows someone is nearby may say different things.
