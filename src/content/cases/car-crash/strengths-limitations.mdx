The outline asks for at least three of each. Below are more than three, so you can choose the ones that fit your argument. For each one, the reason is given, because a strength or limitation without a reason earns little credit.

## Strengths

### 1. Only one word changes

Within each experiment, every student saw the same films and answered a printed questionnaire that differed in a single word. Nothing else about the session changed between conditions. So when the averages differ, the word is the only obvious cause. This is high <Term>internal validity</Term>, meaning confidence that the study measured a real cause and effect within its own setting. Few experiments in psychology isolate their cause as cleanly.

### 2. A known truth to check memory against

In real cases nobody knows exactly what happened, so nobody can say for certain that a witness is wrong. Here the researchers knew. There was no broken glass in the film, so every "yes" was an error. Four of the crashes in Experiment 1 had been staged at known speeds, so the estimates could be checked against reality, which showed how poor they were (p. 586). Only a laboratory study can provide this kind of certainty about what the witness should have remembered.

### 3. The second experiment tests between two explanations

The authors did not stop at their first result. They named a dull explanation for it themselves, response bias, and designed Experiment 2 to tell it apart from a change in memory (pp. 586-587). A push on one answer should not make students "remember" a new detail a week later; a changed memory should. The second experiment also repeated the first result with new students and a new film: *smashed* again produced higher speeds than *hit* (10.46 against 8.00 mph). A study that tests its own rival explanation, and repeats its main finding, is stronger than one that reports a single result.

### 4. A control group that sets a baseline

Experiment 2 included 50 students who were never asked about speed. Their answers show how often people "remember" broken glass with no suggestion at all: 6 of 50. The *hit* group gave almost the same figure, 7 of 50. This shows that being asked about speed in a mild way did not, by itself, produce false memories of glass, and it places the *smashed* group's 16 of 50 in context.

### 5. Careful control of materials and order

The films in Experiment 1 were shown in a different order to each testing group, so no film was always judged first or last (p. 586). The broken glass question appeared in a random position among ten questions, so it did not stand out (p. 587). The statistical analysis treated the seven films as a sample of possible accident films, following Clark (1973), so that the result should apply to accident films in general and not only to these seven. Using seven films, rather than one, also protects against a result that depends on one odd film.

### 6. Objective measures

The students wrote numbers and ticked boxes. No observer had to judge or rate anything, so the researchers' expectations could not colour the scoring. The questions were printed, so no experimenter could stress the verb or say it in a leading tone. Every student in a condition read exactly the same words.

### 7. A method others could reuse, with large practical value

The study introduced a simple procedure, an event, a misleading question, then a later memory test, that other researchers could copy and vary. Loftus and her colleagues used it again within a year with barns, stop signs and numbers of demonstrators (Loftus, 1975), and it became the standard method of the research field described on the [importance page](/cases/car-crash/importance/). The finding that wording matters has a direct use: it tells police and lawyers how to question witnesses without changing what they remember.

## Limitations

### 1. Small groups and limited statistics

Each verb in Experiment 1 was heard by only 9 students. The paper reports no standard deviations, so readers cannot see how much answers varied, or how large the effect was. It reports one overall test for the five verbs and one for the three groups in Experiment 2, but no tests between pairs. When this guide tested *smashed* against *hit* alone for broken glass, the result was borderline (p of about .03, or about .06 with a more cautious test; see the [findings page](/cases/car-crash/findings/)). Table 3, which carries the argument that *smashed* did more than raise speed estimates, has no test, and its highest speed bands probably held only a handful of students.

### 2. Response bias is not fully ruled out

Experiment 2 fits the idea that the memory changed, but other readings remain. A student who read "smashed" a week earlier might not remember glass at all, but reason: "It was a smash, and smashes usually break glass, so probably yes." That is a judgement, not a memory. Students might also have guessed what the researchers wanted, a problem Martin Orne (1962) called <Term>demand characteristics</Term>: cues in a study that tell participants how they are expected to behave. Later work sharpened the doubt. Michael McCloskey and Maria Zaragoza (1985) argued that the standard test cannot show that the original memory was impaired. When they changed the final test so that the misleading option was not offered, misled and control participants did equally well. The paper's own wording, that the results are "consistent with" a change in memory (p. 585), is the right level of confidence.

### 3. A forced choice between "yes" and "no"

The broken glass question allowed only "yes" or "no", with no "I don't know" (p. 587). A student with no memory either way had to guess. Loftus and Zanni (1975) showed in the following year how much this matters: when "I don't know" was allowed, many participants used it, and the wording of the question changed how often they did. Gross cites Cohen (1993) for the wider point that forcing "yes" or "no" answers inflates errors in eyewitness experiments. With open questions, real witnesses can say "I'm not sure".

### 4. Low realism and nothing at stake

The students watched short films of other people's accidents, in a classroom, with nothing riding on their answers. Real witnesses see events in person, often feel fear or shock, and know their words may decide a case. Foster, Libkuman, Schooler and Loftus (1994) found that believing an event was real and consequential changed how witnesses performed on an identification task. John Yuille and Judith Cutshall (1986) interviewed 13 people who had witnessed a real shooting in Canada, months later. Later accounts of their study report that most resisted the misleading questions they were asked. A case study of 13 self-selected witnesses cannot settle the question. But it warns against assuming that a classroom finding applies unchanged to the street.

### 5. A narrow and undescribed sample

The paper says only "students" (pp. 586-587). It gives no ages, sexes, backgrounds or driving experience. Driving experience could matter a great deal for speed estimates. Later work suggests the verb effect varies with the witness. In a study of 128 schoolchildren and 32 college students, the word *fast* rather than *slow* raised speed estimates at every age. But *smashed* rather than *hit* raised them only for children in the seventh and eighth grades, aged about 12 to 14; the effect "did not occur at other grade levels sampled" (Lipscomb, Bregman and McAllister, 1985, abstract). Section D asks what a sample of young American students in 1974 can tell us about witnesses elsewhere.

### 6. Allocation and testing conditions are not described

The paper does not say how students were placed in conditions, or whether students in the same room received different verbs. If whole testing groups received the same verb, differences between groups, such as the time of day or the mood of the session, would be mixed up with the verb. If students in one room received different verbs, they might have noticed the difference. The paper does not say which, so readers cannot judge.

### 7. The theory came after the results

The two-sources framework is proposed in the discussion "as a framework for discussing these results" (p. 588). Only one prediction, about broken glass, was stated in advance (p. 587). The process the framework describes, two kinds of information being integrated into one memory, was not measured directly. The study shows that the answers changed in the way the framework expects. It does not show that integration is what happened.

### 8. Speed is an easy target

People are poor at judging speed, as the study's own data show (p. 586). An uncertain judgement is exactly where a hint in a question has most room to work. The effect may be much weaker for details a witness is sure about. Loftus (1979) herself reported that when misleading information was blatantly wrong about the central detail of an event, the colour of a stolen red wallet, almost everyone resisted it. A large, preregistered replication later failed to reproduce the protective effect she reported for blatant misinformation, so this boundary is less certain than textbooks suggest (O'Donnell and Chan, 2025). Later research found that people who only heard an accident, and so were more uncertain, were the most affected by the verb (McAllister, Bregman and Lipscomb, 1988). So the study shows how memory for uncertain, peripheral details can be shifted, not that any memory can be rewritten by a word.

### 9. Data collected but not reported

Every student first wrote a free account of each accident in their own words (pp. 586-587). These accounts could have shown, before any leading question, how students described the crash, and whether anyone mentioned broken glass unprompted. The paper does not report them, or the other questions on the questionnaires. A reader cannot check whether the effect also appeared in the students' own words.

<Note title="Weighing strengths against limitations">
The strengths and the limitations come from the same choice: a short, tightly controlled laboratory study. Changing one word in a printed question, with a filmed event as the known truth, gives a clean causal test (strengths 1, 2 and 6). The same choices make the situation artificial, the answers forced, and the judgement one that people find hard anyway (limitations 3, 4 and 8). A strong evaluation says that the study shows clearly *that* wording can change what witnesses report, and is much less clear about *how* it does so and *how much* it matters outside the laboratory.
</Note>

Filming the event reduced realism, but it also gave the researchers a known truth, without which they could not say for certain that any report was false. That trade-off between control and realism runs through the whole design.

Meera's point is reasonable but overstated and unsupported. "Useless" ignores that the study's main finding, that the wording of a question can change what a witness reports, was later found in many studies and shaped police interviewing practice. She could improve it in three steps. First, name what the film lacked: emotion, personal involvement and consequences. Second, give evidence that this matters, such as Yuille and Cutshall's (1986) real witnesses or Foster and colleagues' (1994) work on consequences. Third, conclude that the size of the effect in real cases is uncertain rather than zero.
