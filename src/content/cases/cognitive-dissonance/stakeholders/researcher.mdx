## What you care about

As a researcher you want a result you can trust and a clear idea of what causes it. You care about <Term>internal validity</Term>, whether the study shows a real cause and effect, and <Term>construct validity</Term>, whether it measures the thing it claims to measure. Here that thing is dissonance. You also care whether other laboratories can repeat the study and get the same result.

## What worries you about the original study

- **The experimenter knew the condition.** He made the offer, handled any hesitation and paid the money (p. 205). His manner may have differed between the $1 and $20 sessions.
- **Dissonance was never measured.** The study infers discomfort from the change in opinion. There was no check that the $1 students felt more tension, or even that they felt free to refuse.
- **The Control group differs in two ways.** Control students told no lie and received no money, so comparing them with the $1 group mixes the two.
- **Uneven exclusions.** Seven students were removed from the $1 group, four from the $20 group and none from Control (p. 207). The paper does not say whether the exclusion rules were fixed before anyone saw the ratings.
- **Small groups and one key question.** Twenty students per group, and the clearest result rests on a single rating. The supporting results did not reach significance.
- **One sample.** Seventy-one men at one American university.

## Your redesign

1. **Blind the experimenter.** Put the payment amount in a sealed envelope that the experimenter hands over without knowing its contents, and script the request word for word, ideally as a recording.
2. **Add groups paid without lying.** Use a 2 × 2 design: paid $1 or $20 (or the local equivalent), crossed with telling the waiting person the task was fun or saying nothing. This separates the effect of the lie from the effect of the money.
3. **Add a manipulation check.** After the request, ask how free the participant felt to refuse. The theory only applies if people felt they chose.
4. **Measure discomfort directly.** Use a short self-report scale of discomfort straight after the lie, and a physiological measure such as skin conductance, as Croyle and Cooper (1983) did.
5. **Set exclusion rules in advance**, apply them equally to every condition, and report the results with and without the excluded participants.
6. **Plan the sample size** with a <Term>power analysis</Term>, a calculation of how many people are needed to detect an effect of a given size, and publish the hypotheses and analysis plan before collecting data. This is <Term>preregistration</Term>.
7. **Run it in many places**, including at least one Indian university, with the same written protocol translated carefully.
8. **Measure later behaviour.** A week later, invite participants to sign up for another session of the same tasks and record who does.

## Why

Each change answers a specific weakness. Blinding deals with the <Term>experimenter expectancy effect</Term>, the risk that an experimenter's expectations change how participants behave. The 2 × 2 design separates lying from payment. Manipulation checks and discomfort measures test whether the process the theory describes is really happening, rather than assuming it. Preset exclusion rules stop data being removed selectively.

The need for large, preregistered studies is not hypothetical. A preregistered replication in 39 laboratories found no effect of choice in the modern, essay-writing version of the method (Vaidis et al., 2024; details on the [importance page](/cases/cognitive-dissonance/importance/)). None of its laboratories was in India, which is one reason step 7 matters.

## What your redesign gives up

- **Cost and time.** Many sites, physiological equipment and large samples are expensive and slow.
- **Some realism.** A recorded request and a sealed envelope may feel more artificial than a hesitant experimenter. The 1959 study's impact may have depended on that live, awkward moment.
- **Still a laboratory.** Even a perfect replication tells you about a staged lie about a dull task, not about opinions people care about.
- **Translation is hard.** A dollar amount, a "boring task" and a white lie to a stranger may not mean the same thing in every country, so differences between sites may reflect meaning rather than mind.
