## How this guide was checked

Hock's chapter was used only to get oriented. Every number, quotation and procedural detail in Sections A and E was then checked against the original 1959 paper, including its two tables. Later studies were checked against their original papers where these could be read in full, and against abstracts or reliable summaries where they could not. Bibliographic details were checked against Crossref. Where a claim could not be verified, the guide either leaves it out or says so.

Three things in the 1959 paper are often misreported, so it is worth stating them plainly. The paper does not say how students were assigned to conditions. It reports no standard deviations. And it does not say in which year the experiment was run; the manuscript was received by the journal on 18 November 1957.

## Corrections to Hock's account

If you use Hock, note these differences from the original paper. Each one is explained on the page linked.

- Table 24-1 drops the minus signs from four averages: the Control and Twenty Dollar results for enjoyment (-.45 and -.05) and for wanting to take part again (-.62 and -.25). As printed, the Control group seems to have enjoyed the tasks. ([Findings](/cases/cognitive-dissonance/findings/))
- The question 4 differences were not "clearly significant". They reached only the .08 and .15 levels. ([Findings](/cases/cognitive-dissonance/findings/))
- The Control group did hear the false explanation and see the "For Group B" sheet. Only the request to lie was different. ([Method](/cases/cognitive-dissonance/method/))
- The paper does not say participants were "randomly assigned". Gross repeats the same claim. ([Method](/cases/cognitive-dissonance/method/))
- The waiting "participant" was a hired female undergraduate, a confederate, who told each student that a friend had found the experiment boring. Hock never says this. ([Method](/cases/cognitive-dissonance/method/))
- The usual assistant did not "call in sick"; "something or other came up for him". The money was for doing the job now and being on call in future. ([Method](/cases/cognitive-dissonance/method/))
- The "For Group B" sheet included "it was very interesting", which Hock leaves out. ([Method](/cases/cognitive-dissonance/method/))
- Eleven of the 71 students were excluded, seven from the One Dollar condition and four from Twenty Dollars. Hock moves from 71 students to "three groups of 20" without mentioning them. ([Method](/cases/cognitive-dissonance/method/))
- Hock omits the experimenter's remark to every student that most participants found the tasks interesting, the class announcement that set up the interview, and the debriefing. ([Method](/cases/cognitive-dissonance/method/))
- The tape ratings did not show "no differences": the $20 students scored slightly higher on all five, and "time spent on the topic" came near significance. ([Findings](/cases/cognitive-dissonance/findings/))
- Arousal was never measured, so it is not "safe to assume" it was present. ([Biological lens](/cases/cognitive-dissonance/lenses/biological/))
- Carlsmith was not Festinger's "associate". He was an undergraduate, and the study was his senior honours thesis. ([Before the study](/cases/cognitive-dissonance/start-here/))
- The "92% versus 55%" condom figures could not be traced to any published study, and Hock merges two different hypocrisy studies. ([Why it matters](/cases/cognitive-dissonance/importance/))
- Hock tells the earthquake-rumour story as Festinger's solitary insight, places the rumours "throughout India" rather than in Bihar and Nepal, and does not name Jamuna Prasad, the Indian psychologist who collected them. ([India page](/cases/cognitive-dissonance/india/))

Gross is more accurate than Hock on the procedure and reports the exclusions correctly, but also says assignment was random, calls Aronson and Carlsmith's (1963) forbidden-toy study a "replication", and attributes to Carlsmith, Collins and Helmreich (1966) a video-recording condition that belongs to Helmreich and Collins (1968).

## The original study

Festinger, L., & Carlsmith, J. M. (1959). Cognitive consequences of forced compliance. *Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology, 58*(2), 203-210. https://doi.org/10.1037/h0041593. Read in full, with both tables, via [Classics in the History of Psychology](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/Festinger/). Page numbers follow the page markers in that transcription. Obvious typing errors in the transcription, such as "mariner" for "manner", are corrected silently in quotations.
