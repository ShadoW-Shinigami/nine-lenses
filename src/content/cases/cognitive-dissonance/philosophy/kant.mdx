## The tradition in brief

Immanuel Kant (1724 to 1804) wrote the most influential modern account of morality as a matter of reason and duty. Four of his ideas matter here.

**Duty, not reward.** An act has moral worth only when it is done from duty, for the sake of the moral law itself, and not from inclination, fear or advantage. The rule a person actually acts on is their <Term>maxim</Term>. Kant's test for a maxim is whether you could will that everyone act on it: "I ought never to act except in such a way that I could also will that my maxim should become a universal law" (*Groundwork*, 4:402).

**Truthfulness is a duty to oneself.** Lying is, for Kant, not mainly a wrong against the listener. It is a wrong the liar does to their own standing as a person.

**Persons are ends.** "So act that you use humanity, whether in your own person or in the person of any other, always at the same time as an end, never merely as a means" (*Groundwork*, 4:429). Kant's own test for "merely as a means" is whether the other person could agree to how they are being treated.

**Two standpoints, and the limits of self-knowledge.** A human being can be studied as part of nature, where every event has a cause, and respected as a free agent answerable for what they do. Introspection does not escape the first standpoint: "the human being cannot claim to cognize what he is in himself through the cognizance he has by inner sensation" (*Groundwork*, 4:451).

## What Kant says about lying, self-deception and reward

Each idea below starts with Kant's own words, then explains them in plain English, then says why they matter for this study.

**Lying is the greatest violation of a duty to oneself.** "The greatest violation of a human being's duty to himself regarded merely as a moral being (the humanity in his own person) is the contrary of truthfulness, lying" (*Metaphysics of Morals*, 6:429). The wrong does not depend on harm: lying "need not be harmful to others in order to be repudiated" (6:430). It does not even depend on anyone being deceived: "A human being who does not himself believe what he tells another (even if the other is a merely ideal person) has even less worth than if he were a mere thing" (6:429). A person may not use himself "as a mere means (a speaking machine)" (6:430).

In plain words: most people think the wrong of a lie lies in the harm to the person deceived. Kant locates it somewhere else. Speech exists to communicate what one thinks; to use it to say what one does not think is to turn one's own power of speech against its purpose, and so to throw away one's standing as a person. A <Term>duty to oneself</Term> is a duty owed to the humanity in one's own person, not to anyone else. That is why Kant says the lie is wrong even if nobody is harmed, and even if the listener is "a merely ideal person", that is, nobody real at all. A person who says what he does not believe has made himself a "speaking machine".

Why it matters here: the design's reassurance that the waiting woman was not really deceived does nothing for Kant. The wrong was done by the student to himself the moment he spoke.

**The inner lie.** Kant saw the puzzle that dissonance theory later took up. "It is easy to show that the human being is actually guilty of many inner lies, but it seems more difficult to explain how they are possible; for a lie requires a second person whom one intends to deceive, whereas to deceive oneself on purpose seems to contain a contradiction" (6:430). His answer is that self-love supplies the motive and the mind lets itself be persuaded. Someone "from self-love, takes a wish for the deed because he has a really good end in mind", as "a lover's wish to find only good qualities in his beloved blinds him to her obvious faults" (6:430 to 431). "By an external lie a human being makes himself an object of contempt in the eyes of others; by an internal lie he does what is still worse: he makes himself contemptible in his own eyes" (6:429). From this "rotten spot" the "ill of untruthfulness spreads into his relations with other human beings" (6:430 to 431). In the *Groundwork* he had already warned that "we like to flatter ourselves by falsely attributing to ourselves a nobler motive, whereas in fact we can never, even by the most strenuous self-examination, get entirely behind our covert incentives" (4:407). Everywhere "we come upon the dear self, which is always turning up".

In plain words: a lie needs two people, a deceiver and a deceived, so how can one person play both parts? Kant's answer is that <Term>self-love</Term>, the wish to think well of oneself, supplies the motive, and the mind lets itself be persuaded, as a lover is blind to the faults of the beloved. Taking "a wish for the deed", as Kant puts it, means treating the fact that you wanted to be good as if it proved that you were. Kant thought this inner lie worse than the outward kind, because it corrupts the judge inside, and he thought it was the root from which lying to others grows, the "rotten spot". He also admitted that no amount of self-examination gets to the bottom of our motives; "the dear self" keeps slipping in.

Why it matters here: a student who said the tasks were fun and then came to rate them as fun is, in Kant's vocabulary, a textbook inner lie, and Kant predicts that the student will not experience it as lying.

**Acting for a price.** Kant's shopkeeper does not overcharge an inexperienced customer, but only because "his advantage required it", so his honesty has no moral worth (*Groundwork*, 4:397). More sharply: unless duty is "exalted above everything else in actions", a person "is for sale and can be bought for a price that the seductive inclinations offer him" (*Metaphysics of Morals*, 6:483).

In plain words: Kant's shopkeeper charges a child the same fixed price as everyone else, though the child would not notice being overcharged. His act is right, but it has no moral worth, because he does it for business reasons; honesty is good for trade. The <Term>moral worth</Term> of an act, for Kant, lies in the reason behind it, which he calls its maxim, not in the act itself or in its results. The same act can be worthy or worthless depending on why it was done. His harsher sentence says what happens when advantage becomes the reason for everything: the person "is for sale", and the only question left is the price.

Why it matters here: both paid groups sold a declaration. Kant's framework asks not how much they were paid but whether the payment was among the reasons, and in both groups the students took it.

**Conscience as an inner court.** "Every human being has a conscience and finds himself observed, threatened, and, in general, kept in awe (respect coupled with fear) by an internal judge". "It follows him like his shadow when he plans to escape. He can indeed stun himself or put himself to sleep by pleasures and distractions, but he cannot help coming to himself or waking up from time to time" (6:438). A wrongdoer may paint his act "as something in which he was carried away by the stream of natural necessity", yet "the advocate who speaks in his favor can by no means reduce to silence the prosecutor within him" (*Critique of Practical Reason*, 5:98).

In plain words: Kant pictures conscience as a courtroom inside the person. There is a prosecutor, who accuses; a defence counsel, or "advocate", who makes excuses; and a judge. The discomfort you feel after doing wrong is the prosecutor speaking. The reasons you give yourself, "I was carried away", "I had no choice", are the defence counsel. Kant's claim is that the defence never fully wins. You can distract yourself with pleasures, but the prosecutor "follows him like his shadow" and you keep waking up to the case. Conscience, on this view, is not a feeling that can be reasoned away but a judgement you cannot stop making.

Why it matters here: Kant gives the unmeasured drive in this study another name. The tension the theory posits is the prosecutor, and the attitude change is the defence counsel persuading the court.

**Acting "as if" can become real, within limits.** In the *Anthropology* Kant made a claim that sounds like this study's finding. "The more civilized human beings are, the more they are actors. They adopt the illusion of affection, of respect for others, of modesty, and of unselfishness without deceiving anyone at all, because it is understood by everyone that nothing is meant sincerely by this." And: "when human beings play these roles, eventually the virtues, whose illusion they have merely affected for a considerable length of time, will gradually really be aroused and merge into the disposition" (7:151). He approved of this, with a condition. The pretence must be transparent and aimed at the good. "It is only the illusion of good in ourselves that must be wiped out without exemption" (7:153).

In plain words: this passage surprises people who think Kant was against all pretence. He says that civilised life runs on polite performances: we act more friendly, modest and interested than we feel, and nobody is fooled, because everyone knows the convention. He approves, because playing a virtue long enough can grow into having it. Kant himself says, then, that behaviour can change the person who performs it. But he sets two conditions. The pretence must deceive nobody, and it must be aimed at the good. Pretending to oneself about one's own goodness is the one illusion he says must be rooted out entirely.

Why it matters here: Kant would accept the study's finding that saying can change believing, and would say that this case breaks both of his conditions. The student set out to deceive a listener, and the belief he changed, about the tasks, was changed to protect his picture of himself.

**Character, and its one proof.** To have character is to bind oneself "to definite practical principles" by one's own reason (*Anthropology*, 7:292). "The imitator (in moral matters) is without character" (7:293). And "the only proof within a human being's consciousness that he has character is that he has made truthfulness his supreme maxim, in the heart of his confessions to himself as well as in his behavior toward everyone else" (7:295). Kant illustrated the point with a man ordered, on pain of death, to give false testimony against an honest person. Whether he would resist, "He would perhaps not venture to assert", but that he could resist "he must admit without hesitation", because "he can do something because he is aware that he ought to do it" (*Critique of Practical Reason*, 5:30).

In plain words: <Term>character</Term>, for Kant, is not a personality you are born with or drift into. It is a decision to live by principles you have set yourself, often made once and decisively. He gives a list of what a person of character will not do, and the first item is to say what is false. In fact he says that truthfulness, to others and in one's "confessions to himself", is the only sure sign that a person has character at all. His example of the false witness makes the point about freedom. A man ordered to lie under threat of death cannot be sure he would resist, but he knows he could, and he knows it because he knows he ought to. Being aware of a duty is, for Kant, how we discover that we are free.

Why it matters here: the six students who refused to lie or undid the lie are, in Kant's terms, the ones who showed character, and their existence is what makes the others answerable.

## What Kant says about knowing, believing and being persuaded

Kant's ethics sits on top of a theory of knowledge, or <Term>epistemology</Term>, which he set out in the *Critique of Pure Reason* (1781, second edition 1787). One short section of that book, "Of opinion, knowledge, and belief", bears closely on this experiment, although Kant wrote it about belief in general and especially about religious belief. It separates beliefs that rest on the object from beliefs that rest on something about the believer, and it gives tests for telling them apart. Together with his rules for thinking well, it explains how Kant would judge the $1 student's new opinion, and why, unusually for these pages, his epistemology also supports the way the study was designed.

**Persuasion feels exactly like conviction from the inside.** "The holding of a thing to be true is a phenomenon in our understanding which may rest on objective grounds, but requires, also, subjective causes in the mind of the person judging" (*Critique of Pure Reason*, A820/B848). "If a judgement is valid for every rational being, then its ground is objectively sufficient, and it is termed a conviction. If, on the other hand, it has its ground in the particular character of the subject, it is termed a persuasion." "Persuasion is a mere illusion, the ground of the judgement, which lies solely in the subject, being regarded as objective" (A820/B848). And: "Persuasion, accordingly, cannot be subjectively distinguished from conviction" (A821/B849). Kant even allows: "Persuasion I may keep for myself, if it is agreeable to me; but I cannot, and ought not, to attempt to impose it as binding upon others" (A821/B849).

In plain words: every belief is also an event in a mind, with psychological causes. Kant's question is what is really holding the belief up. A <Term>conviction</Term>, in his sense, rests on grounds that would move any reasoning person, because they concern the object. A <Term>persuasion</Term> rests on something about the believer, such as a wish, a situation or a need for comfort, but presents itself to him as a belief about the object. From the inside the two feel the same. Kant says a person may keep a persuasion privately if it makes him feel better, but may not put it forward to others as true. (The German word for "if it is agreeable to me" means, more literally, "if I feel well with it".)

Why it matters here: on Festinger's own explanation, the real ground of the $1 student's rating was his need to justify a lie told for very little money. That is a fact about him, not about the spools and pegs. Yet he reported it as a fact about the tasks. This fits Kant's definition of persuasion exactly, and Kant's next sentence explains why the report was sincere: persuasion "cannot be subjectively distinguished from conviction". So Kant gives a precise name to what dissonance theory calls reduction: keeping a persuasion because one feels better with it. Kant would add one point the theory leaves out. The student did not keep it to himself. He offered it as his honest opinion to an interviewer, after his class had been urged to be completely honest in such interviews. That is the step Kant says one "ought not" to take.

**The test of a belief is whether it holds for other people's reason.** "Truth depends upon agreement with the object, and consequently the judgements of all understandings, if true, must be in agreement with each other" (A820/B848). "Conviction may, therefore, be distinguished, from an external point of view, from persuasion, by the possibility of communicating it and by showing its validity for the reason of every man" (A821/B849). Kant's German word for this test is *Probirstein*, a touchstone, the stone once used to test gold. He adds a second remedy: "If we can, in addition to this, develop the subjective causes of the judgement, which we have taken for its objective grounds, and thus explain the deceptive judgement as a phenomenon in our mind ... we can then expose the illusion and need be no longer deceived by it". But he warns: "if its subjective cause lies in our nature, we cannot hope altogether to escape its influence" (A821-822/B849-850).

In plain words: if a belief is true, it agrees with the facts, so everyone judging the same facts properly should agree with it. The outward test of a belief is therefore whether the grounds that move me would move any other reasoning person. A second test is to explain the belief by its psychological causes alone. If you can fully explain why someone holds a belief without mentioning the object at all, you have good reason to treat it as an illusion. Kant's warning is that an illusion rooted in human nature keeps its pull even after it has been exposed, as a stick still looks bent in water after you know it is straight.

Why it matters here: here Kant's epistemology supports the study. The control group is exactly "the reason of every man" applied to the same object: the same hour of tasks, the same false explanation, the same remark on the way out. They rated the tasks -0.45. The $20 group, who also lied, agreed with them at -0.05. Only the $1 group's judgement failed to agree with others judging the same thing, which by Kant's test marks it as private. And the paper then does what Kant's second remedy asks. It explains the $1 rating entirely by the student's situation, without needing any fact about the tasks. Read this way, dissonance theory is a theory of illusion, and the experiment is a method for exposing one. Kant's warning still applies: a student who had read the theory would probably still feel its pull.

**Opinion, belief and knowledge, and the test of a bet.** "Opinion is a consciously insufficient judgement, subjectively as well as objectively. Belief is subjectively sufficient, but is recognized as being objectively insufficient. Knowledge is both subjectively and objectively sufficient" (A822/B850). To test how firmly someone holds a view, Kant proposed a wager: "The offer of a bet startles him, and makes him pause. Sometimes it turns out that his persuasion may be valued at a ducat, but not at ten" (A824/B852). "Thus pragmatical belief has degrees, varying in proportion to the interests at stake" (A825/B853).

In plain words: an <Term>opinion</Term>, for Kant, knows that it lacks enough support. A <Term>belief</Term> is firm for me but admits that it lacks proof for everyone. <Term>Knowledge</Term> is firm for me and proved for everyone. All three require the person to know the status of what he holds. A persuasion fits none of them, because it mistakes its own status. The bet is a practical test. A man may confidently assert something until you ask him to stake money on it. He might risk one ducat, a gold coin, but not ten, and the gap shows how firm his view really is.

Why it matters here: the interview cost the student nothing. It was presented as a department survey with no consequence, so on Kant's account it measured persuasion at zero stake. Kant would suggest an obvious extension: offer the $1 students a wager, for example on how a fresh group would rate the tasks, and see whether +1.35 survives one ducat, then ten. He would also enjoy an irony. The experiment used money to buy a statement. Kant used money to test a belief. The two uses run in opposite directions. Paying a person to say something weakens the link between what he says and what he believes. Asking him to bet on a belief makes the link stronger.

**Think for yourself, and do not pay others to think for you.** Enlightenment is the exit from a minority that "is self-incurred when its cause lies not in lack of understanding but in lack of resolution and courage to use it without direction from another" ("What is enlightenment?", 8:35). "It is because of laziness and cowardice that so great a part of humankind ... nevertheless gladly remains minors for life", for "I need not think, if only I can pay; others will readily undertake the irksome business for me" (8:35). "Precepts and formulas, those mechanical instruments of a rational use, or rather misuse, of his natural endowments, are the ball and chain of an everlasting minority" (8:36).

In plain words: a <Term>minor</Term>, here, is anyone who lets someone else do his thinking. Kant says most people stay minors not because they are stupid, but because thinking for oneself is tiring and takes courage. It is easier to take a judgement ready-made from a book, an expert or an authority, and the line "I need not think, if only I can pay" shows how easily that dependence can be bought. Ready-made "precepts and formulas" keep people in it.

Why it matters here: two details of the procedure offered the students ready-made judgements. The sheet headed "For Group B", which each paid student read before meeting the woman, gave him the words to say. The experimenter's remark, on the way to the interview, that most students had found the tasks interesting was the voice of an authority telling them what others had judged. Every group heard it, and the control and $20 groups did not move, so the remark cannot explain the result by itself. But if a $1 student leaned on it, Kant would read that as a person using another's judgement when it suits his inclination. Writing about people who follow religious formulas laid down by others, Kant notes that they do so partly "in order to be able to push the blame on to someone else" (*Anthropology*, 7:200). Applying this to the experiment is the page's extension.

**Consistency is the hardest rule of thinking, and it comes last.** Kant gave three rules for sound thinking: "1° to think for oneself; 2° to put ourselves in thought in the place of every one else; 3° always to think consistently" (*Critique of Judgment*, section 40, 5:294). He warned that "The third maxim, viz. that of consecutive thought, is the most difficult to attain, and can only be attained by the combination of both the former, and after the constant observance of them has grown into a habit" (5:295). The second rule means "comparing our judgement with the possible rather than the actual judgements of others" (5:294). The opposite of reasoning well is what Kant called rationalising: "Rationalizing (without sound reason) is a use of reason that misses its final end, partly from inability, partly from an inappropriate viewpoint" (*Anthropology*, 7:200).

In plain words: Kant's rules are an order of work. First, judge for yourself rather than by authority. Second, check your judgement against how it would look from anyone else's standpoint, imagining how a fair-minded person would judge, not just counting the opinions you happen to hear. Third, think consistently with yourself. He calls the third rule the hardest, and says it can only be reached through the first two. Reasoning that has the form of thought but not its aim he calls <Term>rationalising</Term>, and his word for it is close to the modern one.

Why it matters here: this is Kant's sharpest disagreement with dissonance theory, and it is a disagreement about a word. Festinger's students restored consistency between what they said and what they thought. Kant has a rule of consistency too, but he insists it is reached only after thinking for oneself and from everyone's standpoint. Consistency reached by abandoning both, by adopting a belief because it fits an act one wants to excuse, is what he calls rationalising. So what the paper calls restored consonance, Kant would call counterfeit consistency: the form of his third rule without the first two. In one respect the $20 student is the more consistent thinker in Kant's sense, because his judgement of the tasks still agrees with everyone else's. His inconsistency is between word and belief, and Kant treats that as a lie, not as a failure of thinking.

**People who know they are watched change, and the observer cannot see the mind at work.** "If a human being notices that someone is observing him and trying to study him, he will either appear embarrassed (self-conscious) and cannot show himself as he really is; or he dissembles, and does not want to be known as he is" (*Anthropology*, 7:121). And: "when the incentives are active, he does not observe himself, and when he does observe himself, the incentives are at rest" (7:121). Self-observation brings its own errors: "without noticing it, we make supposed discoveries of what we ourselves have carried into ourselves" (7:133). In 1786 Kant concluded that the study of minds could not be an experimental science, partly because "the observation itself, alters and distorts the state of the object observed" (*Metaphysical Foundations of Natural Science*, Preface, 4:471).

In plain words: Kant lists the obstacles to a science of human beings. People who know they are being studied become awkward or put on an act. While a strong motive is at work, we do not watch ourselves, and by the time we look, the motive has gone. When we search ourselves, we tend to "discover" what we brought with us. And the act of observing changes what is observed. To <Term>dissemble</Term> is to hide your real feelings behind a show.

Why it matters here: the design answers Kant's first obstacle directly. The students were told earlier in the course about a department survey, the interviewer did not know their condition, and the rating was not supposed to look like part of the experiment. That is a technical solution to people who "dissemble" when watched. Kant's second obstacle describes the interview exactly. The student rated the hour after the pressure was over, so the rating was a reconstruction. And his line about "supposed discoveries" fits the $1 student, who "discovered" that the tasks were interesting after carrying that judgement in from his speech to the woman. The study does not overturn Kant's 1786 verdict, but it partly avoids the problem. By comparing groups rather than looking inside any one mind, and by hiding its purpose, it gets evidence about the mind without asking anyone to watch himself. Kant would still say that the study gives knowledge of how the student appears from outside, not of his mind as it is in itself.

## Reading the study through this tradition

**The lie to the waiting woman.** The students told a young woman, hired to play a participant, that the tasks were interesting, using words from a sheet headed "For Group B". The design's safeguard, that nobody was really deceived, is exactly what Kant's ethics of lying sets aside. The wrong is in the speaker's use of his own speech against his own belief, and it stands "even if the other is a merely ideal person". Speaking lines he had been given on a sheet makes the student, in Kant's phrase, a speaking machine. Festinger and Kant agree that the lie is the act that matters. For Festinger it is a lever. For Kant it is a self-inflicted wound.

**One dollar or twenty: no grading of the wrong, but a different aftermath.** Both paid groups sold a declaration, and Kant does not rank the wrong by the price. What differs is what happens afterwards. The $20 student has a motive he can admit, like the prudent shopkeeper: no moral worth, but no inner lie either. The $1 student has a motive too small to admit, and so "takes a wish for the deed", re-describing a boring hour as enjoyable to keep his picture of himself. Kant's remark that we never get behind our covert incentives predicts that the student will not experience this as lying to himself. Kant and Festinger both expect the small inducement to produce the most inner work. They name the result differently. Festinger calls it consistency restored. Kant calls it the "rotten spot" from which further untruthfulness spreads.

**The course requirement and the professor's request.** The students were required to serve as participants, and the request came from an embarrassed experimenter speaking for a professor. Kant's essay on enlightenment describes a society of guardians who tell people "Do not argue but drill!" and finds that "It is so comfortable to be a minor!" (8:35, 37). He allows that institutions need people to act "merely passively" in their offices. He never allows that this licenses untruth: even a statement one "is compelled to make by an unjust constraint" must be truthful (8:426). Later research found that the dissonance effect depends on students feeling free to refuse. Kant would say that the morally interesting moment was always the choice, and that "feeling free" and being answerable are not the same thing.

**The students who refused or told the truth: the gallows case made real.** Of the 11 students set aside, three refused the money and the job, two told the woman the truth, and one wanted her number so that he could explain. For Kant these six are the most important people in the experiment. They show that the request could be refused, which is what makes the compliers answerable for complying: "he can do something because he is aware that he ought to do it". The paper reports them frankly and then removes them, because they did not produce the behaviour. Kant would say that the analysis thereby removed the evidence of freedom and kept only the mechanism.

**The experimenters' deception: persons treated merely as means.** A false announcement to a whole class, a false title on a notice board, a false explanation, a fake absent assistant, a hidden recorder. Kant's test for using someone merely as a means is whether they "cannot possibly agree" to the treatment (*Groundwork*, 4:429 to 430). A cover story is built so that they cannot. The experimenters told more lies, more carefully, than any student did, and did so inside a course whose teachers are, on Kant's account, the "experimental (technical) means" of moral education through their own example (*Metaphysics of Morals*, 6:479). Kant was not against experiments. He praised experimental schools and warned that "the results of an experiment are often entirely different from what we expected" (*On Education*, section 20). His experiments were to be run in the open. The careful debriefing and the returned money are, to borrow a phrase Kant used of the virtues in social circulation, "small change" (*Anthropology*, 7:152), not a cure. The comparison is this page's, not Kant's.

**The unmeasured drive, and another name for it.** Dissonance was never measured; it was inferred from the ratings. Kant holds that self-knowledge through inner sense gives only appearances, so neither the student's report nor Daryl Bem's later idea, that the student inferred his attitude from his own behaviour like an outside observer, reaches the real cause. Both describe the student as a clockwork of representations. Kant also offers a rival name for the unpleasant state the theory posits: conscience. The discomfort after acting against one's belief is the inner prosecutor. Excuses are the defence counsel. And Kant predicts that the defence never fully wins: the prosecutor "follows him like his shadow". The relief Festinger measured would be, for Kant, the court being talked round, not a verdict changed. The guide presents this as a competing interpretation, not a refutation.

## Where it agrees, and where it clashes

Kant and the paper agree that the lie is the act that matters, that a small inducement does more inner work than a large one, and that behaviour can change what a person comes to believe. The *Anthropology* says as much about role-play growing into disposition.

They clash on the names. What Festinger calls dissonance, Kant calls conscience. What Festinger calls reduction of dissonance, Kant calls an inner lie. What the paper calls a consonant cognition, the $20 payment, Kant calls a price. And where the paper treats the students who refused as lost data, Kant treats them as the proof that everyone else could have refused.

## Questions it would put to the researchers

- Was the student's lie wrong? Your paper has no word for it.
- You offered money to make a person say what he did not believe. Did you consider that you were buying a declaration, and what that makes of the seller?
- What did the six who refused or confessed show you, and why did you remove them?
- Could any participant have agreed to the procedure had he known it? If not, in what sense was he a participant rather than an instrument?
- You call the unpleasant state "dissonance" and never measure it. Why is it not conscience?
- You taught a class of students, in a psychology course, that the department lies to them for good reasons. What example did you set?
- Your theory says the $1 student restored consistency. By your own account he now believes something false about his afternoon. Is that a restoration or a loss?
- Is the $1 student's rating a conviction or a persuasion? By your own explanation its ground is his need to justify himself, not the tasks.
- What would the $1 students have staked on their rating? You paid them for a statement; you never asked them to bet on a belief.
- Five students saw through your cover story and were removed. Were they not the ones using their own understanding?
- Your rating came after the incentive had gone. When the incentive is active a person does not observe himself, and when he observes himself the incentive is at rest. What exactly did the interview measure?

## Cautions

- **How absolute is Kant on lying?** In "On a supposed right to lie" he refused any exception, even to a murderer asking where a friend is hiding, because one excused lie undoes the trust that makes speech work. But that essay concerns duties of right, the duties that law can demand of everyone, rather than duties of virtue, which concern a person's own motives, as Kant's own footnote says (8:426), and he treated polite formulas such as "your obedient servant" as deceiving nobody (6:431). Scholars disagree about how strict his position is. The safe statement is that Kant held truthfulness to be an unconditional duty and refused the murderer exception.
- **Kant did not "anticipate cognitive dissonance".** He described self-deception and conscience, which is different. He also did not deny that behaviour has causes; he allowed "psychological causality" and wrote an empirical *Anthropology*. His claim is that causal explanation is one standpoint and cannot settle questions of worth.
- **"The more civilized human beings are, the more they are actors" is partly approval**, not a complaint. Do not quote it as Kant's attack on hypocrisy.
- **The lectures on education were compiled from Kant's notes** by Rink in 1803. Cite them as lectures.
- **"Belief" in Kant mostly means religious and moral faith.** The triad of opinion, belief and knowledge, and the test of a bet, are general, but Kant's own examples of belief are about God and the future life. Applying them to an interview rating is this page's extension.
- **Kant's third maxim is consistency of thought with itself**, the "consecutive" or logical way of thinking. It is not agreement between belief and act. Do not present Kant as a forerunner of a need for consistency.
- **Meiklejohn's translation leaves out the word "touchstone".** The German *Probirstein* is in Kant's text; the English quotation above does not contain it. Do not put "touchstone" inside a Meiklejohn quotation.
- **Kant's 1786 view that psychology cannot be an experimental science** is his claim, not a settled result. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy (entry "Kant's View of the Mind and Consciousness of Self") notes that his views on empirical psychology are "not entirely consistent".
- **Kant is not hostile to feeling.** He said that beneficence from sympathy "deserves praise and encouragement but not esteem" (4:398). Whether that is fair to feeling is contested; do not write that a good person, for Kant, acts coldly.

The dissonance account says that the second reviewer had too little justification for praising a weak paper, felt an unpleasant tension, and reduced it by coming to believe the praise. The first had a good enough reason and felt no tension.

Kant agrees about the direction of events and disagrees about the names. Both reviewers made a declaration they did not believe, which is a lie, and a violation of a duty to oneself; the board seat does not grade the wrong. The first reviewer can admit his motive, like the shopkeeper who is honest for profit, so he committed no second wrong, though his report has no worth. The second reviewer has "taken a wish for the deed": he now believes something false about the paper to protect his picture of himself as an honest judge. That is the inner lie, the "rotten spot" from which further untruthfulness spreads, and what the theory calls consistency restored, Kant calls a loss.

His advice to the second reviewer follows from the first duty to oneself, to "know your heart": admit that he softened a report to please an editor; treat the discomfort as the prosecutor in the court of conscience rather than as a problem to be solved; and let action follow the admission, by writing to the editor to restore his judgement or by declining to review for that editor again. The concepts in play are duty against inclination, the inner lie, and conscience as an inner court.
