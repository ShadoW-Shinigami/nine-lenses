## The research question

The paper states its aim in one sentence. Having shown that people are poor at estimating speed, it says:

> The present research was conducted to investigate one such variable, namely, the phrasing of the question used to elicit the speed judgment. (p. 585)

"One such variable" means one of the things that might influence a speed estimate. "Elicit" means to draw out. In plain words: **does the wording of a question about speed change the speed a witness reports?**

The second experiment adds a further question: **if the wording changes the answer, does it also change the memory?** The paper puts it as a choice between two explanations, which the next sections explain.

## What the researchers expected

A <Term>hypothesis</Term> is a specific, testable prediction made before the results are known. The paper does not use the word "hypothesis", and for Experiment 1 it states no formal prediction. Its expectations can be read from the text.

**Experiment 1: a more violent verb, a higher speed.** Before the method, the paper notes that verbs such as *hit* and *smashed* may describe "differential rates of movement" and different "likely consequences". In its words, "The impact of the accident is apparently gentler for *hit* than for *smashed*" (p. 586). The expectation is that the question with *smashed* will produce higher speed estimates than the question with *hit*. The paper also used three other verbs, *collided*, *bumped* and *contacted*, but says nothing in advance about where they would fall.

**Experiment 2: a more violent verb, a false detail remembered.** Here the paper states its expectation directly. There was no broken glass in the film, "but, since broken glass is commensurate with accidents occurring at high speed, we expected that the subjects who had been asked the *smashed* question might more often say 'yes' to this critical question" (p. 587). "Commensurate with" means fitting with, or in proportion to.

<SourceCheck title="Source check: an order for the five verbs">
Gross says that "by implication" the five verbs were expected to produce "increasingly higher speed estimates", with *contacted* the lowest and *smashed* the highest. The paper makes no prediction about the order of the five verbs. It discusses only *hit* and *smashed* (p. 586). The order *contacted, hit, bumped, collided, smashed* is the order of the results in Table 1, not a prediction. One detail shows why this matters: *hit* produced a lower average than *bumped*, which few people would have predicted. Describe the expectation as "more violent verbs such as *smashed* would produce higher estimates than gentler ones such as *hit*".
</SourceCheck>

## Two possible explanations

After Experiment 1, the paper sets out two ways the verb could have its effect (p. 586).

**Response bias.** A <Term>response bias</Term> is a tendency to choose one answer over another for reasons other than what you remember. Imagine a student who is unsure whether to say 30 or 40 miles per hour. The verb *smashed* tips the answer towards 40. The student's memory of the accident is unchanged. Only the answer to this question moves.

**A change in the memory.** The verb *smashed* may change the student's <Term>memory representation</Term>, the stored version of the event in the mind, so that the student "sees" the accident as more severe than it was. If so, the change should show up in later answers too. The student should "remember" other details that did not happen but fit a severe accident.

These two explanations predict different things a week later. Experiment 2 was built to tell them apart.

## The theory behind the predictions

The paper's theory comes mostly in its final discussion. It proposes that "two kinds of information go into one's memory for some complex occurrence" (p. 588):

1. information "gleaned during the perception of the original event", that is, what the witness saw;
2. "external information supplied after the fact", such as the wording of a question.

Over time, the paper says, information from these two sources "may be integrated in such a way that we are unable to tell from which source some specific detail is recalled. All we have is one 'memory.'" (p. 588). The question with *smashed* supplies a piece of external information, that the cars did indeed smash into each other. Combined with what was seen, it produces the memory of a more severe accident. Broken glass fits such an accident, so the witness is more likely to think it was there.

Textbooks usually describe this as Bartlett's idea of <Term>reconstructive memory</Term>, introduced on the [previous page](/cases/car-crash/start-here/), made specific and testable. The paper itself does not cite Bartlett. It links its idea to Carmichael, Hogan and Walter (1932), whose participants drew a shape to look more like the name it had been given, and to Daniel (1972), who found that verbal labels also shift recognition memory for shapes. Loftus and Palmer argue that the question "effectively" labels the accident "a smash" (p. 588).

<Note title="Theory before or after?">
The paper presents the two-sources framework after its results, as "a framework for discussing these results" (p. 588). The expectation for Experiment 2 was stated in advance, but the full framework was proposed afterwards. This is common in short papers, but it means Experiment 2 is the only test of a prediction that the paper states in so many words. Keep this in mind when you judge, in Section E, how strongly the study supports the theory.
</Note>

## The variables

An experiment changes some things and measures others. The thing the researcher changes on purpose is the <Term>independent variable</Term>. The thing measured to see whether it changed is the <Term>dependent variable</Term>.

| Variable | Type | Levels or measure |
|---|---|---|
| Verb in the speed question, Experiment 1 | Independent variable | *smashed*, *collided*, *bumped*, *hit* or *contacted* |
| Speed estimate, Experiment 1 | Dependent variable | The speed the student wrote, in miles per hour |
| Speed question, Experiment 2 | Independent variable | Asked with *smashed*, asked with *hit*, or not asked at all |
| Speed estimate, Experiment 2 | Dependent variable | The speed written by the two groups who were asked |
| "Did you see any broken glass?" one week later | Dependent variable, the key one in Experiment 2 | "Yes" or "no" |
| The films | Kept the same within each experiment | Seven films in Experiment 1, one film in Experiment 2 |
| The students | Not measured | The paper gives no ages, sexes or other details |

In Experiment 2, the group that was not asked about speed is the <Term>control group</Term>. It shows how often students say "yes" to the broken glass question when nothing in the first questionnaire suggested a violent crash.

<Try
  scenario="A classmate reads Experiment 1 and says: 'This only shows that students gave higher numbers when the question sounded more dramatic. It does not prove anything about memory.'"
  question="Is your classmate right about Experiment 1? Explain how Experiment 2 answers the objection."
>
Your classmate is right about Experiment 1 on its own. The paper itself accepts that the higher estimates could be a response bias: unsure students choosing a higher number because the verb suggested one, with their memory of the film unchanged (p. 586). Experiment 1 cannot tell this apart from a real change in memory.

Experiment 2 answers the objection by measuring a different thing at a different time. A week later, students were asked about broken glass, a detail that was not in the film and was not mentioned in the speed question. A push on one answer should not make students "remember" a new detail a week later. A change in the memory of the accident should. More "yes" answers in the *smashed* group would therefore support the memory explanation. The [findings page](/cases/car-crash/findings/) shows what happened.
</Try>
