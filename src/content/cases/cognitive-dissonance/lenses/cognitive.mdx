## The lens in brief

Cognitive psychology studies mental processes: how people notice, interpret, remember and reason about information. It grew in the 1950s and 1960s as a reaction against behaviourism. Ulric Neisser named the field in his 1967 book *Cognitive Psychology*, and George Miller's work on memory helped launch it. In social psychology, the most important early cognitive thinker was Fritz Heider, who studied how ordinary people explain the behaviour of others and themselves.

Two ideas matter most for this case. <Term>Cognitive consistency</Term> is the idea that people prefer their beliefs, feelings and actions to fit together. <Term>Attribution</Term> is the process of explaining why something happened, for example deciding whether a person acted because of the situation or because of what they are like.

This lens is the closest of the nine to the study's own perspective. The theory has "cognitive" in its name. But a cognitive psychologist would notice something odd: the study is about thoughts, yet it measured none.

## How this lens reads the study

**One theory in a family of consistency theories.** Heider's <Term>balance theory</Term> (1946, 1958) says that people feel strain when their likes and dislikes do not fit together, for example when a friend loves a film you hate. Osgood and Tannenbaum's congruity theory (1955) made a similar claim. Dissonance theory was the most general of these consistency theories. Its accounting rule, that dissonance depends on the dissonant cognitions divided by all the relevant ones (pp. 203-204), treats the mind as something that weighs pieces of knowledge against each other. A cognitive psychologist would ask how those weights are set. The paper assumes that $20 weighs more than $1, which is reasonable, but the theory gives no way to measure the weight of "the experimenter needed help" or "I told her it was fun".

**Explaining your own act.** Every paid student had to answer, at least silently, a question: why did I just tell her that? Harold Kelley's attribution theory offers a rule for questions like this. His <Term>discounting principle</Term> says that when one obvious cause for a behaviour is present, people give less weight to other possible causes. For a $20 student, the money is an obvious cause. It explains his statement, so he does not need to conclude that he liked the tasks. For a $1 student, the money explains almost nothing, so another cause is needed, and "maybe the tasks were not that bad" is available. The [behavioural lens](/cases/cognitive-dissonance/lenses/behavioural/) describes Daryl Bem's version of this idea, self-perception theory. The cognitive lens frames the same reasoning as information processing: the student searches for the most plausible explanation of his own behaviour.

**Information planted in the situation.** People build explanations from the information available to them, and three pieces were planted. The "For Group B" sheet gave the words to use. The young woman's remark, that a friend had found the experiment boring, sharpened the clash, because the student had to contradict her directly. And on the way to the interview, the experimenter told every student that most participants "found it quite interesting" (p. 206). In cognitive terms, this supplied a ready-made interpretation that only students who needed one would use.

**What the students remembered.** The study had no measure of opinion before the lie, and it never asked students whether they thought they had changed their minds. A cognitive psychologist would want to know. Later research suggests that people who change an attitude after acting against it often misremember what they used to think, so that the new attitude feels as if it was always theirs (Bem & McConnell, 1970). If this happened in 1959, the $1 students would not have experienced themselves as changing at all. They would simply have "always" found the tasks fairly interesting.

**Specific, not general.** The question about how much students learned about their own ability showed no differences between groups (p. 208). The cognitive lens values this. It shows that the change was tied to the particular belief that clashed with what the student had said, not a general good mood. The mind adjusted one piece of knowledge, the one that did not fit.

## What this lens values in the study

It values the study's place in history: it showed that how people think about their reasons can matter more than the size of a reward. It values the precise prediction derived from a mental process, the control question that showed the effect was specific, and the blind ratings of the tape recordings that ruled out a rival cognitive explanation, rehearsal.

## What it would question

- The thoughts the theory depends on were never measured. The students were "first encouraged to talk" in the interview (p. 206), but the paper reports none of what they said.
- There was no measure of opinion before the lie, and no measure of what students remembered of their earlier view.
- The theory gives no independent way to weigh cognitions, so it is hard to know in advance how much dissonance a situation creates.
- A rating given in an interview may show a change in what students were willing to say, not a change in how they automatically felt about the tasks.

## Evidence from this tradition

**Attribution and misattribution.** Zanna and Cooper (1974) showed that the way people explain their own feelings decides whether their attitudes change. Students who wrote an essay against their views, and could blame their unease on a pill they had been told would make them tense, did not change their attitudes. This fits the cognitive idea that the person's interpretation of a feeling, not just the feeling, drives the change.

**Memory for earlier attitudes.** Bem and McConnell (1970) found that students who had written essays against their own opinions later recalled their original opinions as closer to their new ones than they really were. Attitude change can come with a rewriting of the past.

**Dissonance without memory of the act.** Lieberman, Ochsner, Gilbert and Schacter (2001) tested patients with amnesia, who could not remember making a choice between two art prints. The patients still came to like the chosen print more afterwards, as people without amnesia do. This is evidence that dissonance reduction does not always need conscious recall of the behaviour. It challenges any account in which people consciously reason their way to a new attitude.

**Words versus automatic evaluations.** Gawronski and Strack (2004) found that writing a counter-attitudinal essay changed people's stated attitudes but not their automatic evaluations, measured by reaction times. On this view, dissonance works on the beliefs people can state and defend, not on every level of evaluation. Applied to 1959, the $1 students may have changed what they would say about the tasks more than how they felt about them.

## A study this lens would design

1. Two weeks before the experiment, measure each student's liking for repetitive tasks, both with a rating scale and with a reaction-time measure of automatic evaluation.
2. Run the boring tasks and the $1, $20 and control conditions as in 1959.
3. Straight after the lie, ask each student to write down every reason he can think of for agreeing to help, a method called thought-listing.
4. In the interview, take both the rating and the automatic measure again, and ask the student to recall the rating he gave two weeks earlier.
5. Repeat the measures a week later.

**Prediction:** $1 students will list fewer outside reasons for agreeing, will change their stated rating more, will misremember their earlier rating as closer to the new one, and will show less change on the automatic measure than on the stated one.
