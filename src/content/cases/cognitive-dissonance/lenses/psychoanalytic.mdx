## The lens in brief

Psychoanalysis was founded by Sigmund Freud in Vienna around 1900. It holds that much of mental life is <Term>unconscious</Term>, meaning outside our awareness, and that inner conflicts shape what we feel, believe and do. The mind protects itself from anxiety, guilt and shame through <Term>defence mechanisms</Term>: unconscious ways of keeping painful thoughts and feelings out of awareness. Anna Freud described these defences systematically in *The Ego and the Mechanisms of Defence* (1936). Psychoanalysts learn mainly from long clinical work with individuals.

One defence matters most here. <Term>Rationalisation</Term> means giving yourself a reasonable-sounding explanation for something you did, while the real motive stays hidden, even from you. The term was introduced by the psychoanalyst Ernest Jones in 1908. Jones wrote that no one will admit to having acted irrationally, so the mind supplies a false explanation with "a plausible ring of rationality". He separated private self-deceptions, which he called evasions, from rationalisations formed "mainly for public consumption", with an eye on the opinions of the people who matter to us (Jones, 1908, p. 166). That last idea links this lens to the [social lens](/cases/cognitive-dissonance/lenses/social/), where the same question appears as impression management.

In method, this lens is far from the study. In content, it is surprisingly close. Dissonance theory describes people who change their beliefs to protect themselves from an uncomfortable truth about their own behaviour, without realising it. That is very near to what psychoanalysts call defence. Elliot Aronson's 1992 paper on the revival of dissonance research was even titled "The return of the repressed", a phrase borrowed from Freud.

## How this lens reads the study

**The new opinion as rationalisation.** The $1 students ended up rating the tasks as somewhat enjoyable (+1.35, against -0.45 for the Control group). A psychoanalyst would read this as a classic rationalisation. The uncomfortable truth is "I misled a fellow student, and I did it for almost nothing." The acceptable story is "The tasks were actually quite interesting, so I did not really lie." The student is not consciously deceiving the interviewer. He comes to believe the story himself. Festinger would call this dissonance reduction. A psychoanalyst would call it defence, and would add that the motive behind it, avoiding guilt and shame, is unconscious.

**Guilt about the lie.** The young woman did not simply listen. She said that a friend had told her the experiment was boring and that "she ought to try to get out of it" (p. 206). So the student's reply, "Oh, no, it's really very interesting", actively talked her out of her friend's warning. In psychoanalytic terms, this is likely to stir the <Term>superego</Term>, the part of the mind that holds a person's moral standards and produces guilt when they are broken. On this reading, $20 works as a kind of payment that settles the moral account: "I was well paid, it was a job." One dollar settles nothing. The guilt remains, and the ego must find another way to quiet it.

**The experimenter as an authority figure.** The request came from a man who had just spent an hour watching the student with a stopwatch, who appeared embarrassed and unsure, and who mentioned that "Professor ------" had suggested asking him (p. 205). Psychoanalysts use the word <Term>transference</Term> for the way people bring feelings from early relationships, especially with parents, into relationships with people in authority. A student who wants to please or rescue an embarrassed authority figure may agree before he has thought. Afterwards, the agreement has to be explained to himself, and "I wanted to help" or "it was interesting anyway" is easier to accept than "I could not say no."

**The responses that were thrown away.** Among the 11 students excluded from the analysis, two told the woman they had been hired to say the experiment was fun, and one, immediately after talking to her, "demanded her phone number saying he would call her and explain things" (p. 207). A psychoanalyst would find these students the most revealing in the study. They did not defend against guilt by changing their opinion. They tried to repair the harm directly. The set-up also had an emotional charge the paper ignores: young men, alone for two minutes with a young woman described as "a girl", asked to deceive her. Wishes to impress, to protect or to win her approval may have shaped what each student did, and the student who wanted her number may have been moved by more than guilt.

**Defence happens out of awareness.** At the end of the interview, students were asked whether they were suspicious of anything (p. 206). Only five said they suspected the real purpose (p. 207). The theory, like psychoanalysis, implies that the others did not notice their change of opinion as a change. A defence that is noticed stops working.

## What this lens values in the study

It values the study as one of the first laboratory demonstrations that people deceive themselves to protect their self-image, something psychoanalysts had claimed for decades from clinical cases. The strongest change came where the outside excuse was weakest, which fits the idea that defences grow stronger when anxiety has no other outlet. It also values the paper's honesty in reporting the students who broke the script.

## What it would question

- A deep conflict, involving honesty, guilt, authority and possibly desire, is reduced to a single number on a rating scale.
- The paper reports nothing the students said in the open part of the interview, which is exactly where a psychoanalyst would look for signs of defence.
- Individual history is ignored. Why did some students refuse or confess while others complied smoothly?
- The theory describes the process as an accounting of cognitions, which leaves out emotion and unconscious motive.

## Evidence from this tradition

**Defences in modern experiments.** Roy Baumeister, Karen Dale and Kristin Sommer (1998) reviewed social psychology research for evidence of the defence mechanisms Freud and his followers described. They found reasonable support for some, such as denial and reaction formation, and little for others, such as sublimation. Their review shows that some psychoanalytic ideas can be tested in experiments, and that results are mixed.

**Defences as measurable and adaptive.** Phebe Cramer (2000) reviewed research showing that defence mechanisms can be measured, for example by coding the stories people tell about pictures, and that the defences people use change with age and stress. She argued that defences are normal ways of adapting, not only signs of illness.

**Motivated reasoning in the brain.** Drew Westen, a psychologist trained in psychodynamic theory, and colleagues (2006) scanned committed supporters of the two main US presidential candidates in 2004 while they read statements showing their own candidate contradicting himself. The supporters found ways to dismiss the contradictions, and brain regions associated with emotion, rather than cool reasoning, were most active while they did so. Westen interpreted this as emotionally driven defence.

**Evidence against the lens.** Dissonance experiments produce their effects without any reference to childhood, sexuality or unconscious wishes, and they make precise predictions about the direction and size of change. Psychoanalytic explanations are harder to test and can often explain any result after the fact. Dissonance effects have also been reported in young children and even in monkeys (see the [biological lens](/cases/cognitive-dissonance/lenses/biological/)), which suggests that something simpler than a complex system of defences may be at work, although those findings are themselves disputed.

## A study this lens would design

1. Recruit a small number of volunteers and assess each person's typical defensive style beforehand, for example by coding stories they tell about ambiguous pictures.
2. Run the boring tasks and the request, with the $1 payment only, so that every participant faces a weak excuse.
3. After the rating, hold a long, open interview in which the participant talks freely about the tasks, the request, the young woman and the experimenter.
4. Analyse the interviews for signs of guilt, defence and attempts at repair.
5. Interview participants again a month later.

**Prediction:** participants with a strong tendency to rationalise will change their rating most and show least guilt in the interview. Participants who show more guilt will be more likely to confess to the woman, refuse the money or try to contact her, the very responses the 1959 study excluded.

Dissonance theory explains the blaming well. The cognitions "I am a loyal friend" and "I did nothing while he was mocked" clash. Deciding that the friend deserved it reduces the clash, just as the $1 students reduced theirs by deciding the tasks were interesting.

The psychoanalytic lens adds the emotional depth and the hidden motive. It reads the blaming as rationalisation protecting him from guilt and shame, and his sudden anger at his roommate as a sign that the defence is being threatened. It would also ask why he froze in the first place, perhaps fear of powerful seniors, and whether this fits a longer personal pattern. Dissonance theory describes the adjustment of beliefs; psychoanalysis asks what painful feeling the adjustment keeps out of awareness.
