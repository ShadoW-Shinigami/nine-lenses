This zone is extra. The school's outline does not ask for it, and nothing here is needed to complete Sections A to G. It exists because every paradigm in psychology rests on answers to questions that philosophers argued about first, and because seeing those arguments makes the paradigms easier to tell apart.

## Why philosophy, in a paradigms course

Section B asked what the study assumes about human nature, knowledge, truth, change and behaviour. Those are philosophical questions, and for this study they are unusually close to the surface. Festinger's theory says that people need their thoughts to fit together, that they are rationalising rather than rational, and that a belief can be changed by an act. Philosophers have written about consistency, self-deception, lying and the ownership of acts for two and a half thousand years. When you can see where the theory's assumptions came from, you can also see what the alternatives were.

The pages in this zone do not claim that any philosopher anticipated the $1 and $20 experiment, or that the experiment proves or disproves a philosophy. They ask what each tradition would say about the study's core idea, using the tradition's own texts.

## The concept under examination

Cognitive dissonance is the uncomfortable state of holding two cognitions that do not fit, and the push to reduce the discomfort, often by changing a belief to fit an act. In this study, students paid $1 to tell a waiting woman that boring tasks were fun later rated the tasks as more enjoyable than students paid $20. The theory raises questions that psychology alone cannot settle:

- Is consistency a need built into the mind, or a norm that people may honour or betray?
- What is self-deception, and who is doing the deceiving when the deceiver and the deceived are one person?
- Is a belief that was changed to end discomfort true, or only comfortable?
- When is an act one's own? Does a payment, or a polite request from authority, make it less so?
- Does the size of a reward excuse a lie, or grade it?
- What is the drive that the study never measured, and does it need to be a drive at all?
- Was the $1 student's new opinion knowledge, a justified belief, or only a comfortable one? Did he know that it had changed, and why?

## Five traditions, and why these five

The zone reads the study through five traditions that span more than two thousand years and disagree about almost everything, which is the point.

- **Aristotle** wrote the first careful account of when an act is voluntary, of truthfulness as a virtue, and of the person whose soul is "torn by faction".
- **Kant** made truthfulness an unconditional duty, described the "inner lie" by which people deceive themselves, and pictured conscience as a court inside the person.
- **Ayn Rand** built an ethics around the refusal to fake reality, and named evasion and rationalisation as chosen failures rather than mental mechanisms. She stands outside academic philosophy, and the page says so.
- **Pragmatism** is the tradition that grew up alongside American psychology. Peirce described the irritation of doubt in 1877, and James described how new beliefs are grafted onto old ones "with a minimum of disturbance" in 1907.
- **Existentialism** gave the twentieth century its sharpest account of lying to oneself, Sartre's bad faith, and its sternest refusal of excuses.

Each tradition page has two halves. The first is about ethics and the self: lying, choice, conscience and self-deception. The second is about knowledge and belief, or <Term>epistemology</Term>: what each tradition thinks knowledge is, how a belief ought to be formed, and what that means for the $1 student's new opinion. Two further pages widen the zone. **Knowledge and belief** sets out eight modern debates that cut across the traditions, from coherence and truth to whether scientists reduce dissonance too. **Other voices** reads the study through Socrates, Epictetus, Hume, Spinoza, the Bhagavad Gita, Nyaya, Buddhist texts and Jain thought.

## Five readings side by side

Use this table to compare, not to replace the pages. Each row is one tradition.

| Tradition | What dissonance reduction is | What it values in the study | What it challenges |
|---|---|---|---|
| Aristotle | The wrong repair of a soul at odds with itself: changing the belief instead of the conduct, which is the first step towards the settled liar who feels nothing | Attention to motive; the finding that people do not stay comfortably divided; every student returning the money | "Forced compliance" when nothing was forced; treating the money as a justification; discarding the students who refused |
| Kant | An inner lie: taking "a wish for the deed" to keep one's picture of oneself; the defence counsel talking round the court of conscience | The small inducement doing the most inner work; honest reporting; the full debriefing | The lie treated as neutral material; the experimenters' own deception; the unmeasured drive, which Kant would call conscience |
| Ayn Rand | Evasion and rationalisation, chosen, not suffered; integrity run backwards, with conviction made to fit action | A counter-intuitive prediction tested; the finding that money is not a simple reinforcer; the control question | The drive as a cause rather than a name for the choice to stop looking; Bem's observer stance; the refusers set aside as noise |
| Pragmatism | For Peirce, the method of tenacity, which settles belief by comfort and fails when other people disagree; for James, the cheapest repair to a stock of opinions | A real test; the tape-recorded check; the demonstration that doing changes believing | Calling the new belief "true"; the contrast between the lie as behaviour and the rating as inner state; the removal of the one dissenting voice |
| Existentialism | Bad faith: a lie to oneself with no liar and no deceived; a flight from freedom by either route, the $20 excuse or the $1 revision | Proof that belief follows action, and that a good enough excuse leaves the old self untouched | The drive as a hidden censor; the size of the payment as the cause; no measure of whether the student owned his act |

## Five theories of knowledge side by side

The table above compares what the traditions say about the act and the self. This one compares what they say about knowledge, and so about the status of the $1 student's new opinion.

| Tradition | What knowledge is | Their verdict on the new opinion | What their epistemology says about the study's method |
|---|---|---|---|
| Aristotle | Grasp of truth; practical truth needs correct desire; opinion is not up to us | A false opinion, not knowledge; pain bent a belief about value, as Aristotle says it can | Planted "received opinions"; claims more precision than the subject allows; measures words, not deeds |
| Kant | Holding true on grounds valid for every rational being | A persuasion mistaken for a conviction, sincerely reported | Supports it: the control group is Kant's test of whether a judgement holds for others' reason |
| Ayn Rand | Grasping reality by looking outward; consistency checked against all one knows | Belief made to fit the self rather than reality; the cheap kind of consistency | Asked what students felt, never why; removed the minds that kept the whole context |
| Pragmatism | What careful inquiry would finally agree on; no inner signal of truth | A claim about a past experience that can be false and has no future to confirm it | No dissent allowed to correct the student; one rating instead of later action; the reflective students excluded |
| Existentialism | Disputed: Sartre and Camus demand clear sight; Nietzsche doubts truth's value | Bad faith, a lowered bar of evidence; for Nietzsche, perhaps harmless | The interview helps make the opinion it claims to read |

## How the traditions connect

The traditions are not five separate verdicts. Noticing where they agree and disagree will make your journal stronger.

- **All five accept that the phenomenon is real.** None denies that the $1 students changed their minds. Their quarrel is about what to call it and whether it had to happen.
- **Aristotle, Kant and Rand call it a failure; the pragmatists call it a mechanism; the existentialists call it a choice.** For Aristotle the student has taken a step towards vice; for Kant he has told himself an inner lie; for Rand he has evaded. Peirce and James describe the same event as the way beliefs normally settle, and are the least moralising of the five. Sartre describes it as a project the person adopts, and holds him responsible without calling it a mechanism.
- **Five names for the unmeasured drive.** Aristotle: a soul torn by faction. Kant: the prosecutor in the court of conscience. Rand: a signal that a premise has been touched. Peirce: the irritation of doubt. Sartre: nothing but the person's own flight. The study's weakest point, that dissonance was inferred and never observed, is where the five differ most.
- **On Bem's self-perception theory, the lines cross.** Peirce, Mead and Sartre agree with Bem that there is no attitude hidden behind the act waiting to be read off. Aristotle and Kant say the inference "I said it, so I believe it" is invalid for an act done at another's request for pay. Rand says Bem's observer has made himself a second-hander to himself.
- **Everyone wants the refusers.** All five traditions treat the students who refused to lie, or who told the woman the truth, as the most important people in the experiment, and all five regret that they appear only as discarded data.
- **On knowledge, four of the five agree on one thing: felt consistency is not rational consistency.** Kant calls consistency the hardest rule of thinking, reached only after thinking for oneself and from others' standpoint. Rand's follower Peikoff says that seeking consistency only among the ideas in front of you is not logic. Peirce and James describe a feeling of fit that can come, in James's words, "from any cause whatever", and Dewey asks for judgement suspended until evidence decides. Sartre says good thinking escapes bad faith by moving towards evidence, not towards firmer belief. On this shared view, dissonance theory describes the felt kind of consistency, and the $1 students show what happens when it is mistaken for the rational kind.
- **On certainty, Rand and the pragmatists are opposites.** Rand and Peikoff hold that certainty is possible and that a person's own perception settles simple questions. James holds that we have "no infallible signal" of truth, and Peirce that no single mind is the judge. Both would doubt the $1 student's rating, for different reasons: Rand because he looked away from what he knew, the pragmatists because nobody was allowed to disagree with him.
- **Kant's epistemology is the friendliest to the study.** Its test of a belief, whether it holds for other people's reason, is what the control group supplies, and its remedy for illusion, explaining a judgement by its causes in the mind, is what the paper does. Aristotle, the pragmatists and the existentialists are more critical of the method.
- **Only the pragmatists and the existentialists make the audience central.** For James and Mead, the lie to the woman and the rating to the interviewer are two social selves before two audiences. For Beauvoir, the request arrived wrapped in ready-made authority. The other three focus on the student alone with his conscience.

## Using philosophy responsibly

- **Quote texts, with locators.** The pages give the translation and the standard reference, such as Bekker numbers for Aristotle and Akademie pages for Kant, so that a reader can check. Do the same in your journal.
- **Beware famous misquotations.** "Contradictions do not exist. Check your premises" is a character in *Atlas Shrugged*, not an essay by Rand. "Hell is other people" is from Sartre's play *No Exit*, not from the works on these pages. Each page lists the lines it did not find in the texts.
- **Separate the philosopher's words from the page's reading.** Each page quotes first and interprets second. "Kant would say" is a reading, and should be presented as one.
- **Do not claim anticipation or proof.** Peirce's doubt is a parallel to dissonance, not a forecast of it, and no experiment settles a philosophical dispute.
- **Paraphrase encyclopedias, quote texts.** The page on knowledge and belief summarises the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy in its own words. If you quote an encyclopedia, check the live entry first.
- **Where it helps your journal.** The zone feeds Section B, where you name the study's assumptions, and Section G, where you say what changed in your own thinking. It also sharpens the behavioural and humanistic lenses in Section C, which already carry the Bem debate and the language of self-deception.

Nietzsche would agree most readily: "'I have done that,' says my memory. 'I cannot have done that,' says my pride, and remains inexorable. Eventually, memory yields" (*Beyond Good and Evil*, section 68). He treated self-justification as the normal working of the mind, and he thought even philosophers find reasons after the fact.

The warning is that the other traditions accept the phenomenon without accepting the assumption. Kant would say that people rationalise, and that the inner lie is common, but that reason remains the judge in the court of conscience, which is why the rationalisation is a failure rather than a nature. Rand would say that rationalisation is a choice and that a focused mind does not do it. Peirce and James would say that beliefs settle by the cheapest repair, which is neither rational nor irrational but how belief works, and that other people and later experience are the correction. Aristotle would say that a divided soul is a stage of character, curable while the person still feels regret. So the classmate can fairly write that the study assumes a rationalising human being, and should add that the philosophers who accept the observation disagree about whether it describes human nature or a human failing, which is exactly the kind of disagreement a paradigm analysis should expose.
