## The lens in brief

Psychoanalysis was founded by Sigmund Freud in Vienna around 1900. It holds that much of mental life is <Term>unconscious</Term>, meaning outside our awareness, and that inner conflicts shape what we feel, believe and remember. The mind protects itself from anxiety, guilt and shame through <Term>defence mechanisms</Term>, unconscious ways of keeping painful thoughts and feelings out of awareness. The best known is <Term>repression</Term>: pushing a painful memory or wish out of conscious awareness, where it may still affect behaviour.

Psychoanalysis has always said that memory is not a recording. In his paper "Screen memories" (1899), Freud described vivid, ordinary-seeming childhood memories that stand in for something else, something more important that has been pushed aside. He argued that such memories are shaped, and partly formed, at a later time. A related idea, <Term>Nachträglichkeit</Term>, usually translated "deferred action" or "afterwardsness", holds that an early experience can take on its meaning, even its traumatic force, only later, when a person's understanding changes.

So in content this lens is closer to the study than its methods suggest. Both say that memory is rebuilt after the event. But they disagree about why, and about what follows. In the 1990s, the research this study started became the main scientific challenge to the idea of repressed memory, which the psychoanalytic and trauma traditions defend. This dispute of the 1990s is often called the <Term>memory wars</Term>.

## How this lens reads the study

**Later events reshape memory: an old idea, a new engine.** The 1974 study showed that a word heard after an event changed what students later reported. A psychoanalyst would not be surprised. Freud's screen memories and deferred action both describe memories reshaped by what comes later. The difference is the engine of change. For Loftus and Palmer, it is information: a verb that supplies "external information" (p. 588). For psychoanalysis, it is meaning and emotion: what the event comes to signify for the person.

**The verb carries emotion, not only speed.** *Smashed* is a violent word. The paper says the verb may make a student "see" the accident "as being more severe than it actually was" (pp. 586-587). A psychoanalyst would notice that severity is an emotional quality. The word may have changed how frightening or destructive the crash felt in memory, and broken glass, sharp and dangerous, is an emotionally loaded detail. The study recorded only a number and a tick, so it cannot tell.

**These memories mattered to nobody.** The students watched short films of strangers' accidents, with nothing at stake. Nothing in the procedure touched their own lives, wishes or fears. For psychoanalysis, the memories that matter most are exactly the ones that do: memories of family, loss, shame or danger. The trauma researchers Bessel van der Kolk and Fisler (1995) began their own study by doubting whether "laboratory studies of ordinary events are relevant to the understanding of traumatic memories". On this view, the study tells us a great deal about memory for neutral details, and little about memory for personal trauma.

**The free accounts were the closest thing to free association.** Each student first wrote "an account of the accident" in their own words (p. 586), before any specific question. In psychoanalysis, a person's own unprompted words, in their own order, are the main source of evidence. The paper does not report these accounts. A psychoanalyst would see this as discarding the richest data in the study.

**The study's later life put it at the centre of a conflict.** Loftus went on to argue that some "recovered" memories of childhood abuse could be created by suggestion, especially in therapy. She wrote: "Repression is one of the foundation stones on which the structure of psychoanalysis rests" (Loftus, 1993, abstract), and asked how authentic recovered memories are. So a five-page study of broken glass became, twenty years later, part of an argument about the foundations of psychoanalysis.

## What this lens values in the study

It confirms, under controlled conditions, something psychoanalysis has long claimed: that memory is not a fixed record and that the present shapes the past. It also shows that a person can be confident about a detail and still be wrong, and that they may not know how the detail got there. A psychoanalyst would agree that people do not have full access to the sources of their own memories.

## What it would question

- Whether memory for a stranger's filmed crash can tell us anything about memory for events that matter emotionally to a person.
- The absence of any measure of feeling: how upsetting the films were, or how the verb changed the emotional tone of the memory.
- The decision not to report the students' own accounts.
- The assumption that a memory's value lies only in whether its details are accurate, rather than in what it means to the person.
- Moving from "a word can add broken glass to a memory of a film" to claims about whether memories of childhood abuse are real.

## Evidence from this tradition

**Narrative truth.** The psychoanalyst Donald Spence, in *Narrative Truth and Historical Truth* (1982), argued that what therapy produces is a coherent, meaningful story about a person's past, not a verified record of it. This view accepts reconstruction, as the 1974 study does, but treats the meaning of the story as what matters therapeutically.

**Betrayal trauma.** Jennifer Freyd (1994) proposed <Term>betrayal trauma theory</Term>. The theory is about <Term>psychogenic amnesia</Term>, memory loss with a psychological rather than a physical cause. It "suggests that psychogenic amnesia is an adaptive response to childhood abuse": forgetting abuse by a caregiver "enables the child to maintain an attachment with a figure vital to survival". This offers a reason why abuse memories, unlike memories of a filmed crash, might be forgotten and later remembered. Freyd's own family was at the centre of the memory wars: the False Memory Syndrome Foundation was founded in 1992 by her parents after she accused her father of abuse.

**Traumatic memory may work differently.** Van der Kolk and Fisler (1995) studied 46 people with post-traumatic stress disorder. Their traumatic memories first returned "as dissociated mental imprints of sensory and affective elements", fragments of sensation and feeling, with a personal story forming only over time. If traumatic memory is organised differently from ordinary memory, findings about ordinary memory may not transfer.

**The gap between clinicians and researchers.** Lawrence Patihis and colleagues, including Loftus (2014), surveyed beliefs about repressed memory. They found that 82% of hypnotherapists and 69% of psychoanalysts agreed that traumatic memories are often repressed, far more than research psychologists. They described "a substantial gap between the memory beliefs of clinical-psychology researchers and those of practitioners". A later review by Otgaar and colleagues (2019), also including Loftus, combined surveys and found some belief in repressed memory in 58% of 4,745 respondents. It argued that "dissociative amnesia", a diagnosis in the current psychiatric manual, is in effect a new name for repression.

**A sceptical middle ground.** In Britain, a working group of the Royal College of Psychiatrists looked at memories that are "recovered" after long periods of amnesia (Brandon, Boakes, Glaser and Green, 1998). It concluded that, particularly when "extraordinary means were used to secure the recovery of memory, there is a high probability that the memories are false". It also warned that the issue "should not be allowed to confuse the recognition and treatment of sexually abused children". And a review of false memory studies cut against strong claims on both sides. Some recollection of the suggested false event appeared in 47% of participants on average, "but only in 15% are these experiences likely to be rated as full memories" (Brewin and Andrews, 2017).

<Note title="An Indian court uses the language of defence">
The Supreme Court of India, in *Bharwada Bhoginbhai Hirjibhai v. State of Gujarat* (1983), described how an honest witness may "fill up details from imagination on the spur of the moment". It suggested that "the sub-conscious mind of the witness sometimes so operates on account of the fear of looking foolish or being disbelieved", and called this "a sort of a psychological defence mechanism". The Court used this reasoning to set aside minor inconsistencies and uphold a conviction. The [India page](/cases/car-crash/india/) looks at this judgment more closely.
</Note>

**Evidence against the lens.** The 1974 study did not test repression at all: it used neutral films, not personal memories. But the wider evidence is a problem for strong psychoanalytic claims. Laboratory findings on misinformation have been repeated many times, while evidence for repression as a specific mechanism remains disputed (Patihis and colleagues, 2014; Brewin and Andrews, 2017). Critics of Loftus, in turn, argue that laboratory findings about trivial details have been used to cast doubt on real survivors, a concern the [ethics page](/cases/car-crash/ethics/) discusses.

## A study this lens would design

1. Recruit adults who were involved in a real road accident in the past year, through a hospital or a road safety charity, with full consent and access to support.
2. Ask each person to tell the story of the accident freely, in their own words, three times over six months, without leading questions.
3. Record how the story changes: which details come and go, how blame and danger are described, and what feelings come with it.
4. In a final interview, explore what the accident has come to mean in the person's life, and whether that meaning has changed the memory.
5. Do not plant any false information. Implanting false memories of personal trauma would be unethical.

**Aim:** not to count errors but to understand how meaning and memory change together, and whether emotionally important memories change in different ways from the neutral details in the 1974 study.

**Prediction:** details that carry blame or danger will change more over six months than neutral details, such as the colour of a vehicle, and the changes will follow what the accident has come to mean to the person.

The psychoanalytic lens would use the idea of deferred action, or Nachträglichkeit: an early experience takes on new meaning, and even new content, when a later event changes the person's understanding. His father's death has reopened the accident and brought grief, and perhaps guilt, into it. The new detail, his father looking at him, may express a feeling of responsibility more than a fact about the road. A psychoanalyst would explore what this memory does for Arup emotionally, rather than only asking whether it is accurate.

The misinformation research explains changes caused by information arriving from outside, such as a leading question with the word *smashed*. In Arup's case nobody has given him new information. The change comes from inside, from loss and meaning. The 1974 study also used films of strangers' accidents with nothing at stake, so it says little about a memory bound up with a parent's death. The case shows why each lens explains a different kind of memory change, and why neither can simply decide whether Arup's new memory is true.
