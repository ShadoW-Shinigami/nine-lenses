The outline asks you to choose two of these roles. This guide covers all seven, so you can see how different the redesigns become when the person's concerns change. Each page has the same order: what this person cares about, what worries them about the original, their redesign, why, and what the redesign gives up.

The original study used adult university students, not children, so some roles look different from a study like the Bobo doll experiment. The parent here is the parent of an undergraduate. The teacher is the person who runs the course that requires students to take part in experiments. That was the role of the 1959 course instructor, whose announcement in class about a department study became part of the cover story.

## The seven redesigns side by side

| Stakeholder | Their main question | Biggest change to the study | What it costs |
|---|---|---|---|
| Participant | Was I treated honestly, and was I free to say no? | Warning in advance that some details will be hidden, consent to the recording at the end, an easy way to refuse, a debriefing about what the lie says about them | Some students will guess the purpose, so the effect may shrink |
| Parent | Is my son or daughter safe, respected and free to refuse a teacher's request? | A real alternative to taking part, no secret recording, care with gender and privacy, contact details for complaints | Fewer volunteers and a less "natural" setting |
| Teacher | Can my students still trust what I tell them in class? | No false announcements by the teacher, an honest participant pool with alternatives, the study used as a teaching tool afterwards | The disguised interview loses its cover |
| Clinician | Can this effect help people change, without manipulating them? | A study of dissonance used openly, with people's own goals, measuring discomfort and wellbeing over weeks | No longer a test of the 1959 prediction about money |
| Researcher | Is the effect real, and is dissonance the reason? | A blind experimenter, checks on choice and discomfort, groups paid without lying, a large preregistered sample across many sites | Cost, time and still a laboratory |
| Policymaker | Can this be used in public campaigns, and is the research ethical? | Field trials of commitment and hypocrisy messages, for example with smokers, under ethics review | Slow, messy evidence and smaller effects |
| Community leader | Will this help my community act together, or will it be used to trick people? | A campaign designed with residents, using open public pledges and measuring real behaviour | Results may not travel to other communities |

## Choosing your two

The best pairs for the journal are two roles that **disagree**. In this study the sharpest disagreements are about deception and about pressure.

- **Researcher and participant** disagree about deception. The researcher thinks the disguise is what made the result possible. The participant thinks it is what made the study disrespectful.
- **Researcher and teacher** disagree about the classroom. The researcher wants the cover story planted in class, as in 1959. The teacher refuses to say false things to students.
- **Clinician and community leader** both want to use dissonance to help people change. They disagree about where encouragement ends and manipulation begins.

Showing how each would redesign the study, and where their redesigns conflict, demonstrates the perspective-taking the outline describes as "especially powerful".

One compromise is for the department to run a real feedback survey on all the experiments in its participant pool, with honest consent, and for the replication to use that genuine survey as its opinion measure. The teacher then announces something true. Another option is authorised deception: at the start of term, students are told honestly that "some studies hide part of their purpose, and everything will be explained at the end", and they agree to take part on those terms. India's ICMR guidelines (2017) describe this approach as authorized deception.

The cost is that the disguise becomes weaker. A real survey on all experiments may ask questions that do not fit the study exactly, and students warned about hidden purposes may look for one and guess it. Suspicious students were already removed in 1959, and more of them would mean a smaller usable sample or a smaller effect. The teacher keeps the students' trust; the researcher accepts a less perfect cover.
</Try>
