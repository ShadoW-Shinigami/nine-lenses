## The participants

The paper reports two experiments with different students. Experiment 1 had 45 students and Experiment 2 had 150 (pp. 586-587). That makes 195 in all.

The paper calls them only "students". It does not say which college they attended, how they were recruited, whether they were paid or given course credit, or their ages, sexes, ethnic backgrounds or nationalities. Both authors worked at the University of Washington in Seattle, so the students were probably undergraduates there, but the paper does not say so.

Each experiment was run "in groups of various sizes" (pp. 586-587). Students sat together, watched the films on a screen, and filled in paper questionnaires. They were not tested one at a time.

## How the groups were formed

In an experiment, each set of people treated in one way is a <Term>condition</Term>. When each person takes part in only one condition, the design is called <Term>independent groups</Term>. Both experiments used this design. A student in Experiment 1 read only one of the five verbs, for example, and never knew that other students had read different ones.

- **Experiment 1:** five conditions, one for each verb. "Nine subjects were asked, 'About how fast were the cars going when they hit each other?' Equal numbers of the remaining subjects were interrogated with the verbs *smashed*, *collided*, *bumped*, and *contacted* in place of *hit*" (p. 586). So there were 9 students per verb.
- **Experiment 2:** three conditions of 50 students: *smashed*, *hit*, and a group not asked about speed at all (p. 587).

The paper does not say how students were placed in conditions. It does not use the word "random". Because students were tested in groups, it is also possible that everyone in one testing group received the same verb, or that questionnaires with different verbs were handed out within each group. The paper does not say which.

## The materials

**The films.** The films in Experiment 1 were short "segments from longer driver's education films borrowed from the Evergreen Safety Council and the Seattle Police Department" (p. 586). Driver's education films are made to teach new drivers about road safety. Each segment showed a traffic accident and lasted between 5 and 30 seconds. Four of the seven were <Term>staged crashes</Term>, accidents set up deliberately for the film. Their original purpose, the paper says, "was to illustrate what can happen to human beings when cars collide at various speeds" (p. 586). One of these crashes took place at 20 miles per hour, one at 30 and two at 40. The paper does not describe what the other three films showed.

**The film in Experiment 2.** One film "depicting a multiple car accident", meaning several cars. The whole film lasted less than a minute, and the accident in it lasted 4 seconds (p. 587). There was no broken glass in it.

The paper does not describe the film further. Two other studies from the same laboratory and grant describe a film of the same length and timing (Loftus and Zanni, 1975, p. 87; Loftus, 1975, p. 563). In that film, a car turns into the main stream of traffic, the oncoming cars stop suddenly, and "a five car bumper to bumper collision" results. It was probably the same film. If so, it showed a low-speed chain collision, which fits the low speed estimates on the [findings page](/cases/car-crash/findings/). This identification is an inference, not a fact stated in the 1974 paper.

**The questionnaires.** All the questions were on paper. Each questionnaire began with an open question and then moved to specific ones. The paper calls the speed question the <Term>critical question</Term>, meaning the one whose answers were analysed. The other questions are not reported.

## The procedure

### Experiment 1: seven films, one changed word

**Watching the films.** Students watched together in groups of various sizes. The paper does not describe the room or the researchers; this is an illustration based on the paper's description, not a photograph of the study.
</Figure>

The students watched the seven films one after another. "A different ordering of the films was presented to each group of subjects" (p. 586). Changing the order means that no single film always came first, when students were fresh, or last, when they were tired. This is a form of <Term>counterbalancing</Term>: arranging the order of materials so that order effects do not favour any one film. The paper does not say how the orders were chosen.

<Figure src="images/cases/car-crash/questions.webp" alt="The same seminar room with the lights up and the screen blank. In the foreground a young woman student holds a pencil above an open questionnaire booklet and looks up, thinking. Behind her other students write." tag="reconstruction" wide>
**After each film, the questionnaire.** The booklet first asked for a free account of the accident, then specific questions. The booklet in the illustration is only a sketch: the real questionnaire asked first for a written account and then for a speed, as the text below explains.
</Figure>

After each film the students received a questionnaire. It asked them first to "give an account of the accident you have just seen", and then to answer "a series of specific questions about the accident" (p. 586). One of these was the speed question:

> About how fast were the cars going when they **hit** each other?

For the other groups, *hit* was replaced by *smashed*, *collided*, *bumped* or *contacted*. The paper's abstract gives the *smashed* version as "smashed into each other" (p. 585). Everything else about the question, the films and the session was the same. The whole session lasted "about an hour and a half" (p. 586).

### Experiment 2: one film, a week's wait

The second experiment had two sessions, a week apart.

**Day 1.** After the film, the students received a questionnaire "asking them first to describe the accident in their own words, and then to answer a series of questions about the accident" (p. 587). For 50 students the speed question used "smashed into each other". For 50 it used "hit each other". The other 50 were "not interrogated about vehicular speed": they answered the questionnaire without any speed question.

<Figure src="images/cases/car-crash/week-later.webp" alt="The same seminar room in bright daylight, with the screen rolled up and no projector. Students tick boxes on a single sheet of short questions. In the foreground a young man pauses with his pencil raised, looking into the distance as if trying to remember." tag="reconstruction" wide>
**One week later.** No film is shown. Each student answers ten short questions, ticking "yes" or "no" for the broken glass question. The illustration is based on the paper's description.
</Figure>

**One week later.** The students returned and, "without viewing the film again", answered a series of questions about the accident (p. 587). The critical question was:

> Did you see any broken glass?

Students answered by ticking "yes" or "no". The question was "embedded in a list totalling 10 questions, and it appeared in a random position in the list" (p. 587). Putting it among other questions, in a random position, made it less likely to stand out as the point of the study. The paper does not report the other nine questions.

<SourceCheck title="Source check: how long the second film lasted">
Gross says the students in Experiment 2 "watched a film of a car accident (lasting just 4 seconds)". The paper says the film "lasted less than 1 min; the accident in the film lasted 4 sec" (p. 587). The 4 seconds is the accident, not the film. The difference matters when you judge the study: the students saw some lead-up to the accident, but the crash itself was over in moments.
</SourceCheck>

<SourceCheck title="Source check: five groups">
Gross says the 45 students in Experiment 1 were "divided into five groups". The paper says they "participated in groups of various sizes" (p. 586) and that 9 students received each verb. There were five verb conditions of 9 students each, but the paper does not say the testing groups matched them. Write "five conditions of nine students", not "five groups", unless you mean the conditions.
</SourceCheck>

## What was measured

| Measure | When | How it was recorded | Reported in the paper? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Free account of the accident | Straight after each film | Written in the student's own words | No. The paper does not analyse it |
| Speed estimate | Straight after each film, Experiment 1; straight after the film, Experiment 2 | A number written by the student, in miles per hour | Yes, as averages for each verb |
| Answers to the other specific questions | Straight after each film | Not described | No |
| "Did you see any broken glass?" | One week later, Experiment 2 | A tick for "yes" or "no" | Yes, as counts for each condition |

Nothing in these measures needed an observer's judgement. The students wrote numbers and ticked boxes, and the researchers counted them. So there was no question of a scorer being biased, as there can be when observers rate behaviour. The paper does not say whether the people handing out the questionnaires knew the hypotheses.

<Note title="What the paper leaves out">
The paper is five pages long. It does not report the students' free accounts or the wording of the other questions. It does not give the speed of the cars in Experiment 2's film, or say how long the Day 1 questionnaire took. It does not say what the students were told about the purpose of the study, or whether they were told the truth afterwards. It also gives no standard deviations, the measure of how much students' answers varied within each condition. Later pages return to these gaps.
</Note>

<Try
  scenario="A student planning a replication suggests a simpler Experiment 2: 'Ask everyone the speed question with smashed straight after the film. A week later, ask everyone about broken glass. If lots of people say yes, that proves the verb changed their memory.'"
  question="What is wrong with this design, and which feature of the real Experiment 2 fixes it?"
>
With only one condition, there is nothing to compare the result with. Some people might say "yes" to broken glass anyway, because broken glass is part of most people's schema for a car crash, or because they are guessing. A high number of "yes" answers could not be credited to the word *smashed*.

The real Experiment 2 had two comparison conditions. The *hit* group was asked the same speed question with a gentler verb, and the control group was not asked about speed at all. The control group shows how often students "remember" broken glass with no suggestion. The *hit* group shows whether simply being asked about speed makes a difference. Only a higher rate of "yes" in the *smashed* group than in both of these can be credited to the verb.
</Try>
