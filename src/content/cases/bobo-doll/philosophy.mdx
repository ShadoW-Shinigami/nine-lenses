This zone is extra. The school's outline does not ask for it, and nothing here is needed to complete Sections A to G. It exists because every paradigm in psychology rests on answers to questions that philosophers argued about first, and because seeing those arguments makes the paradigms easier to tell apart.

## Why philosophy, in a paradigms course

Section B asked what the study assumes about human nature, knowledge, truth, change and behaviour. Those are philosophical questions. Behaviourism's answers came from a tradition that reaches back through Watson and Pavlov to the empiricist philosophers; the cognitive turn borrowed from Kant's claim that the mind brings structure to experience; the humanistic school took its vocabulary from existentialism. When you can see where an assumption came from, you can also see what the alternatives were, and that is what a paradigm analysis needs.

The pages in this zone do not claim that any philosopher anticipated the Bobo doll study, or that the study proves or disproves a philosophy. They ask what each tradition would say about the study's core idea, using the tradition's own texts.

## The concept under examination

The study's central claim is that children acquire new patterns of behaviour by watching another person perform them, with no reward to the watcher or to the model. The paper calls this imitation, and the theory that grew from it calls it observational learning. The claim raises questions that psychology alone cannot settle:

- Is a person formed by what surrounds them, or do they form themselves out of what surrounds them?
- Is copying the same as learning? What has a child who copies an adult's act actually acquired?
- What does an example do to the one who watches it: plant a habit, offer a model, show a possibility, or set a rule for the room?
- What is anger? Does it build up inside and need release, or is it made by what other people do in response to it?
- What may adults do to children in the name of knowledge?

## Five traditions, and why these five

The zone reads the study through five traditions that span more than two thousand years and disagree about almost everything, which is the point.

- **Aristotle** gave the oldest and most influential account of character as something built by habit, and wrote directly about imitation in children and about what the young should be allowed to see and hear.
- **Kant** gave the Enlightenment's account of persons as free, rational agents who must never be used merely as means, and he wrote more about example, habit and the education of children than most readers expect.
- **Ayn Rand** is a twentieth-century defender of volition who attacked behaviourism in print and wrote at length about nursery-age children. She stands outside academic philosophy, and the page says so.
- **Pragmatism** is the tradition that grew up alongside American psychology. James, Dewey and Mead argued about habit, plasticity and imitation half a century before Bandura.
- **Existentialism** is the sharpest modern challenge to any account of people as products of their environment, and Simone de Beauvoir wrote the clearest philosophical description of a small child's world.

## Five readings side by side

Use this table to compare, not to replace the pages. Each row is one tradition.

| Tradition | What learning by watching is | What it values in the study | What it challenges |
|---|---|---|---|
| Aristotle | The child's first and natural way of learning, pleasant in itself; the start of habituation, which over years makes character | Learning without reward, as Aristotle expected; evidence that what children watch shapes what they do | Calling the children "aggressive" after one afternoon; the adult as a "neutral figure"; provoking the child's spirit before the test |
| Kant | A fact about undeveloped minds, which imitate before they can judge; not moral learning, which needs the child's own maxim | Proof that example works without reward, and the care of the design | Counted behaviour as "learning"; habit as the road to character; upsetting children on purpose; the standpoint of nature mistaken for the whole picture |
| Ayn Rand | The lowest rung of cognition, a "parrot's psycho-epistemology" that children must grow out of by learning to think | A blow against Skinner: copying with no reward needs memory and a mind | The behaviourist vocabulary; the cause placed outside the child; no concept for the child who declines |
| Pragmatism | For James, a blind impulse that ideas issue in movement; for Dewey and Mead, a word that names a result and explains nothing, since the child is taking up an activity or an attitude | A real test by "manipulating real things"; the children's recorded remarks; the practical message about early example | A count of acts as evidence of a habit; the experimenter's silence treated as neutral; conduct studied with its social answers removed |
| Existentialism | A child in a "serious world" doing what the adult gods do; the beginning of becoming "a copy, a number" | A clean demonstration of how cheaply a person becomes a copy, and of children as small judges of what men and ladies do | The language of causes and "transmission"; the one-way mirror as a view from nowhere; no place for refusal or meaning |

## How the traditions connect

The traditions are not five separate verdicts. Noticing where they agree and disagree will make your journal stronger.

- **All five accept the central finding.** Children copy adults without reward. None of them is surprised, and none treats the data as false. Their quarrel is with the words the paper uses for the data.
- **Aristotle and Dewey against Kant and Rand on habit.** For Aristotle, habituation is the material of character; for Dewey, habits "constitute the self" (*Human Nature and Conduct*, p. 25). For Kant, a habit "is not one that proceeds from freedom"; for Rand, imitation is counterfeit cognition. This is a real disagreement about whether what the children did was the start of something good or the start of something to be outgrown.
- **Beauvoir and Mead against James on what the child is doing.** James allows a blind impulse to copy. Beauvoir's child does what the adult gods do; Mead's child takes the attitude of Al. The children's remarks, "I want to sock like Al", "That's not the way for a lady to behave", are the evidence that Beauvoir and Mead would claim.
- **Kant and Aristotle agree that the adult was a teacher.** Both say an adult performing violence in a nursery has given a lesson, whatever the paper calls it, and both raise the ethics of the design from that fact.
- **Rand and Sartre agree that the question is choice.** Both grant the environment great influence and refuse the step to determination. Both would want to know about the two children who did not copy.
- **Everyone wants the remarks as data.** Each tradition, for its own reasons, treats the children's spoken judgements as the most interesting thing the paper reports and regrets that they were anecdotes rather than measures.

## Using philosophy responsibly

- **Quote texts, with locators.** The pages give the translation and the standard reference, such as Bekker numbers for Aristotle and Akademie pages for Kant, so that a reader can check. Do the same in your journal.
- **Beware famous misquotations.** "We are what we repeatedly do" is Will Durant's summary, not Aristotle. "Out of the crooked timber of humanity" is Isaiah Berlin's rendering of Kant. "Check your premises" is spoken by a character in *Atlas Shrugged*. Each page lists the lines it did not find in the texts.
- **Separate the philosopher's words from the page's reading.** Each page quotes first and interprets second. "Kant would say" is a reading, and should be presented as one.
- **Do not claim anticipation or proof.** No philosopher anticipated an experiment run in 1961, and no experiment settles a philosophical dispute.
- **Where it helps your journal.** The zone feeds Section B, where you name the study's assumptions, and Section G, where you say what changed in your own thinking. It can also sharpen the humanistic and critical lenses in Section C, which draw on existentialism and on critiques of positivism, the view that only what can be observed and measured counts as knowledge.

  question="What is right and what is wrong with this sentence, and how would you rewrite it?"
>
The first half is fair: the paradigm page shows that the study treats children as shaped mainly by what surrounds them. The second half has two problems. The quotation is not Aristotle's; it is Will Durant's 1926 summary of him, and no translation of the *Nicomachean Ethics* contains it. And Aristotle's genuine view is more complicated than "shaped by the environment". He did say that "by doing just things we become just" (NE II.1, 1103a31-b2), but he also said that children act voluntarily "but not in choice" (NE III.2, 1111b8-9), that character shows in choice rather than acts, and that "one swallow does not make a spring". He would not read twenty minutes of copying as evidence of a formed character.

A better sentence names the assumption, cites a real passage with its locator, and shows the disagreement: "The study treats children as shaped mainly by their environment. Aristotle would accept that habituation 'straight from childhood' makes 'the whole difference' (NE II.1, 1103b23-25), while denying that one afternoon shows a character, and Kant would object that imitation 'has no place at all in matters of morality' (Groundwork, 4:409)." That shows the reader that the assumption has a history and that philosophers disagree about it, which is what a paradigm analysis is for.
