The outline asks you to interpret the study through at least three other perspectives. This guide covers all nine, so you can see the whole range before choosing. Each lens page follows the same order: the lens in brief, how it reads the study, what it values, what it questions, evidence from that tradition, and a study it would design instead.

If the perspectives are new to you, read the short course-wide introduction on [the nine lenses](/lenses/) page first.

## The nine readings side by side

Use this table to compare, not to replace the lens pages. Each row is one lens.

| Lens | What it sees in the study | Its sharpest question | The study it would run instead |
|---|---|---|---|
| Behavioural | Reinforcement hiding in the children's past, in the bouncing doll and in the room's lack of punishment | Was this really learning without reinforcement? | A single-case design that varies consequences for model and child over several weeks |
| Cognitive | Children who noticed, stored and recalled the model's acts, and judged them against gender schemas | Did children who did not copy still learn? | Separate tests of what children remember and what they choose to do |
| Psychoanalytic | Frustrated children displacing anger onto a safe target, or identifying with a powerful adult | What did the hitting mean to each child? | Repeated play sessions with familiar and unfamiliar models, plus parent interviews |
| Biological | An evolved capacity to imitate, and boys near the age peak of physical aggression | Why did some children, and more boys, copy more? | The same procedure with temperament, heart-rate and twin measures |
| Humanistic | Children's own experience and choices, missing from the counts | What was it like for the child, and was the child respected? | Child-friendly interviews and drawings, with the child's assent and no deliberate frustration |
| Social | An adult setting a norm for the room, and an experimenter whose silence looked like approval | Would this happen with real peers and real relationships? | A nursery-class study with models of different status and measures of aggression towards peers |
| Cultural | A universal learning process filled with American content: models, toys and gender roles | Who counts as a model in other cultures? | A multi-community study designed after ethnographic fieldwork |
| Critical | Violence turned into a property of individual children, and gender norms taken as given | Whose interests does this framing serve? | Participatory action research with a community on what makes children unsafe |
| Decolonial | A local Western result exported as universal knowledge, with other traditions ignored | Whose knowledge counts, and who decided? | A study built with an Indian community, in its language and with its concepts |

## How the lenses connect

The lenses are not nine separate verdicts. Some agree with each other and some disagree. Noticing which is which will make your journal stronger.

- **Behavioural and cognitive disagree about the same data.** Both accept that the children copied. The behavioural lens says consequences control what children do. The cognitive lens says children learn more than they show. Bandura's 1965 study gives evidence that both are partly right.
- **Psychoanalytic and humanistic agree that meaning matters.** Both say counting acts misses what the act meant to the child, though they explain meaning differently: unconscious conflict for one, conscious experience and choice for the other.
- **Social, cultural, critical and decolonial form a widening circle.** The social lens widens the view to the room and its norms. The cultural lens widens it to the child's community. The critical lens widens it to society and power. The decolonial lens widens it to world history and whose knowledge counts.
- **Biological and critical pull hardest in opposite directions.** The biological lens looks for causes inside the body. The critical lens warns against locating social problems inside individuals at all. A careful answer can hold both: bodies differ, and societies shape what those differences mean.

<Try
  scenario="Your journal must analyse the study through three other lenses. A friend chooses behavioural, cognitive and social, because 'they are the easiest'."
  question="What is the weakness of this choice, and what would a stronger set of three look like?"
>
These three lenses are all close to the study's own perspective. Social learning theory grew out of behaviourism, turned towards cognition and placed social influence at its centre. Using these three mostly restates the study's own logic, with small disagreements. The analysis will show less range.

A stronger set includes at least one lens that challenges the study's assumptions from outside. For example: cognitive, to show what the study implies about the mind; psychoanalytic or humanistic, to raise the question of meaning; and critical or decolonial, to question the study's universality and framing. This shows the reader that you understand how different paradigms disagree about what counts as knowledge.
</Try>
