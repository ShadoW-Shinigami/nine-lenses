## The tradition in brief

Existentialism is less a school than a family of writers who put one question first: what does it mean to exist as a being who has to choose? The French philosophers Jean-Paul Sartre (1905 to 1980) and Simone de Beauvoir (1908 to 1986) accepted the label. The Danish Christian thinker Søren Kierkegaard (1813 to 1855) and the German philosopher Friedrich Nietzsche (1844 to 1900) are counted as forerunners. Albert Camus (1913 to 1960) is often grouped with them but denied being one. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy notes that only Sartre and Beauvoir called themselves existentialists.

For this study the key idea is Sartre's <Term>bad faith</Term> (in French *mauvaise foi*): the ways a person hides their own freedom from themselves. Its background is his claim that "existence precedes essence": a person is not made to a design but becomes what they do, and so is "condemned to be free" (*Being and Nothingness*, 1943, p. 577). The other writers add their own accounts of the same move, the mind quietly rearranging what it knows so that the person can stay comfortable.

## What existentialists say about lying to yourself

Each idea below starts with the writer's own words, then explains them in plain English, then says why they matter for this study.

**Bad faith is not a lie.** "In bad faith it is from myself that I am concealing the truth. Thus the duality of deceiver and deceived is not present here. On the contrary, bad faith implies in its essence the unity of a single consciousness" (*Being and Nothingness*, p. 90). A cold, deliberate lie to oneself would collapse at once. Bad faith works instead as a kind of belief: "it believes, and it does not believe, that it is in bad faith" (p. 114).

In plain words: an ordinary lie has two people, one who knows the truth and one who is fooled. Self-deception seems to need the same two people inside one head, which looks impossible. Sartre's answer is that <Term>bad faith</Term> is not a cold lie but a kind of belief that keeps itself vague on purpose. The person in bad faith lets himself be half persuaded, decides not to demand strong evidence, and does not look too closely at what he is doing. He half knows what he is up to, which is why Sartre says bad faith "believes, and it does not believe" that it is in bad faith. Think of someone who "forgets" an appointment he did not want to keep.

Why it matters here: the $1 student knew what the hour had been like, so his new rating is not amnesia. Sartre would call it the half-belief of bad faith, a comfortable view adopted by not asking for evidence against it.

**There is no hidden censor doing the hiding.** Sartre's target here is Freud's censor: "how could the censor discern the drives to be repressed without being conscious of discerning them? Can we conceive of knowledge as being ignorant of itself?" (p. 94). So "the censor must be in bad faith".

In plain words: Freud explained self-deception by dividing the mind into parts, with a censor that keeps unwelcome thoughts out of consciousness. Sartre's objection is simple. To keep a thought out, the censor must know what the thought is; so the censor both knows and does not let itself know, which is the very puzzle it was supposed to solve, now moved inside a smaller agent. Sartre concludes that there is no mechanism that hides things from us behind our backs. Self-deception is something a person does, and the whole person does it.

Why it matters here: the same objection applies to a drive that is never measured. Whatever "reduced the dissonance" had to know which thoughts clashed and which change would help, which means it was the student himself, doing something.

**There are no excuses.** "What distinguishes human-reality is that it has no excuse" (p. 720). In the lecture he gave in 1945, published the next year, he put it bluntly: "any man who takes refuge behind the excuse of his passions, or by inventing some deterministic doctrine, is a self-deceiver" (Mairet's word for a person in bad faith). He went further: "I am responsible even for my desire to flee my responsibilities" (p. 721).

In plain words: Sartre's "no excuse" sounds harsh, and he meant it to. His claim is that pointing to a cause outside yourself, a passion, a payment, an order, a bad childhood, never fully explains what you did, because you still had to take that cause up and act on it. The reason you give afterwards is part of the act, not a motor hidden behind it. He went so far as to say that even the wish to escape responsibility is something you are responsible for. In Mairet's translation the person who hides behind such excuses is called a "self-deceiver"; the French word is the same as for bad faith.

Why it matters here: "I did it for twenty dollars" is an excuse in Sartre's sense, and so is "I did it because the experimenter asked". The theory treats the size of the payment as the cause; Sartre would say the cause is what each student made of the payment.

**Memory yields to pride.** Nietzsche compressed self-justification into three sentences. "'I have done that,' says my memory. 'I cannot have done that,' says my pride, and remains inexorable. Eventually – memory yields" (*Beyond Good and Evil*, section 68). Even philosophers, he wrote, defend conclusions that come from "a desire of the heart" with "reasons they have sought after the fact" (section 5). In the *Genealogy* he described a grander version, the "slave revolt in morality", in which the powerless re-describe their weakness as virtue through "the counterfeit and self-deception of impotence" (essay 1, section 13).

In plain words: Nietzsche's three sentences describe a quarrel inside a person. Memory says that the deed was done; pride says that it cannot have been; and pride wins, so the memory is rewritten. He thought this was how minds normally work, not an occasional failure, and he extended the point to philosophers, who, he said, reach their conclusions by wish and then hunt for reasons. The *Genealogy* tells the same story on a historical scale. People who could not fight back re-described their weakness as goodness and their enemies as evil. Nietzsche traces this to <Term>ressentiment</Term>, the resentment of the powerless, which "becomes creative" and makes a bad position bearable by renaming it.

Why it matters here: the $1 student's "it was interesting" is a small revaluation. A wasted hour and a cheap lie become a worthwhile experience, and Nietzsche would find the experiment a tidy confirmation of what he took for granted.

**Willing the good for a reward is double-mindedness.** "The person who wills the good for the sake of reward does not will one thing but is double-minded" ("Purity of Heart", Part One, section II). Such a person "stands at the crossroads; two prospects appear there: the good and the reward". The same holds for willing the good out of fear of punishment, or "only to a certain degree". And the comfortable person resists finding out: he "is usually far from wanting to be wrenched out of his error. On the contrary, he becomes indignant" (*The Sickness unto Death*, p. 43). When a person has worked to cloud their own knowledge, Kierkegaard argued, the fault is "not in the knowing but in the willing" (p. 88).

In plain words: Kierkegaard's test of a person is whether they want one thing or two. Someone who does the right thing for a reward wants the good and also wants the reward, and the two can come apart, so he stands at a crossroads with two wills. The same holds for someone who does right only to avoid punishment, or only up to the point where it starts to cost. Kierkegaard calls this split <Term>double-mindedness</Term>, and he says it is what despair is. He adds that the comfortable person does not want to be told any of this, and treats whoever tells him as an enemy. And when someone has worked to keep themselves from knowing something, the fault is in what they willed, not in what they failed to know. His frame is Christian: "the good" is willed before God.

Why it matters here: for Kierkegaard the $1 and $20 students differ only in degree. Both acted for a reward and so had two wills, and a student who later finds the experiment more "important" is giving himself a neater reason.

**Keep the tension alive.** Camus described the <Term>absurd</Term> as a confrontation between the human "appetite for the absolute" and a world that gives no answer. Religious and philosophical thinkers end the tension by a leap that drops one side. He called this "philosophical suicide" and asked for the opposite: "There can be no question of masking the evidence, of suppressing the absurd by denying one of the terms of its equation" (*The Myth of Sisyphus*, "Philosophical suicide"). His ethic in one line: "Living is keeping the absurd alive" ("Absurd freedom").

In plain words: Camus's absurd is not a word for something silly. It names the clash between two things, the human need for meaning and unity, and a universe that gives no answer. Religious and philosophical thinkers, he says, escape the clash by a leap that drops one side, usually by deciding that the universe does answer after all. He calls this "philosophical suicide", because it kills the question rather than facing it. His own demand is to keep both sides in view and go on living without resolving them. Staying clear-eyed about the contradiction is, for Camus, the only honest response.

Why it matters here: Camus's ethic is the direct opposite of dissonance reduction. The $1 student held two terms, "I said it was fun" and "it was boring", and ended the discomfort by dropping one. Camus would call that a small leap and ask for the harder thing.

## Reading the study through this tradition

**A lie to oneself with no liar and no deceived.** The $1 student told the waiting woman that the tasks were interesting, then rated them as enjoyable to an interviewer he believed came from the department. He had just spent an hour with the pegs and knew what he thought of them. Sartre would say his new rating is neither amnesia nor a second cynical lie. It is the half-belief of bad faith, in which a person stops demanding evidence against a comfortable view. Festinger and Sartre agree on one thing: the whole process happens inside one person and is that person's own work. They part on what to call it. Festinger describes a state that pushes. Sartre describes a project the person adopts, and he would say that the push is the person.

**The drive that was never measured, and the censor.** Dissonance was defined as an unpleasant state like hunger, but no discomfort was ever measured; it was inferred from the ratings (see [strengths and limitations](/cases/cognitive-dissonance/strengths-limitations/)). Sartre's objection to Freud's censor transfers directly. Whatever reduced the dissonance had to know which thoughts clashed and which change would help. That is to say, it had to be the student himself, doing something. A theory that explains the result through an inner force the person does not notice has, for Sartre, moved the puzzle rather than solved it. He and Festinger both reject a two-agent mind of the Freudian kind. They disagree about whether "drive" names anything beyond the person's own choosing.

**Excuses: the $20, the $1 and the ones who refused.** The $20 students rated the tasks almost exactly as the control group did. The $1 students rated them clearly higher. Three students refused to take the money, two told the woman the truth, and one wanted her number so that he could explain. For Sartre the $20 student's position, "I lied, but for twenty dollars", treats the money as a cause and is an excuse: the bad faith of the person who becomes a thing moved by a payment. The $1 student, left without such a cause, flees in the other direction by revising what he thinks, so that there is no longer anything to own. Both groups flee; only the route differs. The students who refused, or told the truth, kept the act as theirs, and the analysis set them aside. Beauvoir adds the frame. The request arrived wrapped in ready-made authority: the professor, the department, the course requirement. A student who complied because "the department" asked is her <Term>serious man</Term>, who "is no longer a man, but a father, a boss, a member" of an institution (*The Ethics of Ambiguity*, Part II). The fake "department survey" exploited exactly that trust.

**Dissonance reduction as an old habit.** Nietzsche would find the experiment a tidy confirmation of something he took for granted: the mind rewrites what it cannot bear, and reasons are collected afterwards. The $1 student's "it was interesting" is a small revaluation. A wasted hour and a cheap lie become a worthwhile experience, just as the lambs' weakness became virtue. The paper's picture of a rationalising rather than rational person is Nietzschean. But Nietzsche would reject the idea that consistency is a need. For him the force at work is pride, not harmony, and he would ask why the psychologists ever expected human beings to be consistent.

**Willing for reward, and not wanting to know.** For Kierkegaard the $1 and $20 conditions differ only in degree. Both students did a favour for payment and so had two wills. The later measures catch the double-mindedness at work: a student who now finds the experiment slightly more scientifically important is giving himself a better reason, which is Kierkegaard's reward-seeker making his motives look neater. The instructor's plea to be completely honest was addressed to someone who had every reason not to want to know. Kierkegaard's claim that the fault lies in the willing fits the paper's finding that opinion moved only on the questions the lie had touched.

**Keeping the tension alive, and the deed without a doer.** Camus's ethic is the direct opposite of dissonance reduction. The $1 student held two terms, "I said it was fun" and "it was boring", and ended the discomfort by dropping one. Camus would call that a small leap and ask for the harder thing: hold both and stay lucid. On Daryl Bem's later reinterpretation, that the student simply observed his own act and inferred his attitude, the existentialists split the difference. Sartre and Nietzsche agree with Bem that no attitude sits hidden behind the act waiting to be read: "'the doer' is merely a fiction added to the deed – the deed is everything" (*Genealogy*, essay 1, section 13), and "feeling is formed by the deeds that one does" (*Existentialism Is a Humanism*). But Bem's student looks at himself as an outsider would, and that is the stance Sartre calls the sincere man's bad faith. His "sincere man" is the person who declares once and for all what he is, "I am a liar", and so turns a free person into a fixed thing; that too is a flight, constituting oneself "as what he is in order not to be it" (pp. 110 to 111). For all of these writers the interesting question is not which mechanism moved the number, but whether the student was willing to own what he had done. The experiment had no measure for that.

## Where it agrees, and where it clashes

The agreements are real. The study shows that belief follows action, and "existence precedes essence" is a claim of just that shape. The study locates the change inside one person's own activity, not in a hidden second self. And the study found that a large payment produced less change, which for Sartre shows what an excuse does: once it is good enough, it lets a person keep their old self untouched.

The clashes are about vocabulary and about what the design leaves out. The paper speaks of a drive that pushes; the existentialists speak of a flight the person chooses. The paper treats the size of the payment as the cause; Sartre would say the cause is what each student made of the payment. The paper's measure of success is a number on a scale; the existentialists' is whether a person owns their act. And the students who refused, who are the clearest cases of owning an act, appear in the paper only as discarded data.

## Questions it would put to the researchers

- You never measured discomfort. What is the difference between saying a student "reduced dissonance" and saying he chose to stop looking at what he had done?
- Three students refused and two told the woman the truth. Why are they absent from the result, and what would it take to make them the finding?
- Is twenty dollars an explanation of a lie, or an excuse for it? Is one dollar?
- Your cover story relied on students trusting "the department" and a professor. How much of your effect is the authority you borrowed, and how much is the money?
- You told every student that "most of our subjects" found the tasks interesting. Is that a help to self-persuasion, or an invitation to become like the others?
- You deceived students in layers and then asked them, through another deception, to be completely honest. What did honesty mean in that room, and for whom?

## Cautions

- **The label is loose.** Kierkegaard's "good" is willed before God, and his despair is a theological idea. Sartre argued from atheism. Camus called Kierkegaard's leap "philosophical suicide". Name the writer you are using.
- **Camus's subject is the meaning of life**, not a lie about peg-turning. Using his ethic for the $1 student is an analogy.
- **Nietzsche's slave revolt is a story about European morality.** His contempt for the "weak" is not something to import. Use his observation about memory and pride, not his judgements.
- **Do not claim that Festinger proved an unconscious drive.** He inferred a drive; the existentialist point is that the inference is doing the work.
- **Translation.** Mairet's 1948 version of the lecture says "self-deception" where later translations say "bad faith"; the French is the same word. Sartre's famous line "Hell is other people" is from his play *No Exit* and does not appear in the works used here.
