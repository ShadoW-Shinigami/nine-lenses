## How this guide was checked

The textbook account supplied for this case was the chapter on Loftus and Palmer in Richard Gross's *Key Studies in Psychology* (chapter 6). Hock's *Forty Studies that Changed Psychology* does not cover this paper. Its Reading 16, "Thanks for the memories!", covers the follow-up study, Loftus (1975). Both books were used only to get oriented.

Every number, quotation and procedural detail in Sections A and E was then checked against the original 1974 paper, read in full from a scan of the journal pages, including its three tables. Page numbers in the guide are the journal's (585-589). Later studies were checked against their full texts where these could be read, and against abstracts or reliable summaries where they could not. Where a later source was read only as an abstract or a summary, the guide's wording is cautious, and some key items below say so. Where a claim could not be verified, the guide either leaves it out or says so.

Some numbers in the guide are this guide's own calculations from the paper's tables, not results the paper reports. Each is labelled where it appears: the risk ratio and odds ratio for broken glass, the two-by-two tests of *smashed* against *hit* and against the control group, Cohen's d for the Experiment 2 speed difference, and the likely number of students in each speed band of Table 3.

Several things are often misreported, so it is worth stating them plainly. The paper does not say how students were assigned to conditions, or that assignment was random. It reports no standard deviations and no tests between pairs of conditions. It gives no ages, sexes, backgrounds, college or method of recruitment for its 195 students. And it says nothing about consent, debriefing or ethical review.

The ethics page was checked against the current published texts of the codes it cites (APA 2017, BPS 2021, ICMR 2017), with section numbers, and against a dated record of the rules of 1973-74. Indian statutes were checked against the Gazette of India texts, and judgments against full reproductions.

## Corrections to Gross's account

If you use Gross, note these differences from the original paper. Where a page of this guide explains the point, it is linked.

- The senior author's initials are E. F. Loftus, not "E.E." (p. 585).
- The title is "Reconstruction of automobile destruction", not "Reconstruction of *an* automobile destruction".
- The 45 students in Experiment 1 were not "divided into five groups". They "participated in groups of various sizes", with 9 students per verb. ([Method](/cases/car-crash/method/))
- The paper's aim is presented as a "hypothesis", and an order for the five verbs is presented as an implied prediction. The paper predicts nothing about the five verbs; it discusses only *hit* and *smashed*. The order is the order of the results. ([Question and hypotheses](/cases/car-crash/question/))
- The mean speed estimates were not shown to be "significantly different from each other". The paper reports one overall test across the five verbs and no tests between pairs. ([Findings](/cases/car-crash/findings/))
- The Experiment 2 film did not last "just 4 seconds". The film lasted less than a minute; the accident in it lasted 4 seconds. ([Method](/cases/car-crash/method/))
- The chance of saying "yes" to broken glass was not shown to be "significantly greater" with *smashed* than with *hit*. The paper tested the three groups together; tested alone, this guide finds the *smashed* against *hit* difference borderline. ([Findings](/cases/car-crash/findings/))
- The "separate analysis" showing that *smashed* did more than raise speed estimates is a descriptive table (Table 3) with no significance test and unknown numbers in each speed band. ([Findings](/cases/car-crash/findings/))
- Gross says Experiment 2 "showed" that students remembered false details. The paper says its results are "consistent with" a reconstruction of memory (p. 585). ([Findings](/cases/car-crash/findings/))
- The new information came from the speed question with *smashed*, not from the broken glass question. The broken glass question was the test. ([Question and hypotheses](/cases/car-crash/question/))
- The question about speed could not have changed "what was originally encoded when seeing the film". It came after the film. The paper places the change in the integration of information supplied afterwards (p. 588).
- The 1974 paper does not say the original information is "erased" or "irretrievably lost". It says only that two kinds of information may be integrated until the witness cannot tell their sources apart (p. 588). The stronger claim comes from later work, such as Loftus and Loftus (1980), and even there it was hedged. ([Findings](/cases/car-crash/findings/))
- Gross's paragraph on the coexistence view says the original memory could not "even in principle" be recovered. The coexistence view holds the opposite: that the original may still be recoverable with suitable cues.
- In the barn study (Loftus, 1975), the control question did not mention a stop sign. It was "How fast was the white sports car going while traveling along the country road?" (p. 566). The film was "a brief videotape of an automobile accident", not "a car travelling through the countryside". The percentages Gross gives (17.3% against 2.7%) are correct. ([Why it matters](/cases/car-crash/importance/))
- In the Loftus and Zanni (1975) headlight study, "the" raised false "yes" answers only for items that were not in the film (15% against 7%), not "far more" in general.
- In Loftus (1979), the stolen item was a red wallet, only about half the participants read the misleading "brown" description, and a large preregistered replication (O'Donnell and Chan, 2025) failed to reproduce the protective effect of blatant misinformation. ([Strengths and limitations](/cases/car-crash/strengths-limitations/))
- The "lost in the mall" case of 14-year-old Chris was first published by Loftus (1993), not Garry et al. (1994). His brother Jim gave him one short false story at the start; the later details came from Chris himself. When told the truth, Chris expressed disbelief, not "dismay".

## Corrections to Hock's Reading 16

Hock's Reading 16 covers Loftus (1975), not this study. If you use it for the follow-up, note these points.

- Hock says the differences between the direct-question group and the false-presupposition group were significant "for each item, as well as for all the items combined". The paper reports item-by-item significance only for the control group against the false-presupposition group. The direct-question against false-presupposition difference is reported only for the five items combined (p. 569).
- Hock leaves out that the control group and the direct-question group did not differ significantly (p. 569).
- The demonstrators in Experiment 2 are "eight demonstrators" in the paper; "antiwar" is Hock's addition.
- Loftus and Ketcham's book is *The Myth of Repressed Memory*, not "Memories".
- Hock describes Loftus as at the University of Washington. She moved to the University of California, Irvine, in 2002.
- Hock says Loftus appears to have demonstrated that repressed memories "simply do not exist". Experiments can show that false memories can be created; they cannot show that a whole class of memories never exists.

## Other common errors

- Simply Psychology describes the Experiment 2 film as "a car driving through the countryside". That film belongs to Loftus (1975), not this study.
- Simply Psychology says one word "can permanently alter" memory. The study tested memory once, a week later, and made no claim about permanence. ([Why it matters](/cases/car-crash/importance/))
- Simply Psychology says participants "greatly overestimated" the speeds. Only the 20 mph crash was clearly overestimated; the point is that estimates did not follow the real speeds. ([Findings](/cases/car-crash/findings/))
- Simply Psychology says the Devlin Report (1976) was based on evidence "such as Loftus's". This guide found no evidence for that. The report was about identifying people, prompted by wrongful convictions, and was a House of Commons paper (HC 338), not "Cmnd 338".
- Some revision sites still recommend sequential line-ups as current best practice. The US National Research Council (2014) called the question "unresolved", and experts are now divided (Seale-Carlisle and colleagues, 2024).
- One revision site says three of the seven films "showed other traffic situations". All seven showed accidents; four of them were staged crashes of known speed.
- An Indian article, Tiwari (2011), describes the study as "Loftus and Loftus (1975)". The correct reference is Loftus and Palmer (1974). ([India](/cases/car-crash/india/))
