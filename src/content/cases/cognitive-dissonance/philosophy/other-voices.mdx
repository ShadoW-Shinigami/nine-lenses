## Why more voices

The five traditions on the earlier pages are all European or American. People thought hard about inner conflict, belief and self-deception long before Festinger, and in many places. This page reads the study through eight more traditions, more briefly than the main pages. Four are Western: Socrates and Plato, the Stoic Epictetus, David Hume and Baruch Spinoza. Four are Indian: the Bhagavad Gita, the Nyaya school of logic, early Buddhist texts and Jain thought.

Two warnings before you start. First, none of these thinkers anticipated the experiment, and none of their texts proves or disproves it. The page asks what each would make of it. Second, the Indian traditions are not "the Indian view". They disagree with each other as sharply as Hume disagrees with Kant, and none of them describes how Indian students actually behave. For evidence about that, see the [India page](/cases/cognitive-dissonance/india/).

Each section gives the thinker's own words with a standard reference, then explains them in plain English, then ties them to a detail of the study.

## Socrates and Plato: contradiction on purpose

Socrates (469 to 399 BCE) wrote nothing. We know him mainly through the dialogues of his student Plato, so "Socrates says" here means "Plato's Socrates says". His method, the <Term>elenchus</Term> (Greek for a testing or refutation), was to question a person until the person's own answers turned out to be inconsistent. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy (entry "Plato's Shorter Ethical Works") notes that an elenchus "usually concludes in the discomfiture of the partner". In other words, Socrates set out to produce a state that resembles what Festinger later called dissonance. The difference is in its purpose: for Socrates, the discomfort was meant to start a search for truth.

**Better that the world disagree with me than that I disagree with myself.** "I would rather that my lyre should be inharmonious ... or that the whole world should be at odds with me, and oppose me, rather than that I myself should be at odds with myself, and contradict myself" (*Gorgias* 482b-c). Being questioned by Socrates felt, said Meno, like the sting of "the flat torpedo fish, who torpifies those who come near him and touch him" (*Meno* 80a-b). A torpedo fish is an electric ray, a sea fish whose shock numbs anyone who touches it; "torpifies" means makes numb. Yet Socrates claimed that this numbness was progress. Of a slave boy he had questioned: "then he thought that he knew, and answered confidently as if he knew ... now he has a difficulty, and neither knows nor fancies that he knows", and so "he was the better for the torpedo's touch" (*Meno* 84a-c).

In plain words: Socrates ranks agreement with oneself above agreement with the crowd. That sounds like Festinger's need for consistency. The difference is in what the discomfort is for. For Socrates, the stuck, numb feeling of finding your own beliefs in conflict, which Greek calls <Term>aporia</Term> (an impasse), is the start of real inquiry. The slave boy was confidently wrong; now he knows that he does not know, and so he wants to find out.

Why it matters here: a lyre is a stringed instrument, and Socrates' image is of strings tuned to each other. On the paper's reading, the $1 student did end his inner discord, as Socrates would want, but he tuned the wrong string. He brought his opinion into line with his own words rather than with his own experience of the hour: his group's average was +1.35, against the control group's -0.45. The design also offered an easy way out before any Socratic impasse could form: the experimenter's remark, "Most of our subjects tell us afterward that they found it quite interesting" (p. 206). Every group heard it, and it moved only the group that had a reason to use it. Socrates would also notice that his method did not always work. After he exposed one politician, "the consequence was that he hated me" (*Apology* 21c-d). That politician reduced his dissonance by rejecting the questioner. The 1959 design gave the student no one to reject: the woman agreed with everything and the experimenter was friendly, so the belief was the easiest thing to move.

**The worst lie is the one in your own soul.** "The true lie, if such an expression may be allowed, is hated of gods and men ... no one is willingly deceived in that which is the truest and highest part of himself"; "this ignorance in the soul of him who is deceived may be called the true lie; for the lie in words is only a kind of imitation and shadowy image" (*Republic* II, 382a-c).

In plain words: Plato distinguishes two lies. A spoken lie is a false sentence told to someone else. The "true lie" is a false belief held in your own soul about the things that matter most. The spoken lie is only a copy of it. Plato says that nobody accepts the true lie willingly. Note that by "the highest part" he means beliefs about the most important realities, not every passing opinion.

Why it matters here: the $1 student told a lie in words to the waiting woman. On Festinger's account he then moved towards a lie in the soul, a false belief about his own experience. Plato says no one accepts that willingly, and Festinger's finding suggests people can come to hold it without noticing, which is a real challenge to Plato. Socrates' opponent Callicles makes a further point that bears on the study: "if a man is ashamed and dares not say what he thinks, he is forced to contradict himself" (*Gorgias* 482e-483a, Lamb's translation). Callicles means that shame makes people say the conventional thing and so contradict themselves. A later objection to the study runs in a related direction: the $1 students may only have told the interviewer what made them look consistent. Both say that what people state in front of an audience may not be what they think. The study's safeguard was a separate interviewer, blind to condition, presented as part of a department survey. Students can ask whether that removes the audience Callicles has in mind.

## Epictetus: the rational soul and the impression to be tested

Epictetus (born in the 50s CE, died about 135) was a slave at the Roman emperor Nero's court who was later freed and became a Stoic teacher. His student Arrian wrote down his teaching as the *Discourses* and the *Enchiridion*, or Manual. The Stoics held that our only real power lies in <Term>assent</Term>: accepting or refusing the impressions that come to us.

**Every error contains a contradiction, and the rational soul cannot bear one.** "Every error involves a contradiction. For since he who is in error does not wish to err, but to be right, it is clear that he is not doing what he wishes" (*Discourses* 2.26). "Now every rational soul is by nature offended by contradiction" (2.26). The chapter ends: "Point out to the rational governing faculty a contradiction and it will desist; but if you do not point it out, blame yourself rather than the man who will not be persuaded" (2.26).

In plain words: nobody wants to be wrong. So every mistake contains a hidden conflict between what the person believes and what he really wants, which is the truth. Show him the conflict and he will drop the mistake, because a reasoning mind cannot stand contradiction. This is an ancient parallel to dissonance theory's starting point. There is one deep difference. The Stoics held that a rational mind cannot make conflicting judgements at one and the same time, so, as the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy (entry "Stoicism") puts it, their account seems to rule out conflict between two judgements held at the same moment, which is the core of Festinger's theory.

Why it matters here: Epictetus agrees with Festinger that a contradiction pushes a mind to change. But he assumes the person drops the false side once he sees it. In the $1 group it was the true cognition, "the tasks were dull", that moved, and the control group's -0.45 suggests it was the true one. Epictetus explains why the push exists. Festinger shows that it does not always push towards truth.

**It is not things that disturb us, but our judgements; so test each impression.** "It is not the things themselves that disturb men, but their judgements about these things" (*Enchiridion* 5). He told his students to slow down before accepting how things look: "Wait for me a little, O impression; allow me to see who you are, and what you are an impression of; allow me to put you to the test" (*Discourses* 2.18). And: "What is the reason that we assent to anything? The fact that it appears to us to be so. It is impossible, therefore, to assent to the thing that appears not to be so" (*Discourses* 1.28).

In plain words: the trouble lies in how we judge events, not in the events. We cannot believe something that looks false to us, but we can stop and examine how things look before we agree. An <Term>impression</Term>, for the Stoics, is the way something first strikes the mind.

Why it matters here: the boring hour did not disturb anyone by itself. The control group sat through it and did not change. What disturbed the $1 student was a judgement: "I misled someone, and for only a dollar." Epictetus would say that this judgement was his to examine. Changing his rating of the tasks treated the task as the thing to fix, when the judgement was. The remark that "most of our subjects" found the tasks interesting is exactly the kind of impression Epictetus would tell a pupil to stop and test. The control group heard it too and stayed at -0.45, so it seems to have worked only where someone already wanted to accept it. And Epictetus, a former slave, had his own reasons to think about refusing a superior's request: money and reputation are not "under our control", but assent is (*Enchiridion* 1). The three students who refused the money and the job used the one power he says is always ours.

## David Hume: belief as a feeling, and the backgammon cure

David Hume (1711 to 1776), the Scottish philosopher, held that reason does not move us to act; feelings do. He thought belief is not chosen but is a feeling of force or liveliness that attaches to an idea. And he was honest about how he himself handled a contradiction he could not solve.

**Reason serves the passions, and belief is not up to the will.** "Reason is, and ought only to be the slave of the passions, and can never pretend to any other office than to serve and obey them" (*Treatise* 2.3.3.4). Belief "may be most accurately defin'd, A lively idea related to or associated with a present impression" (T 1.3.7.5). The difference between belief and fiction "lies in some sentiment or feeling ... which depends not on the will, nor can be commanded at pleasure" (*Enquiry* 5.11).

In plain words: reason cannot set our goals or fight our feelings. It works out how to get what the feelings want. A <Term>passion</Term>, in Hume's English, is any feeling or desire, not only a strong one. Believing something, for Hume, is a way an idea feels: livelier and steadier than a daydream. We cannot believe something just by deciding to. Note that Hume adds at once that passions "yield to our reason without any opposition" as soon as we see that they rest on a false supposition (T 2.3.3.7), so the slogan is not a licence to believe whatever we like.

Why it matters here: Hume fits Festinger's own picture. The $1 student did not decide to find the tasks enjoyable. On Hume's account, the idea "the tasks were rather interesting" became livelier for the $1 group (+1.35) and not for the $20 group (-0.05), although both had said the same words to the woman. Strictly, the -5 to +5 scale measured reported enjoyment. Hume would want a different measure as well: how firmly each student held his rating.

**When reasoning hurts, go and play backgammon.** At the end of Book 1 of the *Treatise*, Hume's own arguments had left him in sceptical despair. His cure: "I dine, I play a game of back-gammon, I converse, and am merry with my friends; and when after three or four hour's amusement, I wou'd return to these speculations, they appear so cold, and strain'd, and ridiculous" (T 1.4.7.9). Even ordinary beliefs survive partly from effort-saving: "If we believe, that fire warms, or water refreshes, 'tis only because it costs us too much pains to think otherwise" (T 1.4.7.11). Elsewhere he put it in five words: "Nature is always too strong for principle" (*Enquiry* 12.23).

In plain words: Hume did not answer his own sceptical arguments. He went to dinner, played a board game, talked with friends, and the arguments stopped feeling real. He openly endorsed this as wiser than torturing his brain. He also kept both sets of beliefs, the sceptical and the everyday, and moved between them by mood.

Why it matters here: this is a philosopher ending an intellectual discomfort by turning away from it, and saying so. The $1 student's setting offered something similar: a friendly experimenter, an agreeable woman, and a pleasant remark about what others had enjoyed, in which the dull hour came to seem not so bad. The difference is that Hume knew what he was doing. Hume also shows something the theory treats as unstable: a person living with a standing contradiction for years, managing it by where he puts his attention rather than by changing either belief. And "it costs us too much pains to think otherwise" is close to the idea that people take the cheapest way out.

## Spinoza: we call a thing good because we want it

Baruch Spinoza (1632 to 1677), the Dutch philosopher, held that every thing strives to keep itself in being. In human beings this striving, when we are aware of it, is desire.

**Judgement follows desire, and the mind strives to think well of itself.** "In no case do we strive for, wish for, long for, or desire anything, because we deem it to be good, but on the other hand we deem a thing to be good, because we strive for it, wish for it, long for it, or desire it" (*Ethics* E3p9s). "We endeavour to affirm, concerning ourselves, and concerning what we love, everything that we can conceive to affect pleasurably ourselves, or the loved object" (E3p25). "The mind endeavours to conceive only such things as assert its power of activity", and "When the mind contemplates its own weakness, it feels pain thereat" (E3p54 and E3p55).

In plain words: we do not first judge things good and then want them. We want them, and then call them good. We also try to believe pleasant things about ourselves, and thinking about our own weakness hurts. Spinoza's remedy for such passions is not to change the belief but to understand its causes.

Why it matters here: Spinoza's reversal describes the $1 student's rating: his judgement of the tasks followed what he needed to believe. "I misled someone for a dollar" is a thought of one's own weakness, which brings pain; "I was well paid", the $20 student's thought, is not. This comes close to Elliot Aronson's later revision of dissonance theory, which says that the discomfort is strongest when an act threatens a person's picture of himself as decent and sensible. Spinoza differs from Festinger in one deep way. He was a strict <Term>determinist</Term>, someone who holds that every event, including every choice, is fully caused. The 1959 design tried to make the lie feel chosen, and later studies found that the effect depends on people feeling that they chose freely. Spinoza would call that feeling an inadequate idea, one that sees the effect without its causes. His remedy, understanding the causes, is closest to the study's full debriefing, in which each student was told exactly what had been done to him and why.

## The Bhagavad Gita: action without attachment to its fruit

The Bhagavad Gita is part of the *Mahabharata*, formulated around the start of the Common Era. It is a dialogue on a battlefield in which Krishna persuades the warrior Arjuna, who does not want to fight his own relatives, to do his duty. Commentators such as Shankara, Ramanuja and, in the twentieth century, Gandhi read it in very different ways.

**Act, but not for the reward.** "Your business is with action alone; not by any means with fruit. Let not the fruit of action be your motive (to action). Let not your attachment be (fixed) on inaction" (Gita 2.47). In a modern rendering: "Your right is for action alone, never for the results" (Gambhirananda). The Gita asks the actor to be "equable in success or ill-success" (2.48), which Gambhirananda renders "Equanimity is called Yoga", and to look "alike on pleasure and pain, on gain and loss" (2.38). It also says that one "whose mind is deluded by egoism thinks himself the doer of the actions" (3.27).

In plain words: do what is yours to do, but do not do it for the reward, and do not escape into doing nothing. Later commentators summed this up as acting without desire for the fruit. The Gita's word for the steady mind it asks for is <Term>samatva</Term>, evenness: the same in gain and in loss. Its claim about "the doer" is that the sense "I, alone, did this" is a kind of ego.

Why it matters here: it is tempting to say that the $1 student, who acted for almost nothing, was closer to acting "not for the fruit". He was not. He acted for a fruit, only a small one, and then needed an inner reason to make up the difference. In the Gita's terms he was still attached: to the dollar, and to being the kind of person who would not lie for a dollar. The Gita also offers a real alternative to the theory's assumption that tension must be reduced by changing a belief. A person even-minded in "gain and loss" would not be shaken by the gap between $1 and $20, and so would have nothing to repair. And its teaching on "the doer" matches a point on the India page: the design tried to make the student feel that he chose the act, later research found that this feeling matters, and research by Krishna Savani, Hazel Markus and others suggests that people in India construe fewer acts as personal choices. That is a parallel in ideas, not evidence about any student.

**Do not unsettle the beliefs of others.** "A wise man should not shake the convictions of the ignorant who are attached to action" (Gita 3.26; Gambhirananda: "should not create disturbance in the beliefs of the ignorant").

In plain words: the Gita advises the wise to lead by example, not by unsettling the beliefs of people who are not ready to give them up.

Why it matters here: put this beside Socrates and beside the experimenters, and you get three positions on changing other people's minds. Socrates unsettled beliefs on purpose. The Gita advises against it. The experimenters first shaped beliefs with a cover story and a planted remark, and then unsettled them in the debriefing. That contrast suits a seminar question on the ethics of the study.

## Nyaya: where did the new belief come from?

Nyaya ("method of reasoning") is the classical Indian school of logic and theory of knowledge. Its root text, the *Nyaya-sutra*, is attributed to Gautama, around 200 CE. Nyaya holds that knowledge is a cognition produced by a reliable source, a <Term>pramana</Term> (means of valid knowledge), and it accepts four: perception, inference, comparison and testimony.

**Valid knowledge needs a reliable source, and testimony needs a reliable speaker.** "Perception, inference, comparison and word (verbal testimony) – these are the means of right knowledge" (*Nyaya-sutra* 1.1.3). "Word (verbal testimony) is the instructive assertion of a reliable person" (1.1.7). Vidyabhusana's note explains that a reliable person is one "who as an expert in a certain matter is willing to communicate his experiences of it".

In plain words: you know something only if your belief came from one of the proper sources. Hearsay counts as knowledge only when the speaker is reliable, meaning someone who knows the matter and is honestly reporting their experience.

Why it matters here: Nyaya would ask which source produced the $1 student's new rating. His first judgement, "this is dull", came from perception. The later +1.35 came from no new perception, no inference from new evidence and no reliable testimony. Nyaya would call such a cognition non-valid: in Vidyabhusana's English, misapprehension, not knowledge, whatever comfort it brought. The rule about testimony applies in two more ways. The paid student told the woman the opposite of his experience, so she could not gain knowledge from him even if she believed him. The two students who told her they had been hired restored her access to the truth, and the study set them aside. And the experimenter's remark about "most of our subjects" was testimony the students had no reason to doubt, yet it was planted.

**Doubt has causes, and it calls for examination.** "Doubt, which is a conflicting judgment about the precise character of an object, arises from the recognition of properties common to many objects, or of properties not common to any of the objects, from conflicting testimony, and from irregularity of perception and non-perception" (1.1.23). "Examination should be made of each case where there is room for doubt" (2.1.7). "Ascertainment is the removal of doubt, and the determination of a question, by hearing two opposite sides" (1.1.41).

In plain words: Nyaya defines doubt carefully, as being pulled between two judgements about a thing, and lists its causes. One is "conflicting testimony", two people telling you opposite things. The proper end of doubt is examination, ideally by hearing both sides. Note that Nyaya does not make doubt the starting point of all thinking. The Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy (entry "Nyaya") says the school treats trust as the default; doubt starts a particular investigation.

Why it matters here: the waiting woman offered conflicting testimony, her friend's report that the experiment was boring. That is one of Nyaya's listed causes of doubt. Nyaya's rule is that doubt calls for examination by hearing both sides. The design closed this down: the woman then accepted everything the student said, and the experimenter's remark supplied one side only. Dissonance theory describes how people end doubt by taking a side. Nyaya says how doubt should end, and the difference between those two is the difference between a description and a standard.

## Buddhist texts: clinging to views

The early Buddhist texts treat <Term>grasping</Term> (Pali *upadana*) as a root of suffering, and they list grasping at views among its kinds: "Grasping at sensual pleasures, views, precepts and observances, and theories of a self" (*Samyutta Nikaya* 12.2).

**Do not believe because of a teacher or because it fits what you think.** In the Kalama Sutta, villagers confused by rival teachers ask the Buddha whom to believe. He first accepts their state: "It is enough, Kālāmas, for you to be doubting and uncertain. Doubt has come up in you about an uncertain matter." Then he gives a list of bad reasons for belief, ending: "don't go by the appearance of competence, and don't think 'The ascetic is our respected teacher.'" His test is whether a teaching is "unskillful, blameworthy, criticized by sensible people", and whether acting on it leads "to harm and suffering" (*Anguttara Nikaya* 3.65). Another translator, Thanissaro Bhikkhu, renders one item on the list as "agreement through pondering views", which he glosses as what seems to fit in with what you already believe.

In plain words: doubt is natural when authorities disagree. Do not accept a claim because of tradition, clever reasoning, a teacher's standing, or because it agrees with what you already think. Test it by whether it causes harm, and by what wise people say.

Why it matters here: the Kalama Sutta's list of bad reasons matches the pressures in the study closely: the professor's request, passed on by the experimenter; the remark about what other students had said; and, on the paper's reading, the $1 student's drift towards what fitted his recent words. "What seems to fit in with what you already believe" is a warning against exactly the consistency-seeking Festinger describes. The sutta's test, harm and the judgement of sensible people, would also have caught the lie to the woman.

**Views defended for praise, and confession as another way out.** Of people who argue over doctrines: "when they're repudiated they get embarrassed; upset at criticism, they find fault in others" (*Sutta Nipata* 4.8), and "Each has built up their own view to be the truth, which is why they take the other as a fool" (4.12). In his advice to his son Rahula, the Buddha says that "when someone is not ashamed to tell a deliberate lie, there is no bad deed they would not do", that deeds "should be done only after repeated checking", and that after a harmful deed "you should confess, reveal, and clarify such a deed to the Teacher or a sensible spiritual companion" (*Majjhima Nikaya* 61).

In plain words: people defend views they have stated because being refuted is embarrassing, not because the views are true. The advice to Rahula adds a route that Festinger's list leaves out. Festinger names changing behaviour, changing a belief or adding new beliefs. The Buddhist text adds checking the act before, during and after, and confessing a harmful act to a wise companion.

Why it matters here: the $1 student had stated a position, briefly and to one listener, and then held to it. The two students who told the woman they had been hired, and the one who wanted her number so that he could explain, did something close to the confession the Buddha describes. The design treated them as unusable data. The Buddhist aim, unlike Festinger's, is not consistency at all. It is to stop holding views as possessions, and on this reading a self that needs its views to fit is part of the problem.

## Jain thought: many-sidedness

Jainism, whose teaching in its present form goes back to Mahavira (traditionally 599 to 527 BCE, in what is now Bihar), developed a doctrine called <Term>anekantavada</Term>, the many-sidedness of things. Reality has many aspects, so a claim is true from a standpoint and in a respect. The related method, <Term>syadvada</Term>, qualifies each claim with *syat*, "in some respect". The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy quotes the philosopher Samantabhadra: an entity "is somehow possessed of the character 'being', somehow possessed of the character 'non-being' ... all these four features characterizing it in accordance with the speaker's intention and not in an absolute fashion" (*Aptamimamsa* 14, as quoted in SEP "Jaina Philosophy", section 3.4).

In plain words: the same thing can truly be said to be and not to be, in different respects. No single statement is the whole truth about something complex. Rival schools go wrong, on the Jain view, by taking one true perspective and treating it as the only one. This is not relativism: the Stanford Encyclopedia stresses that the Jains were <Term>realists</Term>, who held that the many perspectives exist because the object really has many aspects.

Why it matters here: Festinger's student held two cognitions, "the tasks were dull" and "I said they were fun", and resolved them by changing one. A Jain approach to an apparent contradiction is to specify the respects in which each holds, rather than give one up. That models the other route the paper measured: a student could think "dull as a task, but of scientific value", which is what the question about the experiment's importance picked up. The single -5 to +5 scale, in Jain terms, forced an absolute judgement on a many-sided hour, and the $1 group's +1.35 might partly reflect a different weighting of real aspects rather than pure distortion. But there is a limit, and it matters most. Many-sidedness concerns real aspects of an object. "I told her it was fun" is a fact about a lie, not an aspect of the tasks. Many-sidedness cannot turn a lie into a perspective.

## How these voices connect with each other and with the main five

- **Epictetus against Hume.** One says a reasoning mind cannot hold a contradiction once it is shown one. The other shows a philosopher living with one for years by turning his attention away. Festinger sits between them: the pressure is real, as Epictetus says, but it can be relieved by looking away, as Hume shows.
- **Three positions on changing minds.** Socrates unsettles beliefs on purpose; the Gita advises the wise not to; the experimenters shaped beliefs and then unsettled them in the debriefing. This links directly to the [ethics page](/cases/cognitive-dissonance/ethics/).
- **Doubt from conflicting testimony** appears in both Nyaya and the Kalama Sutta, and both treat it as natural and as a reason to examine. The study shows how quickly it can be closed down.
- **Consistency is not the goal everywhere.** Socrates and Epictetus value consistency reached through truth. Hume, the Gita, the Buddhist texts and the Jains, in different ways, doubt that the aim is to make one's beliefs fit one's acts.
- **The truth-tellers again.** Nyaya's reliable witness and the Buddhist advice to confess both point to the students who told the woman the truth, as the five main traditions did on the [hub page](/cases/cognitive-dissonance/philosophy/).

## Questions these voices would put to the researchers

- You produced inner contradiction on purpose, as Socrates did. Did you give your students a chance to reach his impasse, or did you hand them an exit first? (Socrates)
- If the rational soul is offended by contradiction, why did the true belief give way rather than the false one? (Epictetus)
- What made the idea "the tasks were interesting" more lively for one group than another, and could you measure liveliness directly? (Hume)
- Which source of knowledge produced the $1 student's new rating: perception, inference, comparison or testimony? (Nyaya)
- Why did your list of ways to reduce dissonance leave out confession? (Buddhist texts)

## Cautions

- **These are readings, not anticipations.** No text on this page discusses Festinger, and Festinger did not draw on any of them.
- **Plato's Socrates is a character.** Write "in Plato's dialogues". The popular line "the only thing I know is that I know nothing" is not in the *Apology* in that form; the verified sentence is "he knows nothing, and thinks that he knows; I neither know nor think that I know" (*Apology* 21d). Plato's "lie in the soul" is ignorance about the highest realities, not self-deception in the modern sense.
- **Hume's slogan is about motivation to act.** "Reason is ... the slave of the passions" does not mean that we may believe whatever we like. "Nature is always too strong for principle" is from the *Enquiry*, not the *Treatise*.
- **The Gita is about duty in war**, not a self-help text about rewards. "Desireless action" (*nishkama karma*) is a commentators' summary, so say "the Gita's teaching of acting without attachment to the fruit". The text's teaching on duty includes its account of caste; do not present one without acknowledging the other.
- **Nyaya does not make doubt the start of all thinking.** It treats trust as the default and doubt as the start of a particular inquiry.
- **A fake Buddha quotation.** "Believe nothing, no matter where you read it, or who said it, no matter if I have said it, unless it agrees with your own reason and your own common sense" is not in the Kalama Sutta. Thanissaro Bhikkhu ("Lost in Quotation", 2012) points out that the real sutta warns against relying on one's own reasoning too.
- **Many-sidedness is not relativism.** The Jains were realists, and their doctrine does not make every view equally right. The parable of the blind men and the elephant is told in several Indian traditions; its oldest surviving version is Buddhist (*Udana* 6.4), and there it rebukes people who cling to their part of the truth.
- **A misattributed Gandhi line.** "Happiness is when what you think, what you say, and what you do are in harmony" has no traced source. Gandhi's *Autobiography* (Mahadev Desai's translation) does say that truth "is not only truthfulness in word, but truthfulness in thought also", and says that "to attain to perfect purity one has to become absolutely passion-free in thought, speech and action". Quote those instead.

The first remark is Socratic, with a Nyaya edge. Socrates would want the student to face the contradiction between "I find this dull" and "I say it is fascinating", and to stay with the discomfort long enough to ask which is true. Nyaya would ask where "secretly fascinating" came from: not from perception of the lectures, not from inference or reliable testimony, but from her wish to keep a promise, which makes it a non-valid cognition however comforting it is.

The second remark echoes the Gita. It does not tell her to change her belief about biology. It tells her to act without attachment to the fruit, which here includes her parents' approval and her picture of herself as a dutiful daughter. An even-minded person would not need to rewrite her experience, because the gap between duty and enjoyment would not shake her. The third remark is the Buddhist advice to Rahula: check the act, and confess a harmful one to a wise companion. Dissonance theory predicts that she will reduce the tension by changing what she says she feels. All three traditions offer other routes that keep her actual experience intact.
