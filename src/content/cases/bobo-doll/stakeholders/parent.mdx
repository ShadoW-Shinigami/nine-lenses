## What you care about

You care about your own child first. Will my child be upset? Will my child come home hitting things? You also have practical questions the study could help with. Should I stop my child watching fights on television? Does it matter if I lose my temper in front of them? What should I say when they see violence?

## What worries you about the original study

- **You were not told what would happen.** The paper reports no information given to parents and no consent procedure. You would not have known your child would be deliberately frustrated and shown an adult attacking a doll.
- **Your child may have learned something new and unwanted.** The whole point of the study is that children learn new aggressive acts by watching. Nothing in the paper says those acts were unlearned afterwards.
- **There was no follow-up.** No one checked whether children behaved differently at home or in the nursery in the following days.
- **The study does not answer your question.** It shows that children copy a stranger hitting a toy. It does not tell you what to do as a parent.

## Your redesign

1. **Informed consent with full information.** Tell parents exactly what their child will see and experience, including any mild frustration, before they agree.
2. **Let parents watch.** Parents can observe through the one-way mirror and stop the session at any time.
3. **Follow up.** Ask parents and nursery staff to report any new aggressive behaviour over the next two weeks, with a contact person if anything worries them.
4. **Test something useful for parents.** Add conditions in which an adult watches the model *with* the child and comments, for example "That's not a nice way to play" versus saying nothing. This tests whether what parents say while children watch makes a difference.
5. **Give something back.** Share the results with parents in plain language, with practical advice.

## Why

Parents are legally and morally responsible for their children's welfare, so their informed consent is essential, and their worries about lasting effects are reasonable given what the study itself claims.

The co-viewing condition turns the study towards a question parents can act on. There is some evidence that this matters. In Israel, children raised on kibbutzim, who usually watched television with adults and peers who commented on it, showed no link between watching violence and aggression (see the [cultural lens](/cases/bobo-doll/lenses/cultural/)). A modern version of the Bobo study could test this directly.

## What your redesign gives up

- **Parents watching may change the study.** If children know or sense that their parent is nearby, they may behave more carefully.
- **Full information may change who takes part.** Parents who agree after hearing about the aggressive model may differ from those who refuse, for example in how they treat aggression at home. This is a form of <Term>sampling bias</Term>.
- **More conditions need more children.** Adding co-viewing conditions doubles the number of groups, so a much larger sample is needed to keep each group a sensible size.
