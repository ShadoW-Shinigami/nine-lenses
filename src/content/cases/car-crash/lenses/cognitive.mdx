## The lens in brief

Cognitive psychology studies mental processes: attention, perception, memory, language, reasoning. It grew from the 1950s and was named as a field by Ulric Neisser's book *Cognitive Psychology* (1967). Its central idea is that the mind takes in, transforms, stores and retrieves information, and that these processes can be studied by careful experiments even though they cannot be seen.

Three ideas matter for this study. <Term>Reconstructive memory</Term> is the view that remembering is rebuilding: the mind puts together an account from stored fragments, general knowledge and whatever information is available now. A <Term>schema</Term> is a mental framework of general knowledge about a type of thing or event, such as what usually happens in a car crash. <Term>Source monitoring</Term> is the process of judging where a piece of information in memory came from: something you saw, something you were told, or something you imagined.

This is the study's home lens. The paper was published in a leading cognitive journal, and its explanation is a cognitive one: two kinds of information combined into one memory (p. 588).

## How this lens reads the study

**The verb is information that arrives after the event.** The paper's own framework is cognitive. Two kinds of information go into a memory: "information gleaned during the perception of the original event" and "external information supplied after the fact". Over time they "may be integrated in such a way that we are unable to tell from which source some specific detail is recalled. All we have is one 'memory.'" (p. 588). The question "About how fast were the cars going when they smashed into each other?" supplies one such piece of information: that the cars smashed.

**Verbs carry more than their dictionary meaning.** Following the linguist Charles Fillmore (1971), the paper notes that *hit* and *smashed* imply different speeds and different "likely consequences" (p. 586). A cognitive psychologist would say that each verb activates a different set of expectations. *Contacted* suggests a gentle touch; *smashed* suggests crumpled metal and flying glass. The averages in Experiment 1 rose from 31.8 miles per hour for *contacted* to 40.8 for *smashed* (p. 586), roughly in line with how violent each verb sounds.

**Uncertainty gives the verb room to work.** Students were poor judges of speed. On average they estimated the staged crash at 20 miles per hour at 37.7, and one at 40 at 36.1 (p. 586). When the information from perception is weak, information from other sources, including the question, carries more weight in the reconstruction. This explains why speed, a hard judgement, was a good target for the study.

**Schemas fill the gaps.** Broken glass is part of most people's schema for a car crash. That is why 6 of the 50 control students, who heard no leading question at all, still said they had seen it (p. 587). The verb *smashed* seems to have pushed more students towards their crash schema: 16 of 50 said yes. On this reading, the false "memory" is not random. It is the detail that the schema for a severe crash supplies.

**The verb changed the gist, not only a number.** Table 3 shows that even among students who wrote the same speed, those who heard *smashed* said "yes" to glass more often (p. 587). So the verb was not working only through the speed estimate. A later cognitive theory, <Term>fuzzy-trace theory</Term>, distinguishes between a verbatim trace, an exact record of details, and a gist trace, a record of the general meaning (Brainerd and Reyna, 2002). One reading, offered here as this guide's interpretation, is that *smashed* changed the gist of the memory to "a bad crash". After a week, when verbatim detail had faded, students answered from the gist, and broken glass fits a bad crash.

## What this lens values in the study

It studies memory for a realistic, fast-moving visual event rather than a word list. It names two competing explanations, response bias and memory change, and designs a second experiment to tell them apart. It provides a known truth, the film, against which memory can be checked. And it treated the films as a sample of possible films in its statistics (p. 586), so that the result is meant to apply beyond these particular materials. The research it started led directly to better ways of interviewing witnesses, such as the cognitive interview described on the [importance page](/cases/car-crash/importance/).

## What it would question

- The mechanism was never measured. Nothing in the study shows that two kinds of information were integrated; only the answers changed.
- There was no source test. Asking "Did you see broken glass in the film, or did you only think about it?" could have shown whether students confused sources.
- The researchers collected the students' free accounts of each accident first, but the paper never reports them (pp. 586-587). These accounts could have shown whether the verb changed how students described the crash, without any specific question.
- The paper does not consider whether the original memory survived alongside the new one, a question that became central a decade later.

## Evidence from this tradition

**Words have changed memory before.** Leonard Carmichael, H. P. Hogan and A. A. Walter (1932) gave the same ambiguous drawings different names, such as "eyeglasses" or "dumbbells". When people later drew the shapes from memory, their drawings shifted towards the named object. The abstract states simply: "The directive agency used was language". Loftus and Palmer present their study as an extension of this work (p. 588).

**The effect grows into a research field.** Within a year, Loftus (1975) showed that a question mentioning a barn that was not in a film raised later reports of a barn from 2.7% to 17.3%. Loftus, Miller and Burns (1978) then showed students slides of a car at either a stop sign or a yield sign. A later question mentioned either the sign they had seen or the other sign. Shown both pictures at the end, 75% of students whose question named the right sign chose correctly, against 41% of those whose question named the other sign. This three-step method, an event, misleading information and a test, is the standard procedure for studying the <Term>misinformation effect</Term>: the change in a person's report of an event after they receive misleading information about it.

**But is the original memory lost?** Michael McCloskey and Maria Zaragoza (1985) argued that the standard test cannot tell. When they removed the misleading option from the final test, misled and control participants did equally well, and they concluded that "misleading postevent information has no effect on memory for the original event". Stephen Lindsay and Marcia Johnson (1989) found that much of the effect could be removed by asking people to think about the sources of their memories. A review of 25 studies found that warnings given after the misleading information reduced the effect "to less than half of its size on average" (Blank and Launay, 2014). Both findings suggest that the original is often still there, and that the error lies in judging where a memory came from. This is the idea behind the <Term>source monitoring framework</Term> (Johnson, Hashtroudi and Lindsay, 1993). The framework treats source judgements as "subject to error and disruption". The 1974 phrase, "unable to tell from which source" (p. 588), anticipates it closely.

**A caution about Bartlett.** Frederic Bartlett, a British psychologist, showed in *Remembering* (1932) that people retelling an unfamiliar folk story, "The War of the Ghosts", changed it to fit what they expected. His study is often cited as proof that memory is unreliable. Ost and Costall (2002) argue that "Bartlett did not himself provide such an unqualified account of remembering", and that "Bartlett himself provides several examples of impressively detailed and accurate recall". The cognitive lens says memory is reconstructive. That is not the same as saying it is usually wrong.

**Evidence against the specific effect.** Two recent studies that tested the verb idea in new ways found no effect on speed estimates. One used written descriptions of an ice hockey collision as a "contact", "bump" or "smash" (Goldschmied, Sheptock, Kim and Galily, 2017). The other used car crash videos and found that estimates depended on people's prior beliefs about car speeds, not on whether the cars "made contact" or "smashed" (Raghunath, Fultz and Sanchez, 2021). The general misinformation effect is well supported. The exact verb effect is less certain.

## A study this lens would design

1. Show a short accident film. Before any specific question, ask every student to describe the accident in their own words, and analyse these accounts.
2. Ask the speed question with *smashed*, *hit* or no verb, using three different verb sets and several films so that the result does not depend on one word or one film.
3. Warn half the students, after the questionnaire, that some questions may have contained misleading wording.
4. A week later, ask about broken glass with three options: "yes", "no" and "don't know".
5. For every "yes", ask a source question: "Did you see this in the film, or were you told or did you think about it afterwards?"

**Prediction:** if the effect comes mostly from source confusion, it will shrink when students are warned and when they are asked about sources. If the original memory has been replaced, warnings and source questions will make little difference.

The cognitive lens would explain it as a source monitoring error. Shalini holds two pieces of information about the event: what she saw at the bus stop, and what her neighbour told her afterwards. As in the two-sources framework of Loftus and Palmer (p. 588), these have been combined into one memory. She remembers a red cap but can no longer tell where the memory came from. Her confidence is not evidence of accuracy, because the merged detail feels like any other part of the memory.

The officer could start with an open question, "Tell me everything you saw", and note whether the cap appears before any mention of it. Then the officer could ask a source question: "Did you see the cap yourself, or did someone tell you about it?" Lindsay and Johnson (1989) found that asking people to think about the sources of their memories can remove much of this kind of error. The officer should also record that Shalini spoke to her neighbour before the interview, because information from other people is a known source of contamination.
