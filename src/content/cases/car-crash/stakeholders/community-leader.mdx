## What you care about

Imagine you are an elected ward councillor in a busy neighbourhood of Indore. A crossing in your ward sees a minor accident almost every week: a scooter and an autorickshaw, a car and a cyclist. You care about three things. You want injured people to get help quickly, which means bystanders must not be afraid to stop. You want the police to get a fair account of what happened, so that the wrong driver is not blamed because a crowd decided too quickly. And you want your community to be treated with respect by the police and the courts.

After every accident, you see the same scene: a crowd gathers, everyone talks at once, and within minutes there is a single story of what happened. You wonder how much of that story anyone actually saw.

## What worries you about the original study

- **Its witnesses answered alone.** Students sat in groups, but each answered a printed questionnaire on their own (pp. 586-587). The paper does not say whether they talked about the film, in the room or, in Experiment 2, during the week before the second session. A <Term>co-witness</Term> is another person who saw the same event. Nothing in the design made use of what co-witnesses say to each other. Real witnesses usually do talk. In a survey of real witnesses who had a co-witness, 86% said they had discussed the event with them (Paterson and Kemp, 2006, abstract).
- **It looked at one source of suggestion.** The study changed one word in a researcher's question. In your neighbourhood, the strongest suggestions come from other people at the scene, from family at home, from local news and from social media.
- **Its witnesses had nothing to fear.** The students did not risk being called to a police station or a court. In India, bystanders often fear exactly that. The Supreme Court has described what often happens to a bystander. The bystander "hesitates: suffers a reactive paralysis, sometimes due to fear of legal proceedings, of getting summoned to the police station as a witness" (*SaveLIFE Foundation v. Union of India*, judgment of 26 May 2026).
- **It never asked the community.** The research was designed and run entirely by researchers. Nobody asked the people who actually witness accidents what happens after them.

## Your redesign

1. **Study witnesses in groups, as they really are.** Volunteers from the neighbourhood watch a short film of a minor road collision, in small groups. Some groups discuss what they saw before answering questions; others answer alone. In some discussion groups, one member, a trained volunteer, mentions a detail that was not in the film, such as a broken side mirror.
2. **Use local language and local interviewers.** Questions are asked in Hindi or the participant's own language, by interviewers from the area, and include the word for a violent crash in one version and a mild word in another.
3. **Measure contamination.** Later, each person answers alone. Researchers count how many report the mirror, or adopt other details that came from the discussion and not from the film.
4. **Ask bystanders about their experience.** Separately, interview people who have actually stopped to help at an accident: what they were asked, by whom, how many times, and whether they would do it again.
5. **Design guidance together.** Share the results at a ward meeting and write simple guidance with residents, for example: "Before you talk to others, note down or record on your phone what you yourself saw."

## Why

Research on co-witnesses shows the danger clearly. In one study, pairs of people each saw a slightly different video of the same event and then discussed it. Afterwards, "71% of witnesses who had discussed the event went on to mistakenly recall items acquired during the discussion" (Gabbert, Memon and Allan, 2003, abstract). A replication in ten countries, including a sample of 40 students at NIMHANS in Bengaluru, found the same effect in every country. Errors were especially likely "when the witness had not disputed the co-witness's report" (Ito and colleagues, 2019). This is the community version of the car crash study: the misleading information comes from a neighbour, not a questionnaire.

Indian law now protects people who help. Guidelines that the Supreme Court gave force to in 2016 say that a Good Samaritan, a bystander who helps an injured person, shall not be forced to become a witness. If they choose to be one, they should be examined "in a single examination", at a time and place of their convenience (*SaveLIFE Foundation v. Union of India*, 2016). The officer should be in plain clothes, and an interpreter should be provided if needed. The Motor Vehicles Act, 1988, section 134A, added in 2019, protects Good Samaritans from civil or criminal action for injury or death caused by their negligence while helping. A single, early, careful examination also happens to be what memory research recommends.

The need is large. In 2023, police in India reported 4,80,583 road accidents and 1,72,890 deaths (Ministry of Road Transport and Highways, 2025). Behind each one is a crowd of possible witnesses.

## What your redesign gives up

- **Less control.** Group discussions differ in who talks most and how confident they are, so results will be noisier than in a laboratory. One study of pairs found that a person's confidence strongly predicted whether they persuaded their partner (Wright, Self and Justice, 2000, abstract).
- **Talking together is also how people cope.** Telling neighbours not to discuss an accident may feel cold and unnatural. Discussion helps people make sense of a shock and support each other, so guidance must not stop people from caring for each other.
- **A trained volunteer misleads neighbours.** The study plants a false detail through someone the participants may know. That needs careful ethics review and a thorough debrief, or it could damage trust in the neighbourhood.
- **Local results.** What happens in one ward of Indore may not hold in a village in Assam or a suburb of Chennai.
