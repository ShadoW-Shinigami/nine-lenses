## What you care about

Imagine you are the parent of a nine-year-old in Lucknow. Your child saw a motorbike hit a cyclist outside the school gate, or, much worse, has told you that an adult touched them in a way that frightened them. Either way, police will want to talk to your child, and perhaps a court will too. You care that your child is believed when they tell the truth, and that nobody puts words into their mouth. You also care that your child is not frightened, questioned again and again, or made to face the accused.

You read about this study and wonder: if one word changed what university students remembered, what might a police officer's questions do to a nine-year-old?

## What worries you about the original study

- **It tells you almost nothing about children.** The participants were "students" (pp. 586-587), presumably young adults. Children may react differently. A later study found that the word *fast* rather than *slow* raised children's speed estimates at every age tested. But *smashed* rather than *hit* raised them only in the seventh and eighth grades (Lipscomb, Bregman and McAllister, 1985, abstract). Words affect children, but not always in the way they affect adults.
- **One printed question is not how children are questioned.** Your child will face a person, not a paper. A study of interviews with preschool children looked at social pressure from the interviewer, such as praising some answers or telling the child what other children had supposedly said. This pressure produced far more false accusations (58%) than suggestive questions alone (17%) (Garven, Wood, Malpass and Shaw, 1998, abstract).
- **Your child could not have said "I don't know".** The broken glass question allowed only "yes" or "no" (p. 587). Children often feel that an adult's question must have a right answer. A forced choice pushes them to guess.
- **Repeated questioning was never tested.** The students were asked once and tested once, a week later. A child witness in India may be questioned by police, a magistrate, lawyers and a judge, sometimes years apart.

## Your redesign

1. **Study children, with the right permissions.** Children aged 7 to 14 take part, with written consent from a parent and the child's own agreement. The ethics guidelines of the Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR) require "verbal/oral or written assent" from children aged 7 to 18, in addition to parents' consent (section 6.5.4).
2. **Use a harmless, staged event.** A visitor comes into the classroom and knocks over a cup of water, or a short cartoon shows a bicycle bumping into a fruit cart. Nothing frightening, and nothing about abuse.
3. **Compare kinds of question.** One group gets only open invitations, such as "Tell me everything that happened". Another gets the same plus specific questions. A third gets leading questions, such as "How fast was the bicycle going when it smashed into the cart?"
4. **Allow "I don't know", and teach it first.** Before the interview, the interviewer practises with the child: "If you don't know the answer, just say 'I don't know'. That is a good answer."
5. **A trusted adult in the room, and a recording.** A parent or teacher sits quietly with the child, and the interview is video-recorded so that every question can be checked.
6. **One interview, then a gentle debrief.** Each child is interviewed once. Afterwards, the child and parent hear what really happened, in simple words, and the child is praised for helping, whatever they said.

## Why

India's law already reflects much of this. Under the Protection of Children from Sexual Offences (POCSO) Act, 2012, police should record a child's statement at the child's home or a place of their choice. As far as practicable, the officer should be a woman police officer who "shall not be in uniform" (section 24). The magistrate must record the statement "as spoken by the child" (section 25), in the presence of a parent or "any other person in whom the child has trust", and wherever possible by audio-video means (section 26). In court, lawyers do not question the child directly: they "communicate the questions to be put to the child to the Special Court which shall in turn put those questions to the child" (section 33(2)). The child must not be "called repeatedly to testify" (section 33(5)) or "exposed in any way to the accused" (section 36).

Some of these rules control the thing this study tested, the wording of the questions. Under section 33(2), the lawyers' questions reach the child only through the Special Court. Other rules control something the study did not test, but which follows from its logic. They make sure the child's words are recorded as spoken, not rewritten by an adult (sections 25 and 26).

Research supports the same approach. The US National Institute of Child Health and Human Development developed a structured interview protocol that relies on open invitations. A review of research on this protocol found that interview quality "reliably and dramatically improves" when it is used (Lamb and colleagues, 2007). A major review of children's suggestibility concluded that there are "reliable age differences in suggestibility but that even very young children are capable of recalling much that is forensically relevant" (Ceci and Bruck, 1993). Children are not hopeless witnesses. They need to be asked well.

The British Psychological Society reminds researchers that "children are used to being in inferior power relations with adults", so a researcher "will have to make special efforts" to show the child they are free to say no (section 4.5).

## What your redesign gives up

- **It cannot study what matters most.** A spilled cup is not abuse. No ethics committee would allow researchers to create frightening events for children, so the results can only suggest how a child would respond under real stress.
- **The trusted adult may influence the child.** A parent's presence can calm a child, but a parent's face or a small nod can also signal what answer is wanted.
- **Teaching "I don't know" changes the answers.** Children who have practised saying "I don't know" will guess less. That is the point, but it means the study measures children who have been prepared, not children questioned as police often question them.
- **Small groups by age.** To compare ages, the study needs enough children in each age band, which means more schools, more consent forms and more time.
