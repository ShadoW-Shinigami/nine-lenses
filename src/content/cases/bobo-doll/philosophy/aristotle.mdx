## The tradition in brief

Aristotle (384 to 322 BCE) was born at Stagira in northern Greece, studied with Plato in Athens, and later founded his own school there, the Lyceum. The works that survive are lecture notes, reworked over years. Three of them matter here: the *Nicomachean Ethics*, on how a person becomes good; the *Politics*, on how a city should educate its young; and the *Poetics*, on why people enjoy imitation.

Three ideas run through this page.

**Character comes from habit.** Aristotle's word for character, *ethos* with a long e, is a near twin of his word for habit, *ethos* with a short e, and he makes the pun do real work. A settled trait, which his translators call a <Term>characteristic</Term>, is built up by doing acts of that kind many times. This process is <Term>habituation</Term>.

**Acting is not yet choosing.** Children and animals act voluntarily, but they do not yet <Term>choose</Term> in Aristotle's strict sense, which involves deliberating about what to do and why. Character shows in choice, not in acts alone.

**Education trains pleasure and pain.** A well-brought-up child is not one who merely does the right thing but one who has come to enjoy it. What the young see, hear and practise first makes, in his words, "the whole difference".

## What Aristotle says about habit, imitation and the young

**Character is neither given by nature nor against it.** "None of the moral virtues are present in us by nature, since nothing that exists by nature is habituated to be other than it is" (NE II.1, 1103a18-20). A stone will not learn to fall upwards "not even if someone habituates it by throwing it upward ten thousand times" (1103a20-22). People are different: "Neither by nature, therefore, nor contrary to nature are the virtues present; they are instead present in us who are of such a nature as to receive them, and who are completed through habit" (1103a23-26). Nature supplies the capacity. Practice supplies the character.

**We learn by doing.** "For as regards those things we must learn how to do, we learn by doing them – for example, by building houses, people become house builders, and by playing the cithara, they become cithara players. So too, then, by doing just things we become just; moderate things, moderate; and courageous things, courageous" (1103a31-b2). A cithara is a plucked instrument like a lyre. Practice can go either way: "as a result of building houses well, people will be good house builders; but as a result of doing so badly, they will be bad ones" (1103b9-11). That is why "there would be no need of a teacher" if people were simply born good or bad (1103b12-13).

**Anger is habituated too.** "The case is similar as regards desires and bouts of anger. For some people become moderate and gentle, others licentious and irascible, the former as a result of conducting themselves in the one way, the latter as a result of doing so in the other" (1103b17-21). Then the sentence that matters most for this study: "It makes no small difference, then, whether one is habituated in this or that way straight from childhood but a very great difference – or rather the whole difference" (1103b23-25). Aristotle does not treat anger as an energy that builds up and must escape. Each act of anger makes the next more likely.

**Imitation is natural, early and pleasant.** "The instinct of imitation is implanted in man from childhood, one difference between him and other animals being that he is the most imitative of living creatures, and through imitation learns his earliest lessons; and no less universal is the pleasure felt in things imitated" (*Poetics*, chapter 4). The pleasure comes from learning: recognising a likeness is a small act of understanding. Imitation, he concludes, "is one instinct of our nature".

**What children see and hear forms them.** In his ideal city, officials would "be careful what tales or stories the children hear" (*Politics* VII.17). "There is nothing which the legislator should be more careful to drive away than indecency of speech; for the light utterance of shameful words leads soon to shameful actions. The young especially should never be allowed to repeat or hear anything of the sort." The same goes for pictures and plays: "Let the rulers take care that there be no image or picture representing unseemly actions". His reason is about first impressions: "we always like best whatever comes first. And therefore youth should be kept strangers to all that is bad, and especially to things which suggest vice or hate."

**Children act voluntarily but do not yet choose.** "Both children and animals share in what is voluntary but not in choice" (NE III.2, 1111b8-9), and "choice seems to belong very much to virtue and to distinguish people's characters more than actions do" (1111b5-6). An act counts as coming from virtue only "if he acts knowingly; second, if he acts by choosing and by choosing the actions in question for their own sake; and, third, if he acts while being in a steady and unwavering state" (NE II.4, 1105a31-33). One act proves nothing: "one swallow does not make a spring, nor does one day" (NE I.7, 1098a18).

**Spirit hears reason, but mishears it.** Anger, which Aristotle calls spiritedness, "seems to hear reason in some way, but to mishear it, like swift servants who run off before they hear what is said in its entirety and then err in carrying out the command, or as dogs bark if there is merely a knock at the door, before examining whether it is a friend" (NE VII.6, 1149a25-29). "To become angry belongs to everyone and is an easy thing"; getting it right, "to whom [one ought to do so], how much, when, for the sake of what, and how", is hard (NE II.9, 1109a26-29). He is not against anger. People who "do not get angry at the things they ought are held to be foolish" (NE IV.5, 1126a4-5).

## Reading the study through this tradition

**The central finding is what Aristotle expected, reward or no reward.** Children copied the model's unusual acts and exact words, and about a third repeated the harmless phrases "He keeps coming back for more" and "He sure is a tough fella". The absence of any reward, which was Bandura's main blow against the reinforcement theorists, is no puzzle for Aristotle. Copying is an instinct and pleasant in itself. The harmless phrase is the best evidence from his point of view: it shows a child reproducing a whole performance, not discharging anger. He and Bandura agree that learning does not require reinforcement. Aristotle would add that pleasure and pain still matter, because they are how a child comes to like or dislike what she copies, and the paper did not measure them.

**An act, not a character: the children were not "aggressive".** Seventy-two children, one ten-minute exposure, a twenty-minute test. For Aristotle a characteristic is built by "doing just and moderate things many times" (NE II.4, 1105b4-5). One session in a strange room is a single act done on another's example. The children's acts were voluntary, so the behaviour is real and matters. But they were not chosen, and they say nothing about settled character. Aristotle would agree with this guide's warning that the study did not show that the children became more aggressive people. He would also notice that the teacher's ratings of the children's everyday behaviour, drawn from months of observation and used only to match the groups, are closer to his idea of evidence about character than the twenty-minute test is.

**The frustration step provoked spirit, and the model had already told it what to do.** Every child was shown attractive toys and then told that they were kept for other children. Aristotle would say the experimenters first stirred the child's spirit and then, in the aggressive condition, had already shown it a target and a script: "Sock him in the nose", "Kick him". Spirit, in his image, hears a slight and sets off after revenge without examining the case. He would also ask what a child is being habituated to feel about adults who take toys away. On catharsis, the idea that watching aggression releases it, Aristotle is on the paper's side. His theory of character predicts, as the study found, that acting out anger makes it more available, not less.

**The adult in the corner was never a neutral figure.** Bandura wanted to show that imitation did not need an emotional bond with the model, so the model was, in the paper's words, a "more or less neutral" figure. Aristotle would reply that an adult welcomed into a nursery by the person in charge carries authority to a child whether or not the child loves him. The children's own words, "He's a good fighter like Daddy" and "I want to sock like Al" (p. 581), treat the model as someone to admire. The political lesson is one Aristotle would press further than Bandura. If what children see and hear forms them, then someone must take care over it, which is exactly what his officials for education do with stories, pictures and comedy. He would also raise the ethics of the design: his officials exist to prevent what the experimenters arranged, and he thinks words lead to deeds. Seventy-two children were shown a bad example so that adults could count the result.

**The sex-typing results show habituation already at work.** Both boys and girls copied the male model's physical aggression more, and some children said "That's not the way for a lady to behave". The children were not blank. They already felt that some acts were fitting for a man and shameful in a woman, and that feeling shaped what they copied. This is habituation doing its ordinary work: "the soul of the student must be prepared beforehand by means of habits so as to feel delight and hatred in a noble way" (NE X.9, 1179b24-26). The paper's own explanation, that children had been rewarded differently for "sex appropriate" behaviour, is a habituation story. One caution: Aristotle held that men and women differ by nature, so he would be less inclined than this guide to treat the gender roles as merely taught.

**The calm model may be the more important lesson.** Children who watched the calm male model were less aggressive than the controls on several measures, and children who watched either calm model spent more than twice as long simply sitting quietly, without touching the toys. For Aristotle the practical question is never only whether bad example teaches bad acts, but what example the young should be given. The quiet adult building with the construction toy is the kind of first impression he wants children to form. He would add that one quiet adult cannot make a gentle child, any more than one aggressive adult can make a violent one. What both show is the direction in which repeated experience would push.

## Where it agrees, and where it clashes

Aristotle and the paper agree that children learn new patterns of action by watching, without reward; that what children watch shapes what they later do; and that acting out anger does not use it up. His framework also supports the study's policy direction, since he wanted the city to control what the young see and hear.

They clash on what the counted acts mean. For Aristotle a single afternoon's copying reveals no character, so "aggressive" is the wrong word for the children. The frustration step confuses imitation with imitation under insult. The teacher's months of observation are better evidence than the mirror's twenty minutes. And an adult who performs violence in a nursery is a teacher, not a neutral stimulus, which raises an ethical question that the paper does not ask.

## Questions it would put to the researchers

- You counted acts in five-second intervals. By what right do you call the children "aggressive" rather than "imitative", given that they had no choice in my sense and no settled state?
- What did the children enjoy? Imitation is pleasant in itself. Did any child laugh, and did any child look ashamed? You measured neither.
- Who, in the child's eyes, was the adult with the mallet: a stranger, a playmate or a teacher? Why do you call an adult welcomed by the teacher a neutral figure?
- You provoked every child's spirit with the toys before the test. Were you studying imitation, or imitation under insult?
- The nursery teacher had rated these children over months. Why is her rating used only to match groups, and not treated as the real measure of character?
- If what children see forms them, who in your city has the task of my officials for education, and how will your finding reach them?
- What would you have found if the model had been copied every day for a year? That is the experiment that would speak to character.

## Cautions

- **"We are what we repeatedly do. Excellence, then, is not an act but a habit" is not Aristotle.** It is Will Durant's summary in *The Story of Philosophy* (1926). The genuine passages are the ones quoted above: "by doing just things we become just" and "one swallow does not make a spring".
- **"Virtue is a habit" is too simple.** Virtue arises from habit, but it also requires choice and practical wisdom. Aristotle is not a behaviourist about habit, and habituation for him trains emotion and pleasure, not only action.
- **The mean is not moderation.** Aristotle's doctrine of the mean says that each virtue lies between two faults, but it does not ask for moderation in everything. Never getting angry is "foolish" and "slavish" (NE IV.5, 1126a4-8). Do not write that Aristotle wanted aggression kept moderate.
- **Catharsis is about music and tragedy**, and what Aristotle meant by it is still debated. Nothing in his texts says that watching violence reduces violence.
- **Aristotle's views on women, slaves and foreigners** belong to his time and place. He cannot be enlisted as a critic of the study's sex-typing.
- **Aristotle did not anticipate this study.** He had no concept of an experiment, no control group and no interest in group averages. His claims concern the formation of character over years. The honest statement is that his framework agrees with some findings and gives reasons to doubt what is concluded from them. A modern philosopher, John Doris, has argued that experiments like this one show that behaviour depends more on situations than on robust traits (*Lack of Character*, 2002). Doris does not discuss the Bobo doll study itself, and Aristotle's defenders reply that the traits such experiments test are not what virtue ethics means by character.
