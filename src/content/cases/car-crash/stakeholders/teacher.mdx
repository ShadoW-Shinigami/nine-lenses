## What you care about

Imagine you teach research methods to BA psychology students at a college in Jaipur. The car crash study is on your syllabus, and it is one of the easiest classic experiments to repeat in class: a short video, a questionnaire with one changed word, and a second questionnaire a week later. You want your students to see an experiment from the inside. You also care that your students trust you, and that nobody feels pressured to take part because you set their marks. And you want them to learn something true about psychology, including what to think if the result does not appear.

## What worries you about the original study

- **It reports nothing about consent or debriefing.** The paper describes the films and the questions, but not what participants were told, whether they could leave, or whether anyone explained the study afterwards (pp. 586-587). If you copy the procedure from the paper, you copy those gaps too.
- **The films were meant to shock.** Four of the seven films were staged crashes made "to illustrate what can happen to human beings when cars collide at various speeds" (p. 586). You do not know which of your students has lost a relative on the road.
- **Its groups were small, and so is your class.** Experiment 1 had 9 students per verb. In Experiment 2, the broken glass difference between *smashed* and *hit* rested on 16 against 7 students, and this guide's own test of that pair was borderline (see the [findings page](/cases/car-crash/findings/)). A class of 40 split into two groups may easily fail to show the effect.
- **Recent attempts have failed.** A <Term>conceptual replication</Term> tests the same idea as an earlier study, but with different materials. Two recent conceptual replications found no effect of the verb on speed estimates (Goldschmied and colleagues, 2017; Raghunath and colleagues, 2021). If your class shows no difference, students may conclude either that they "did it wrong" or that psychology is fake. Neither conclusion is right.
- **You hold power over your students.** If you run the study, collect the answers and mark the course, some students will feel they cannot refuse.

## Your redesign

1. **Ask for ethics review, even for a class demonstration.** The study keeps its purpose from students. The ethics guidelines of the Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR) call this <Term>incomplete disclosure</Term>: leaving out the aim of a study without telling participants anything false. ICMR lists it among its types of deception (Box 9.5), and it sends research involving any type of deception to full committee review (section 9.2.9 and Table 4.2). So the demonstration needs review even though nothing false is said. Your college committee can approve a standard version once and reuse it each year.
2. **Make taking part voluntary and anonymous.** Students write a code word, not their name, on both questionnaires so the two can be matched. A colleague or a student volunteer hands out and collects the forms, so you never see who answered what.
3. **Offer an equal alternative.** Students who prefer not to watch crash videos read the 1974 paper and write a one-page summary instead. Nobody's marks depend on the choice.
4. **Borrow the protections from the other pages.** Use a mild clip with a content warning and debrief on the same day as the second questionnaire, as the [participant page](/cases/car-crash/stakeholders/participant/) proposes. Add the "I don't know" option and the free account that the [researcher page](/cases/car-crash/stakeholders/researcher/) proposes.
5. **Ask the class to predict before it sees any results.** Each student writes down which group will give the higher speed estimates, which group will say "yes" to broken glass more often, and by how much. The predictions stay sealed until the results are in. This is a classroom version of <Term>preregistration</Term>: publishing a study's plan and predictions before any data are collected. Nobody can then claim afterwards that they "knew it all along", or explain away a result they did not expect.
6. **Pool the data across years.** Keep the clip, the questions and the code-word system the same every year, and add each class's answers to one shared file. A class of 40 gives about 20 students per group. After five years the pooled data hold about 100 per group. That is still fewer than the hundreds per group a researcher would plan for, but far more than any single class.
7. **Make the debrief a lesson.** In the debrief, open the sealed predictions and compare them with the results. The session becomes a lesson on design, ethics and replication.

## Why

The ethics codes are clear about students as participants. The American Psychological Association's code says that "when research participation is a course requirement or an opportunity for extra credit, the prospective participant is given the choice of equitable alternative activities" (Standard 8.04(b)). The British Psychological Society asks researchers to be "particularly diligent in establishing the valid consent of any person who is in a dependent or unequal relationship to them (e.g. student or client)" (section 8). The ICMR guidelines list students among people with "diminished autonomy due to dependency or being under a hierarchical system" (Box 6.2). They warn that people in a hierarchy "may not be in a position to disagree to participate for fear of authority" (section 6.9.2).

There is a teaching reason too. A demonstration that "fails" is a good lesson, if you prepare for it. You can show students why a sample of 20 per group may miss a real but small effect. You can show why the original statistics were thinner than textbooks suggest. And you can show why researchers now preregister studies and pool data across many sites. The broad finding is that question wording and later information can change what witnesses report. Every one of 76 eyewitness experts in a recent survey agreed with it (Seale-Carlisle and colleagues, 2024), even though the exact verb effect is less certain.

## What your redesign gives up

- **A weaker demonstration.** A mild clip, a content warning and an "I don't know" option all make the effect smaller. Students may see little difference between groups.
- **Less drama in class.** A teacher who reveals a striking result in front of the class makes a memorable lesson. Pooled, anonymous data analysed later is less exciting.
- **More paperwork.** Ethics review, information sheets, code words and a colleague to collect forms all take time in a crowded term.
- **A smaller, more selected group.** Students who choose the written alternative may be those most upset by crash videos, so the class sample becomes less typical.
