## The tradition in brief

Aristotle (384 to 322 BCE) studied with Plato in Athens and later founded his own school there, the Lyceum. His *Nicomachean Ethics* is a set of lecture notes on how a person becomes good, and it contains the earliest careful treatment of three questions this study raises: when an act is really ours, what is wrong with lying, and what it is like to be at odds with oneself.

Three ideas run through this page.

**The voluntary.** An act is forced only when the person contributes nothing to it. Acting under pressure is "mixed", but it is still the person's own act, and praise and blame still apply.

**Truthfulness is a virtue.** Falsehood is "base and blameworthy" in itself, and the truthful person is truthful even when nothing turns on it. Lying for money is the lowest kind.

**The divided soul.** Aristotle's good person is of one mind with himself and has nothing to regret. Bad people are "torn by faction", and the division is painful. This is the nearest thing in Aristotle to a theory of the discomfort of inconsistency, and it is a theory of character, not of single thoughts.

## What Aristotle says about choice, lying and the divided self

Each idea below starts with Aristotle's own words, then explains them in plain English, then says why they matter for this study.

**Forced, voluntary and mixed.** "That which is forced is something whose origin is external, since it is the sort of thing to which the person who is acting or undergoing something contributes nothing – for example, if a wind, or people who have control over someone, should carry him off somewhere" (NE III.1, 1110a1-4). Acts done under pressure are different. A captain who throws cargo overboard in a storm does something "no one voluntarily" does, yet "everyone who has sense would do it". Such actions "are mixed, though they are more voluntary [than involuntary]" (1110a8-12), because "the origin of the movement of the parts [of his body] that serve as instruments in such actions is in the person himself", and so "it is also up to him to act or not to act" (1110a15-18). People are praised when they "endure something shameful or painful in return for great and noble things", and blamed when they endure "shameful things in the service of nothing noble or measured" (1110a19-23).

In plain words: Aristotle is building the first careful account of when an act belongs to the person who did it. An act is <Term>forced</Term> only when the person contributes nothing to it, as when the wind blows a ship off course. Most acts done under pressure are not like that. The captain who throws the cargo overboard in a storm would never choose to lose the cargo, but he does choose to throw it, so the act is "mixed": partly unwelcome, but still his, because the decision came from him and he could have refused. Aristotle adds a rule for judging such acts. If you endure something shameful to gain something great, people praise you. If you endure something shameful for a trivial reason, people rightly blame you, because the pressure was not enough to excuse the act.

Why it matters here: the students told a lie under mild pressure, a polite request from an authority and a small payment. Aristotle's framework asks whether that pressure was a storm or a breeze, and the three students who refused answer the question.

**Pleasure and gain are not force.** If pleasant things counted as compelling us, "everything would be forced: it is for the sake of these things that everyone does everything" (1110b9-12). "But it is laughable to attribute to external things the cause of one's being easily snared by such things, rather than to attribute the cause to oneself, as is attributing the cause of noble things to oneself but that of shameful ones to pleasures" (1110b13-15).

In plain words: someone might object that pleasure and money compel us just as a storm does. Aristotle replies that if that were true, nothing would be voluntary, since everyone does everything for the sake of something pleasant or good. The inducement is outside you, but being the kind of person who is caught by it is inside you, and that is where the cause lies. He then notices a trick that people play on themselves. When they do something noble they credit themselves, and when they do something shameful they blame the temptation. Psychologists later gave this a name, self-serving attribution, and Aristotle treats it as laughable.

Why it matters here: "I did it for the twenty dollars" is, for Aristotle, exactly the laughable move, and so is "I did it because he asked".

**Truthfulness.** "In itself, what is false is base and blameworthy, whereas what is true is noble and praiseworthy" (NE IV.7, 1127a28-30). The truthful person is truthful "in the situations in which being such makes no difference", and so "still more so will he be truthful in the situations in which it does" (1127b4-6). Motives are ranked. Someone who pretends to more than he is "for no particular purpose" is "more silly than bad"; one who does it "for the sake of reputation or honor is not overly blameworthy"; but "he who does this for money (or anything that would lead to money) is more unseemly" (1127b9-13).

In plain words: this chapter of the *Ethics* is about honesty in how people present themselves, between the boaster who claims too much and the falsely modest person who claims too little. Its general claims reach further. Falsehood is bad in itself, not only because of its effects. The truthful person is truthful even in small matters where nothing turns on it, and that is the test of character, because someone honest in small things will be honest when it counts. And Aristotle grades liars by their motive. Lying for no reason is silly rather than wicked. Lying for honour is not too blameworthy. Lying for money, or for anything that leads to money, is the lowest kind.

Why it matters here: both groups in the study lied for money, and Aristotle's ranking runs the opposite way from common sense. The student who took twenty dollars has the worse motive, not the better excuse.

**Knowing and not acting.** Socrates had argued that nobody knowingly does wrong, so apparent weakness of will must be ignorance. Aristotle replied that this "is in contention with the phenomena that come plainly to sight" (NE VII.2, 1145b27-28), then half agreed. The weak-willed person "knows" the way a drunk or a beginner knows: "those who are first learning will put together arguments but not yet understand them", so "those who lack self-restraint speak just as actors do" (NE VII.3, 1147a21-24). Aristotle also praised a character in Sophocles, Neoptolemus, who agreed to a lie and then could not go through with it: he "does not abide by what he was persuaded of by Odysseus, on account of his being pained at telling a lie" (NE VII.2, 1146a19-21).

In plain words: the problem of <Term>weakness of will</Term> is the problem of doing what you know you should not. Socrates said this never really happens: if you do wrong, you did not truly know it was wrong. Aristotle says that contradicts what everyone sees, and then gives a subtler answer. There are two ways of knowing something: having the knowledge, and actively using it. The weak-willed person has it but is not using it at the moment of acting, like a drunk who can recite a poem without taking it in, or a beginner who can repeat an argument without understanding it. He speaks "as actors do", saying lines that are not his. Neoptolemus, in a play by Sophocles, is Aristotle's example of the right kind of inconsistency. He agreed to deceive a man and then could not bring himself to do it, because lying pained him, and Aristotle praises him for breaking his word.

Why it matters here: the students had read their lines from a sheet before going in and then spoke them as actors do, and the two who afterwards told the woman the truth are Aristotle's Neoptolemus.

**The soul at odds with itself.** The decent person "is of like mind with himself and longs for the same things with his whole soul" (NE IX.4, 1166a13-14) and "is without regret, so to speak" (1166a29). Base people "differ with themselves and desire some things but wish for others" (1166b7-8). "Their soul is torn by faction: one part, on account of its corruption, feels pain when abstaining from certain things, while another part feels pleasure; one part drags them here and the other drags them there, as if tearing them asunder" (1166b19-22). Such people "flee themselves because, when by themselves, they are reminded of many odious things", and "when they are with others, however, they forget" (1166b13-16). "For base people teem with regret" (1166b24-25). Regret is also a good sign: the weak-willed person "is apt to feel regret" and is "curable", whereas the settled wrongdoer "abides by his choice" and is "incurable" (NE VII.8, 1150b29-32).

In plain words: Aristotle's good person is of one mind. What he wants, what he does and how he feels about it afterwards all agree, so he has nothing to regret. Bad people are divided. One part of them wants the pleasure of the wrong act and another part wishes they had not done it, and the two parts pull against each other, which is painful. That is why such people avoid being alone: solitude brings the division to mind, and company helps them forget. Regret is the sign of the division, and Aristotle treats it as hopeful. The person who feels regret is still "curable"; the person who has stopped feeling it has settled into vice.

Why it matters here: this is the nearest thing in Aristotle to Festinger's unpleasant inner state. But it belongs to a kind of person, not to a pair of thoughts, and the way out he recommends is to change the conduct, not the belief.

**Self-deception grows out of character.** "In the case of most people, a deception appears to occur on account of the pleasure involved, for what is not good appears to them as good" (NE III.4, 1113a33-b1). How things appear "to each" depends on "whatever sort of person he is", and since we helped to make ourselves that sort of person, we are answerable for the appearance (NE III.5, 1114a31-b3). Character creeps up: "at each moment, the growth ... is not noticed, just as in the case of illnesses" (1114b31-1115a1).

In plain words: Aristotle does not think people usually choose what they know to be bad. Rather, the bad has come to look good to them, and pleasure is the usual reason. How things look to you depends on the kind of person you have become. Since you helped make yourself that kind of person, by your earlier acts, you are answerable for your distorted view. And the distortion creeps up slowly, like an illness, so that no single step is noticed.

Why it matters here: the $1 student who decides the tasks were not so bad after all is, on this account, letting comfort change how the hour looks to him, and taking one unnoticed step.

**Non-contradiction is logic, not a drive.** Aristotle's "most certain of all principles" is that "the same attribute cannot at the same time belong and not belong to the same subject and in the same respect", and he adds that "it is impossible for any one to believe the same thing to be and not to be" (*Metaphysics* IV.3, 1005b19-25).

In plain words: the <Term>principle of non-contradiction</Term> says that a statement and its denial cannot both be true at the same time in the same respect. Aristotle called it the firmest of all principles, and he added that nobody can genuinely believe both halves of a contradiction at once. This is a claim about truth and about what a mind can do, not about discomfort. It matters because Festinger's "dissonant cognitions" are not contradictions. "I found the hour boring" and "I told her it was fun" can both be true; there is no logical clash, only a moral one.

Why it matters here: anyone tempted to present dissonance theory as a psychological version of Aristotle's law should notice that, applied to the students' thoughts, the law would make dissonance impossible rather than motivating.

## Reading the study through this tradition

**Nothing was forced, and the act is the student's.** The paper calls its design "forced compliance". Aristotle would object to the word. A course requirement and a polite request from an embarrassed experimenter are not a storm at sea or a tyrant holding one's family. The lie was, at most, a mixed action, and in truth plainly voluntary: "where there may be a 'no,' there may also be a 'yes'" (NE III.5, 1113b8). The three students who refused prove that "no" was available. Festinger's theory in fact needs the student to experience the act as his own, and later research found that the effect appears only when students feel free to refuse. Aristotle would say that is because an act one contributed nothing to is not one's act, so there is nothing to be at odds with oneself about. Where he parts from the paper is its treatment of the money as a "consonant cognition", the theory's term for a thought that fits with the act and so lessens the inconsistency. Payment is an inducement, not a justification.

**The $20 liar is the more blameworthy, and the $1 liar has no story to tell.** On Aristotle's ranking, lying for money is the lowest kind of falsehood, so the student paid $20 has done the worse thing and has an unseemly motive to show for it. The student paid $1 has done something shameful "in the service of nothing noble or measured" and cannot even plead gain. The only ways to live with that are to accept that he is the kind of person who tells pointless lies, or to decide that it was not really a lie, because the tasks were not really boring. Festinger says the student took the second route. Aristotle supplies the moral psychology for why the first route is unbearable: "the sorts of things each person says and does, and thus how he lives, are determined by the sort of person he is" (NE IV.7, 1127a26-28). But he would call the second route self-deception, not opinion change, and he would expect a decent person to take a third route the experiment never offered: to feel pain, tell the woman the truth, and refuse next time.

**The students who refused or told the truth acted as a truthful person acts.** Of the eleven set aside, three refused the money and the job, two told the woman that they had been hired, and one wanted her number so that he could explain. The design treats them as failures of the manipulation. For Aristotle they are the ones whose behaviour reveals a characteristic, because they acted against inducement and against a request from authority in a situation "in which being such makes no difference": a stranger, two minutes, no consequences. Neoptolemus is exactly the student who agreed to the script and then could not deliver it. Aristotle would want these six studied, not discarded, and he would observe that a theory which can only be tested on people who have just acted basely is a theory about the base.

**The scripted lie was an actor's performance, for both groups.** The students read a sheet of claims and told the woman "it's really very interesting". The recordings showed that the $20 students were, if anything, slightly more persuasive. By Aristotle's lights both groups spoke "just as actors do", reciting enjoyment they did not feel. The difference appeared only afterwards, in which group's opinion shifted. Aristotle would call the rating an opinion about a trivial matter, weakly held, and he would not be surprised that it could be moved. His interest lies elsewhere: not in whether the number moved, but in whether the student will lie more easily next time, because "the growth ... is not noticed, just as in the case of illnesses".

**The unmeasured drive has an older name.** Festinger treated dissonance as an unpleasant state like hunger, but the paper measured no discomfort, no arousal and no regret. Aristotle has a vivid description of inconsistency as painful, and of the person who avoids his own company because solitude reminds him of what he did. So he can accept that the $1 students felt something. But in his account the pain is a symptom of a divided character, and a sign that the person is still curable. For Festinger the discomfort is relieved by making the belief fit the act. For Aristotle, relieving it that way is how someone becomes the settled liar who no longer feels it. Read through Aristotle, the experiment demonstrates the first step from weakness of will towards vice, taken by twenty young men in one afternoon.

**Bem's self-perception theory reads character off the wrong kind of act.** Daryl Bem later argued that the $1 students simply observed their own behaviour and reasoned that they must have found the tasks fun. Aristotle agrees that people read character off acts; that is what praise and blame do. But the reading is valid only when the act was chosen for its own sake from a settled state. A two-minute speech made at another's request, for a fee, from no settled state at all, fails every test: "it is possible to do something skillful in letters by chance or on the instructions of another" (NE II.4, 1105a22-23). So the inference "I said it, therefore I believe it" is exactly the error his three conditions exist to block. Neither Festinger nor Bem asks what kind of person the student was before he arrived, and for Aristotle that is where the explanation lives. He would predict differences between students in who shifts and who does not, which group averages hide.

**The researchers, too, were forming habits.** Aristotle would turn his chapter on truthfulness on the experimenters: the cover story planted in the first week of term, the false explanation, the hidden tape recorder, the hired "girl", the professor's name used to press the request. "The activities that pertain in each case produce people of a corresponding sort" (NE III.5, 1114a7), and he would ask what a laboratory makes of people who deceive for a living. The debriefing, being a speech, would not impress him as a remedy, since "it is not possible, or not easy, to alter by means of speech what has long been enforced by their characters" (NE X.9, 1179b16-18).

## Where it agrees, and where it clashes

Aristotle and the paper agree that acting against one's convictions is uncomfortable, that the discomfort moves people to repair the division, and that rehearsal is not what changes a person. He would value the paper's attention to motive and its honest reporting of refusals. He would also value one recorded fact: every student, "without exception", gave the money back when told the truth, which he would read as shame still at work.

They clash on the direction of repair. For the paper, consistency is restored when the belief moves to fit the act. For Aristotle, that is the wrong repair: the healthy response to having lied is to feel pain and change one's conduct, and a person who instead changes his belief is one step closer to the settled liar who feels nothing. They also clash on the unit of explanation. The paper explains the result through a pair of thoughts; Aristotle explains it through the kind of person the student is becoming.

## Questions it would put to the researchers

- Why do you call it "forced compliance" when three students refused and nothing but a polite request was applied? Your theory needs the act to be voluntary.
- You set aside the students who told the woman the truth and the students who refused. They acted as a truthful person acts, when nothing turned on it. What were they like, and why are they not your result?
- Did the $1 students feel pain or regret? You say dissonance is like hunger, but you asked no one whether he was hungry.
- Which is worse, to lie for a dollar or for twenty? You assume that the money excuses. I say that lying for money is the more unseemly kind.
- Is a shift of about two points on a scale about a boring task a change of belief, or a young man's opinion on a trivial matter, weakly held and easily moved?
- Would the same student lie more readily tomorrow? You measured the opinion and not the growth.
- You and your assistants deceived seventy-one young men, in layers, over a term. What has that practice made of you?

## Cautions

- **Weakness of will is contested.** Whether Aristotle's weak-willed person really "knows" at the moment of acting is disputed among scholars; the Stanford Encyclopedia summarises his answer as "yes and no". Do not state one reading as settled.
- **"Voluntary" is wider than "responsible".** Children and animals act voluntarily for Aristotle. Be careful when translating his verdicts into modern talk of responsibility.
- **Non-contradiction is not dissonance.** Aristotle's logical principle, applied to Festinger's cognitions, would make dissonance impossible rather than motivating, because the cognitions are not contradictions.
- **Aristotle did not anticipate this study.** He had no concept of an experiment and no interest in group averages. His claims concern the formation of character over years; the study measures one afternoon.
- **A modern challenge.** The philosopher John Doris has argued, from experiments such as Milgram's, that behaviour depends more on situations than on robust character traits, and that single-trial studies are "silent on the crucial question of consistency" (*Lack of Character*, 2002, p. 48). Doris does not discuss Festinger or Bem. Aristotle's defenders reply that virtue is rare, and that the few who refused are the relevant cases.
- **Translations differ.** Quotations here follow Bartlett and Collins. Other editions use different English for the same Greek. Name the translator when you quote.
