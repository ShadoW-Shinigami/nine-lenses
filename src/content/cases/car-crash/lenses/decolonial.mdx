## The lens in brief

<Term>Decolonial psychology</Term> starts from history. Modern psychology grew mainly in Europe and North America, at a time when European powers ruled much of the world. Through universities, textbooks, journals and law, this psychology spread globally and came to be treated as simply "psychology". Knowledge from elsewhere was often treated as culture, religion or folklore, or as raw data for Western theories.

In India, Durganand Sinha's *Psychology in a Third World Country* (1986) questioned how well imported Western psychology fitted Indian lives. The title of an early article by Ashis Nandy (1974) names the problem: Indian psychology as "a recipient culture of science", receiving its questions and methods from elsewhere. Ajit Dalal and Girishwar Misra (2010) defined Indian psychology as "a school of psychology primarily rooted in the diverse Indian thought systems with panhuman appeal". Sunil Bhatia's *Decolonizing Psychology* (2018) studies how Western psychological ideas shape young people's identities in India today. Glenn Adams and colleagues (2015) argued that most psychological science "still reflects and promotes the interests of a privileged minority of people in affluent centers", and named "three approaches to decolonization: indigenization, accompaniment, and denaturalization".

This lens differs from the [cultural lens](/cases/car-crash/lenses/cultural/). The cultural lens asks whether the finding holds in other languages and places. The decolonial lens asks who made the rules for judging witnesses, who produces knowledge about memory, and whose traditions of knowledge are left out.

## How this lens reads the study

**A colonial law of witnesses.** The paper begins from the legal idea of a leading question (p. 585). In India, that idea arrived through colonial law. The Indian Evidence Act of 1872 was drafted by James Fitzjames Stephen, a British official, who moved the Bill in the colonial Legislative Council on 5 March 1872 (Stephen, 1872, preface). Section 141 defined a leading question as "Any question suggesting the answer which the person putting it wishes or expects to receive". Its model was the English courtroom, where two sides question witnesses in turn. The historian Elizabeth Kolsky (2005) opens her history of colonial criminal procedure with the view of Thomas Babington Macaulay, another British official. In his view, India should receive "good government" but not "free government". Law was to be the gift.

In 2023 India passed a replacement, the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, which came into force on 1 July 2024. Its section 146(1) repeats the 1872 definition word for word, and its rules on when leading questions may be asked are almost unchanged. The [India page](/cases/car-crash/india/) gives the full text.

**The drafter's insight.** Stephen himself wrote about the weakness of witnesses. In a footnote to his introduction, he observed that the "power to tell the truth" is "much less common than people usually suppose it to be". He went on: "It is extremely difficult for an untrained person not to mix up inference and assertion. It is also difficult for such a person to distinguish between what they themselves saw and heard and what they were told by others, unless their attention is specially directed to the distinction" (Stephen, 1872, pp. 43-44). This is very close to Loftus and Palmer's own conclusion, a century later, that witnesses become "unable to tell from which source some specific detail is recalled" (p. 588). The idea did not begin in an American laboratory.

**The drafter's prejudice.** The same footnote opens with the story of a lawyer in Ceylon who watched "the witnesses' toes", because "As soon as they began to lie they always fidgeted about with them" (p. 43). It then says: "The commonplaces about the evidence of policemen, children, women, and the natives of particular countries belong to this subject". So the man who wrote India's law of evidence placed "natives" alongside children and women as people whose testimony needed special suspicion. The decolonial lens asks how much of that suspicion survives in rules and habits that were never rewritten, only renumbered.

**An American sample as the measure of "a witness".** The study's 195 participants were described only as "students" (pp. 586-587). The authors worked at an American university and used American driver's education films. Yet the paper's conclusions speak of "a witness" in general (p. 586). Joseph Henrich, Steven Heine and Ara Norenzayan (2010) called the usual participants of psychology research <Term>WEIRD</Term>: from Western, Educated, Industrialised, Rich and Democratic societies. They described them as "among the least representative populations one could find for generalizing about humans". Jeffrey Arnett (2008) noted that Americans are "less than 5% of the world's population", yet dominate the participants of leading psychology journals. Indian students now learn about memory from these samples, and Indian courts cite the findings, while research on Indian witnesses remains thin.

**Indian traditions already distinguished memory from addition.** Classical Indian thought has detailed theories of memory and testimony. In the *Yoga Sutra* of Patanjali, in the translation by James Haughton Woods (1914), sutra 1.11 defines memory (*smriti*) as "not-adding-surreptitiously to a once experienced object" (p. 32). The commentary printed with it adds that a "memory" which brings in something that was never experienced "is not [really] memory, but is misconception". It is called memory only "in so far as it resembles memory" (p. 33). By this definition, the broken glass report is not memory at all. It is a misconception that resembles memory. The Yoga tradition drew a line that Loftus and Palmer showed people cannot always draw from the inside.

Indian theories of knowledge make a similar demand. In the Nyaya school, for example, a memory is judged by the quality of the original experience that formed it (Phillips and Vaidya, 2024). Testimony is judged by the knowledge of the person giving it. After the word *smashed*, the original experience and the questioner's words are mixed, and that is exactly the problem.

**A court's decolonial move, and its limits.** In *Bharwada Bhoginbhai Hirjibhai v. State of Gujarat* (1983), the Supreme Court of India refused to adopt a Western rule requiring corroboration of a rape victim's testimony. It wrote: "We must not be swept off the feet by the approach made in the Western World which has its own social mileu" (spelling as in the judgment). It saw no need "to import the said concept on a turn-key basis". This is a rare judicial attempt to reason from Indian conditions. But, as the [critical lens](/cases/car-crash/lenses/critical/) page shows, the Court's picture of those conditions rested on claims about "a girl or a woman in India", not on evidence. Replacing a Western assumption with an unexamined local one is not the same as building knowledge from Indian lives.

## What this lens values in the study

The study gives a precise, testable form to an insight that Stephen in 1872 and the Yoga commentators long before him had already stated: what people report as memory can include what they were told. That shows the finding is not a purely Western possession, and it gives Indian scholars a bridge between traditions. The study's method is also cheap and simple enough to be run by researchers anywhere, with their own films, languages and questions.

## What it would question

- Teaching the results of 195 students in one American study as facts about "a witness" everywhere.
- A law of evidence written for colonial courts and kept almost unchanged after independence and again in 2023.
- The absence of Indian traditions of memory and testimony from psychology textbooks, when they speak directly to this problem.
- Research questions set in the United States, about American roads and American courts, and then applied to Indian ones.
- Who designs and interprets eyewitness research in India, and whether Indian police, lawyers and witnesses have any say.

## Evidence from this tradition

**Indian research on suggestion and memory.** The Indian evidence base is small, and no Indian replication of the speed-verb design was found for this guide. The nearest studies are these. Sondhi and Gupta (2007), at the University of Delhi, misled 36 participants after a live event and found that "the suggested memories were as confidently held as accurate memories". A study at Banaras Hindu University with 120 participants found that misled participants "retained less number of details", and that people in angry or tense moods were affected more (Tiwari, 2011). A study at Pondicherry University found that changing the adjectives in questions changed people's judgements of time (Srinivasan and Ganth, 2017, abstract only). And 40 students in Bengaluru took part in a ten-country study of co-witness contamination, which found the effect in every country (Ito and colleagues, 2019).

**Global South research on witnesses.** Studies in Ghana by Anakwah and colleagues (2020, 2024) found that witnesses recalled central details better in settings familiar to them. They also found that participants from a collectivistic culture accepted more misleading items on a recognition test. Research built in and for a non-Western setting can find differences that a Western sample would never show.

**WEIRD samples.** Henrich and colleagues (2010) and Arnett (2008) document how narrow the samples of mainstream psychology are.

**Evidence against a strongly decolonial reading.** The ten-country co-witness study found the effect in India as well as elsewhere (Ito and colleagues, 2019). Indian courts have also used memory science on their own terms since the 1980s. The basic finding is not simply a Western artefact. The decolonial lens is strongest when it asks who designs, runs and interprets research, and whose concepts frame it, not when it denies the findings.

<Note title="A caution on Indian concepts">
Indian philosophy also discusses <Term>pratyabhijna</Term>, usually translated as recognition, for example recognising "This is that Devadatta I saw yesterday" (Phillips and Vaidya, 2024). It is tempting to present this as an Indian theory of eyewitness memory or of misinformation. It is not. In the sources checked for this guide, it belongs to debates in the theory of knowledge, not to questions about how witnesses are misled. Use Indian concepts carefully, with their own meanings, and name the translation you rely on.
</Note>

## A study this lens would design

1. Form a research team led by Indian psychologists, with police officers, lawyers and community members as partners from the start.
2. Ask the partners which problems of witness memory matter most in their work, for example statements written by police, translation between languages, or witnesses who testify years later.
3. Film events typical of Indian streets and villages, and question witnesses in their own languages.
4. Include the distinction drawn by Stephen and the Yoga commentators: after each answer, ask the witness whether they saw the detail themselves, heard it from someone else, or are inferring it.
5. Study community fact-finding, such as a village meeting that pieces together an event, as a different model of testimony, not only as a source of error.
6. Publish the results in Indian languages and share them with the partners before publication.

**Aim:** knowledge about witness memory that is built from Indian problems and concepts, tested in Indian settings, and useful to Indian courts, rather than imported and translated.

**Prediction:** asked whether they saw, heard or inferred each detail, witnesses will mark some of their details as heard or inferred, and those details will be wrong more often than the ones they mark as seen.

The supervisor means that the plan copies the American procedure without asking whether its question fits India. Translating word for word assumes that the English verbs have Hindi equivalents with the same force. Using American films and miles per hour assumes that American roads and units can stand for Indian ones. The plan treats the 1974 study as the universal template and India as a place to test it. That is the pattern the decolonial lens criticises: questions and methods set in the West, with Indian participants supplying data.

A redesign would start from Indian problems. The student could ask local police or lawyers when wording actually matters in their work, for example when a statement is recorded in one language and read in court in another. She could film a typical Indian collision, choose Hindi verbs with native speakers, and use km/h. She could also draw on Indian concepts, such as the distinction between memory and an added "misconception" in the Yoga tradition, by asking witnesses whether each detail was seen, heard or inferred. The result would still test the same principle, but it would produce knowledge that belongs to its setting.
