## The tradition in brief

Existentialism is less a school than a family of writers who put one question first: what does it mean to exist as a being who has to choose? The French philosophers Jean-Paul Sartre (1905 to 1980) and Simone de Beauvoir (1908 to 1986) accepted the label. Two nineteenth-century writers are counted as forerunners: the Danish Christian thinker Søren Kierkegaard (1813 to 1855) and the German philosopher Friedrich Nietzsche (1844 to 1900). Albert Camus (1913 to 1960) denied being an existentialist, and the German philosopher Martin Heidegger (1889 to 1976) supplied key ideas without accepting the label. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy notes that only Sartre and Beauvoir called themselves existentialists.

Three ideas matter for this study.

**Existence precedes essence.** A knife is made to a design. A human being, Sartre argued, is not: "Man is nothing else but that which he makes of himself. That is the first principle of existentialism" (*Existentialism Is a Humanism*, 1946). He denied that temperament or upbringing decide a person. Of the coward he wrote that "he is like that because he has made himself into a coward by actions. There is no such thing as a cowardly temperament."

**Freedom in situation.** Sartre did not deny facts. Your body, your family, your country and the adults in your nursery are what he called <Term>facticity</Term>, and the whole set of facts you face is your <Term>situation</Term>. "I am absolutely free, and responsible for my situation. But in addition I am only ever free in situation" (*Being and Nothingness*, 1943, p. 663). Facts limit what is possible. They do not decide what a person makes of them.

**The serious world.** Beauvoir's name for treating values "as ready-made things", handed down rather than chosen, is the <Term>spirit of seriousness</Term> (*The Ethics of Ambiguity*, 1947, Part II). Her account of childhood, below, is the part of existentialist writing that speaks most directly to this study.

## What existentialists say about becoming who you are

**The child's world is serious, and adults are its gods.** Beauvoir wrote the clearest existentialist account of childhood. "The child's situation is characterized by his finding himself cast into a universe which he has not helped to establish, which has been fashioned without him, and which appears to him as an absolute to which he can only submit. In his eyes, human inventions, words, customs, and values are given facts, as inevitable as the sky and the trees" (*The Ethics of Ambiguity*, Part II). The child "believes in the being of his parents and teachers. He takes them for the divinities which they vainly try to be". And: "Rewards, punishments, prizes, words of praise or blame instill in him the conviction that there exist a good and an evil which like a sun and a moon exist as ends in themselves." The child "plays at being, at being a saint, a hero, a guttersnipe". In plain words: for a small child, what adults do is not one option among others. It is the shape of the world. Beauvoir does not treat this as a failing. "Normally the child escapes the anguish of freedom." Only at adolescence, when "Men stop appearing as if they were gods", does the young person face choosing for themselves.

**Nobody is born a tough boy.** In *The Second Sex* Beauvoir argued that "One is not born, but rather becomes, woman", and that the same is true of the tough little man. The boy is told "A man doesn't ask for kisses ... A man doesn't cry", and "the pride of his virility is breathed into him". Through "games, sports, fights, challenges, and exploits" he "learns the severe lessons of violence; he learns to take blows, to deride pain, to hold back tears from the earliest age". The girl is given a doll, told to be "pretty as a picture", and "is forbidden to fight". Yet "if she were encouraged, she could show the same vibrant exuberance, the same curiosity, the same spirit of initiative, and the same intrepidness as the boy." Beauvoir also noticed that the girl with her doll "sometimes even tears off her arms, beats her, tortures her". Girls are not without aggression. They are taught where to put it, and to be ashamed of it.

**The copy and the number.** Kierkegaard, writing under a pen name as a Christian, described a self not as a thing but as a task: "The self is a relation that relates itself to itself" (*The Sickness unto Death*, p. 13). One way to fail at the task is to dissolve into other people. A person "finds it too hazardous to be himself and far easier and safer to be like the others, to become a copy, a number, a mass man" (p. 34). This failure, he added, "goes practically unnoticed in the world", because the copy gets on so well in "business and social life".

**The herd and the "anyone".** Nietzsche thought obedience had been bred into people for so long "that the need for it is now innate in the average man", a need that "accepts whatever is shouted into its ears by someone who issues commands – parents, teachers, laws, class prejudices, public opinions" (*Beyond Good and Evil*, section 199). He called this "the herd instinct of obedience". Heidegger's idea is related but less hostile. According to the Stanford Encyclopedia's account, his "anyone" (in German *das Man*) is the anonymous standard of what "one does", and living as an "anyone-self" "disburdens us of the responsibility of choice". What these writers share is a worry that most people live as copies of a norm rather than as themselves.

## Reading the study through this tradition

**Freedom in situation, not transmission.** The paper's title speaks of the "transmission of aggression", and the [paradigm page](/cases/bobo-doll/paradigm/) shows that the study treats children as shaped mainly by their environment. Sartre would accept most of the facts and reject the vocabulary. A model's example is part of the child's situation, something the child takes up and makes something of. It cannot "produce" a punch the way heat produces steam. On two points Sartre and Bandura agree: neither believes in an inner aggressive drive, and neither explains acts by temperament. Where they part is the question the paper never asks: what did the hitting mean to the child who did it?

**Why no reward was needed.** The paper's puzzle, that children copied without any reward to the model or to themselves, is no puzzle for Beauvoir. In the serious world an adult does not need to reward an act for it to count as allowed. An adult doing it, in the nursery, under the nursery's authority, already makes it part of what is done. The paper's own evidence fits her picture. Some children said of the woman model, "That's not the way for a lady to behave" (p. 581). These are Beauvoir's children treating values "as ready-made things". She would also ask who built the situation: three adults staging a scene for a four-year-old under what she called "the sovereign eyes of grown-up persons". Is a university nursery an environment, or a small kingdom of gods?

**Becoming gendered: the same mechanism, a different stance.** The paper explains sex differences by what parents reward (p. 575). That is Beauvoir's thesis in behaviourist words. But the paper takes the "masculine-typed" character of aggression as a background fact and predicts from it. Beauvoir takes it as a made arrangement, and she would see the study as one more occasion on which adults showed girls what ladies do. Her girls who tear the arms off their dolls suggest a different reading of the girls' lower physical scores: not less aggression, but aggression that has been taught to be ashamed of itself.

**The one-way mirror and the Look.** For Sartre, being seen changes what an act is, and he called the experience of being seen by another person the Look. "Shame in its primary structure is shame before somebody" (*Being and Nothingness*, p. 308). A person absorbed in peering through a keyhole simply acts, until "somebody was there, and has seen me", and then "I am ashamed of myself as I appear to the Other". He also noted "the method of education that consists in making children 'ashamed' of what they are" (p. 309). The study's design works only if the child does not feel looked at. The mirror removes the Other's eyes while keeping the Other's judgement, and the experimenter at the desk looks without looking. Sartre would say the data record a child who believes himself unseen, which is one particular human situation, not "behaviour" in general. The paper's own remark that "many subjects were under the impression that they were no longer on the nursery school grounds" (p. 576) shows how carefully that situation was built.

**A copy in ten minutes.** The children reproduced the model's exact sequence and exact words, including the harmless phrase "He sure is a tough fella". Kierkegaard, Nietzsche and Heidegger would not dispute the result. They would dispute the word "learning". For them, copying an adult's violence is the beginning of an anyone-self, not the acquisition of a skill. The study is an unusually clean demonstration of the thing they feared: a person becoming a copy in ten minutes, with no reward, because an adult did it first. What would interest them most is what the analysis sets aside: the two children in the aggressive condition who did not copy, and the children who copied while judging, saying the lady was not behaving as a lady should. Those children were doing something more than reproducing.

**Quiet is not the absence of aggression.** Every child was frustrated by having the best toys taken away. The paper argues against the catharsis idea, and finds that children who watched the calm male model were less aggressive and sat quietly for longer. Nietzsche agrees with the study against catharsis, since for him release does not drain a drive: "A living thing seeks above all to discharge its strength" (*Beyond Good and Evil*, section 13). But he adds a prediction the study could not test. "All instincts that do not discharge themselves outwardly turn inward – this is what I call the internalization of man" (*On the Genealogy of Morals*, essay 2, section 16). On this reading the quiet children were not less aggressive but children whose frustration had nowhere to go. A method that counts only outward acts in five-second intervals cannot see inward turning. Nietzsche's claim is a story about the history of civilisation, not a theory of childhood, so treat this as an analogy.

## Where it agrees, and where it clashes

The agreements are real. The existentialists, like the paper, reject an inner aggressive drive that must come out, reject explaining behaviour by temperament, and accept that what was observed was real and not a mask over something hidden. Sartre's claim that "feeling is formed by the deeds that one does" is close to the paper's claim that children acquire behaviour by performing it.

The clashes are about vocabulary and about what the design leaves out. The paper speaks of causes, transmission and production; the existentialists speak of situations that a person takes up. The paper records the child from behind a mirror; the existentialists insist that the observer is part of the situation and that there is no view from nowhere. The paper counts acts; the existentialists want to know what the act was for the child. And the paper has no place for refusal, whereas for the existentialists the children who did not copy, or who copied while judging, are where the human being shows itself.

## Questions it would put to the researchers

- What did the hitting mean to the child who did it: play, obedience, a test of what was allowed, or anger at the toys taken away? Nobody asked, and the design left no room to answer.
- Where in your design is there room for a child who refuses? Two did, and the analysis has nothing to say about them.
- You write that aggression is "masculine-typed" and then predict from it. Did you consider that your experiment was one more adult telling girls what ladies do?
- Your observers sat behind a mirror and your experimenter sat at a desk pretending not to watch. What is the status of behaviour recorded from a child who believes himself unseen?
- The calm-model children sat quietly. Is quiet the absence of aggression, or aggression turned inward? How would your method tell the difference?
- In whose world did the adult's acts count as permitted: the child's, or the nursery's?

## Cautions

- **The label is loose.** Kierkegaard wrote as a Christian, and his task of becoming a self is a task before God. Sartre argued from the premise that God does not exist. Camus denied the name, and Heidegger did not accept it. Say which writer you mean.
- **Nietzsche's herd is a polemic.** His contempt for ordinary people and for democracy is part of a wider position that this guide does not endorse. Use his observation about obedience, not his judgement of the obedient.
- **These are not developmental theories.** Beauvoir's account of childhood is philosophical description. Nietzsche's internalisation belongs to a genealogy of morality, a history of how moral ideas came to be. Neither studied children. Present them as readings, not as rival findings.
- **Sartre did not deny upbringing.** He denied that it decides. His later work, the *Critique of Dialectical Reason* (1960), gave social conditions a larger role; the Stanford Encyclopedia describes the shift as a move to "freedom-in-situation", which *Being and Nothingness* already states.
- **Do not claim the existentialists would reject the data.** Their quarrel is with the language of causes and with what the design left out.

A design that records only acts cannot tell a child who copies a game from a child who copies because he is afraid, or a child who refuses from a child who was simply not interested. Beauvoir would add a caution: neither child has yet reached the freedom she describes, because both still live among adults who are "like gods". The question for the researcher is not whether the children were free, but what the adults around them made it possible to do.
