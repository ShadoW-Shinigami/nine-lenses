## What you care about

You spend your day with 30 or 40 children. You care about how they treat each other: pushing in the queue, grabbing pencils, name-calling, fights at break time. You also know that children watch you all day. Your own tone, your reactions to misbehaviour and the way you settle disputes are models too.

## What worries you about the original study

- **It is far from the classroom.** One child, alone with an adult, in a strange building, playing with a toy built to be hit. Your classroom has peers, rules, friendships and rivalries.
- **The model was a stranger.** In school, the most important models are classmates, older pupils and teachers, people the children see every day.
- **It lasted 20 minutes.** You need to know what builds up or fades over a term.
- **It measured only aggression.** You are equally interested in sharing, helping and calming down.

## Your redesign

1. **Run it in real classrooms.** Choose several nursery or primary classes. Randomly assign classes, not individual children, to conditions. This is a <Term>cluster randomised</Term> design.
2. **Use real models.** In some classes, teachers are trained to model calm conflict resolution aloud ("I feel cross, so I'm going to take a breath and ask nicely"). In others, teaching continues as usual.
3. **Measure peer behaviour over weeks.** Trained observers who do not know the class's condition record aggression and kindness between children at regular times, before and for several weeks after the change.
4. **Include the teacher's own discipline.** Record whether teachers shout, threaten or use physical punishment, since these are models too.
5. **Involve teachers in interpreting results.** They know which behaviours are play-fighting and which are hurtful.

## Why

A teacher's question is about everyday behaviour, so the study needs <Term>ecological validity</Term>: results that apply to real settings. A field experiment keeps random assignment, so it can still show causes, but it measures behaviour that matters to schools.

The teacher's own behaviour is especially important in India, where a government study found that 65% of school-going children reported corporal punishment at school. If children learn from adult models, a teacher who hits is teaching that hitting solves problems. Indian law and child-rights guidance now ban such punishment in schools, using exactly this social learning argument (see the [India page](/cases/bobo-doll/india/)). Note that this redesign is a prevention study rather than a replication: it tests whether calm modelling by teachers reduces aggression, not whether aggressive models increase it.

## What your redesign gives up

- **Less control.** Classrooms differ in many ways, so differences between classes may come from things other than the teacher's modelling. Randomising many classes helps, but needs a large study.
- **Teachers know their condition.** A teacher trained to model calm behaviour may also change in other ways, such as giving more attention, so the cause is less precise.
- **No clear test of copying.** Without unusual, countable acts like the Bobo model's, it is harder to show that children are copying a specific model rather than responding to a calmer classroom in general.
