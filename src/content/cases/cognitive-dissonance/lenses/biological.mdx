## The lens in brief

The biological perspective explains behaviour through the body: the brain and nervous system, hormones, genes and evolutionary history. Its roots include Charles Darwin's theory of evolution, Donald Hebb's work linking brain activity to thought, and Roger Sperry's studies of the two halves of the brain.

Three terms matter for this case. <Term>Physiological arousal</Term> is the body's state of activation: a faster heart, sweatier skin, readiness to act. It is controlled largely by the <Term>sympathetic nervous system</Term>, the part of the nervous system that prepares the body for action. Arousal can be measured by <Term>skin conductance</Term>, also called galvanic skin response, which rises when the skin sweats slightly. The <Term>anterior cingulate cortex</Term> is an area deep in the front of the brain that becomes active when a person faces conflict between competing responses.

This lens is distant from the study's methods, which used no physical measures, but closer to its theory. Festinger described dissonance as an unpleasant state that motivates people as hunger does. If so, it should be visible in the body.

## How this lens reads the study

**A drive that was never measured.** The whole explanation rests on the claim that the $1 students felt more tension than the $20 students. Nobody measured it. There were no heart rates, no skin measures and not even a question about how uncomfortable the students felt. The only evidence for the tension is the change in opinion it is supposed to cause. Hock writes that it is "safe to assume" that arousal was present, but that is an assumption, not a finding. A biological psychologist would see this as the study's biggest gap.

**Money as an indirect way to change a bodily state.** The study varied money in order to vary dissonance. Money affects many things at once: excitement, suspicion, pleasure, embarrassment. The $20 offer, worth about $240 today, may itself have been arousing. A biological study would measure or change the state directly rather than infer it.

**A body under mild stress, measured once.** Every student spent an hour on monotonous tasks while being watched and timed (pp. 204-205). The paid students then had to talk a doubtful stranger round, face to face, after an awkward request. These are the kinds of situation that raise arousal, and arousal rises and falls within minutes. The interview came only a few minutes after the conversation. A biological lens would ask whether the change in opinion would last once the arousal had gone, which a single measurement cannot answer.

## What this lens values in the study

It values the clear, controlled manipulation. The 1959 logic, a weak external reason against a strong one, was carried almost unchanged into brain-scanning studies fifty years later. It also values Festinger's idea that dissonance is a motivational state, because a motivational state is the kind of thing biology can study.

## What it would question

- The use of an unmeasured internal state to explain the results.
- The assumption that money changes dissonance and nothing else in the body.
- The absence of any measure of how long the effect lasts.
- A sample of young, healthy men only, which says nothing about age, sex or health differences in the body's response to stress.

## Evidence from this tradition

**Arousal measured directly.** Robert Croyle and Joel Cooper (1983) had students write essays against their own views under conditions of high or low choice while their skin conductance was recorded. Students who freely chose to write the essay showed more signs of arousal than the other groups. In this experiment their attitudes did not change, and the authors suggested that the students blamed their unease on the recording equipment attached to them. Elkin and Leippe (1986) later found both raised skin conductance and attitude change after counter-attitudinal behaviour, and the arousal did not fall once attitudes had changed.

**Drugs change the result.** If arousal drives attitude change, then changing arousal with a drug should change attitude change. Cooper, Zanna and Taves (1978) gave students a pill before they wrote an essay supporting a view they opposed. All were told the pill was a placebo, but some received a sedative, some an amphetamine, which increases arousal, and some a true placebo. When students had been given a free choice about writing the essay, the sedative reduced their attitude change and the amphetamine increased it (as described by Croyle & Cooper, 1983). Steele, Southwick and Critchlow (1981) found that letting students drink alcohol after a counter-attitudinal essay also reduced attitude change. Arousal, or the discomfort that comes with it, seems to be part of the mechanism.

**Discomfort as well as arousal.** Losch and Cacioppo (1990) found evidence that people change their attitudes to reduce the unpleasant feeling, not the arousal itself. Elliot and Devine (1994) measured self-reported discomfort and found that it rose after counter-attitudinal behaviour and fell after attitude change. So the bodily state matters, but the feeling of unpleasantness may matter most.

**The brain during a counter-attitudinal act.** Vincent van Veen and colleagues (2009) put the 1959 idea inside a brain scanner. The inside of an MRI scanner is noisy and uncomfortable. Participants were asked to answer questions as though they were enjoying it, supposedly to reassure a nervous patient who, they were told, was watching their answers from the control room. A comparison group was told instead that they would be paid an extra $1 for each such answer: an outside reason, exactly as in 1959. Activity in the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex and the anterior insula while participants made these statements predicted how much their attitudes later changed. Eddie Harmon-Jones and colleagues, using EEG, which records the brain's electrical activity through the scalp, have linked dissonance reduction to activity in the left frontal cortex, which is associated with getting ready to act. They proposed an <Term>action-based model</Term>: dissonance exists because inconsistent thoughts get in the way of effective action. Keise Izuma and colleagues (2010) found brain activity that tracked how preferences changed after people made choices.

**Monkeys and children.** Louisa Egan, Laurie Santos and Paul Bloom (2007) tested four-year-old children and capuchin monkeys with a choice method. After choosing between two equally liked items, both children and monkeys later avoided the item they had rejected, as adults do in dissonance studies. The authors argued that dissonance reduction may be an old evolved process that does not need language or a complex sense of self.

**Evidence against.** M. Keith Chen and Jane Risen (2010) showed that the standard choice method can produce this pattern even without any dissonance, because a person's choice reveals preferences that their first ratings measured imperfectly. This weakens the monkey and child findings and some brain studies that used the same method. When later studies corrected for the problem, a real but small effect of choice on preference remained (Izuma & Murayama, 2013). Brain-imaging studies also show where activity happens alongside dissonance, not that this activity causes attitude change. And none of this research tells us whether the 1959 students felt aroused. It shows only that people in similar situations often do.

## A study this lens would design

1. Run the boring tasks and the $1, $20 and control conditions as in 1959, and add two groups who are paid $1 or $20 but are not asked to say anything.
2. Record heart rate and skin conductance continuously, from arrival to the end of the interview.
3. Ask every participant to rate how uncomfortable they feel, both just after the conversation and just after the interview.
4. Give half of each group a reason for any arousal, for example by telling them that the room's lighting makes some people feel tense.
5. Repeat the opinion rating a week later.

**Prediction:** arousal and discomfort will rise most in the $1 group during and after the lie, and will fall once they rate the tasks positively. Participants given another explanation for their arousal will change their opinion less. The groups paid without lying will show no rise.

The group pattern fits the arousal account. Heart rate rising during the lie and falling after the positive rating is what you would expect if the lie created an unpleasant aroused state and changing the opinion reduced it. It matches Cooper, Zanna and Taves (1978), who found that a tranquilliser reduced attitude change and a stimulant increased it.

But the single student is a case, not evidence. He was not assigned to take the tablet, so he may differ from the others in many ways, such as anxiety or attitudes to tasks. The tablet may also affect attention or mood, not only arousal. To test the idea properly, researchers would need to assign the drug or placebo at random, as Cooper and colleagues did, and to measure discomfort as well as heart rate, since later work suggests the unpleasant feeling matters more than arousal alone.
