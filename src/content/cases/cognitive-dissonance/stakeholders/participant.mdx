## What you care about

Imagine being one of the 71 students. You are taking your first psychology course, and you signed up for a two-hour study on "Measures of Performance" because the course required it. You care about being told the truth, being free to say no, not being made to look foolish, and not being used to harm someone else. You also care about what happens to the recording of your voice and the record of what you did.

## What worries you about the original study

- **You were misled before you arrived.** Weeks earlier your instructor told the class about a department study of experiments and urged everyone to be completely honest in its interviews (p. 204). You trusted that announcement. It was part of the experiment.
- **You were bored on purpose.** The hour of spools and pegs was designed to give you "an experience about which he would have a somewhat negative opinion" (p. 205). The experimenter's stopwatch and notes were acting.
- **You were asked to lie to a fellow student.** The experimenter, sounding embarrassed, asked you to tell a waiting student that the tasks were fun. When she said a friend had found it boring, you probably told her "Oh, no, it's really very interesting." You believed you were misleading a real person.
- **You were recorded without knowing.** A hidden tape recorder captured your conversation (p. 206). The paper does not say that you were asked afterwards whether it could be kept.
- **It was hard to say no.** The request came from a man in authority, in the middle of a study you had to take part in for your course. If you hesitated, he added reasons such as "It will only take a few minutes" (p. 205).
- **The money was taken back.** You were paid, signed a receipt, and were then asked to return the money at the end. The paper says everyone was "quite willing" (p. 207). It is hard to refuse in that room.

And spare a thought for the young woman who played the waiting participant. She was hired, but she spent session after session being told things by men who believed she was a stranger they could mislead.

## Your redesign

1. **Warn people in advance that something will be hidden.** The consent form says: "Some details of this study will not be explained until the end. Nothing will cause you harm, and everything will be explained." This is called <Term>authorised deception</Term>. You can then decide whether to take part on those terms.
2. **Offer a real alternative to taking part.** Students who do not want to join any experiment can meet the course requirement with a short written assignment instead.
3. **Ask permission for the recording at the end.** Tell each student about the tape during debriefing and delete it if they ask.
4. **Make refusing easy.** The experimenter says clearly: "You can say no, and it makes no difference to your course."
5. **Let people keep the money.** Asking for it back turns payment into a test of goodwill.
6. **Debrief about the person, not only the procedure.** Explain that most people agree to such a request, that agreeing says more about the situation than about their honesty, and that the "waiting student" was not misled because she was part of the study.
7. **Let people withdraw their data** after they hear the truth.

## Why

Modern ethics codes say much of this directly. The American Psychological Association's code allows deception only when the study has real value and no non-deceptive method would work (Standard 8.07), requires debriefing (8.08), asks for consent before recording voices except in deception studies, where consent to use the recording is sought at debriefing (8.03), and requires a fair alternative when research is a course requirement (8.04). India's ICMR guidelines (2017) list students among people with "diminished autonomy due to dependency or being under a hierarchical system" (Box 6.2) and warn that such people "may not be in a position to disagree to participate for fear of authority" (Section 6.9.2).

There is also a quieter harm. A student who leaves thinking "I lied to a girl for a dollar" may feel ashamed. The debriefing should address that.

## What your redesign gives up

- **Some students will be on guard.** Warned that something is hidden, more of them may guess the purpose. Five suspicious students were already excluded in 1959, and more suspicion means a smaller usable sample.
- **An easy refusal means more refusals.** Three students refused in 1959. If more refuse, the people who comply are a more selected group, which makes the groups harder to compare.
- **Volunteers may differ from conscripts.** Students who choose the study over an assignment may be more cooperative than students in general.
