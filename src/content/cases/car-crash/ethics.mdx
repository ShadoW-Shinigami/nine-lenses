A design critique asks whether a study could answer its question. An ethics critique asks whether it should have been run as it was. For this study the answer is simpler than for many classics. The procedure itself carried little risk. Its one deception was mild, and an ethics committee today would very probably approve it with small changes. The harder ethical questions belong to what came after: false memories planted in later studies, and the use of this research in courtrooms. This page separates the two.

## Two yardsticks

<Term>Research ethics</Term> are the rules for how researchers must treat the people who take part in their studies. Judging a study from 1974 needs two yardsticks.

The first is the standard of its own time. The study was probably run in late 1973 or early 1974: the journal received the paper on 17 April 1974 (p. 589). Judging the study by the rules of that moment is fair to the researchers.

The second is the standard of today. A student in India who proposed this study now would need approval from an <Term>ethics committee</Term>, a group that reviews a study's risks and protections before it begins, applying the Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR) guidelines. A journal would expect the study to meet the American Psychological Association (APA) or British Psychological Society (BPS) codes. This yardstick tells you what you would have to change.

## What the paper reports, and what it does not

The paper is five pages long and has no ethics section. Most of what an ethics committee would ask about is simply not reported.

| Question | What the paper says | Where |
|---|---|---|
| Who took part, and how they were recruited | "Forty-five students" and "One hundred and fifty students", tested "in groups of various sizes". Nothing on recruitment, payment or course credit. | pp. 586-587 |
| What participants were told | Not reported. The study was about the wording of one question, and the design needs that purpose to be kept from participants. | Not in the paper |
| The deception itself | Each student read one version of the speed question, with *smashed*, *collided*, *bumped*, *hit* or *contacted*. Nobody was told that other students read other verbs. | pp. 586-587 |
| What the films showed | Short segments of driver's education films. Four were staged crashes, whose original purpose "was to illustrate what can happen to human beings when cars collide at various speeds". The paper does not say whether people or injuries were shown. | p. 586 |
| Length of the session | Experiment 1 "lasted about an hour and a half", with seven crash films. | p. 586 |
| A return visit | In Experiment 2, students "returned" a week later, so their answers from the two sessions had to be linked. How their names or answers were stored is not reported. | p. 587 |
| Consent, right to withdraw, debriefing, ethics review | Not reported. | Not in the paper |

Not reporting something is not proof that it did not happen. Short papers in 1974 rarely described consent or debriefing. The closest evidence about this laboratory's practice comes from a study published a year later by Loftus and Zanni (1975), using a similar film and the same grant. There, participants "were told that they were participating in an experiment on memory and that they would be shown a short film followed by a questionnaire. The content of the film was not mentioned" (p. 87). That tells us something about the lab's habits, but it cannot be assumed for this study.

**The deception was mild.** Researchers distinguish between <Term>active deception</Term>, telling participants something false, and <Term>withholding</Term>, not telling them everything. This study withheld the purpose. Its only false element was the verb, and Loftus herself later argued that the phrase "smashed into each other" "may be additional information, but it can hardly be said to be false information" (Loftus, 1975, p. 565). That defence is weaker for Experiment 2 if, as seems likely, its film showed a slow, bumper-to-bumper chain collision (see the [method page](/cases/car-crash/method/)). Then "smashed" overstated the event. Even so, it was a single suggestive word, not a false story.

**The films could upset some viewers.** Driver's education films of the period were often made to shock new drivers into caution. Four of these were staged to show "what can happen to human beings" in a crash (p. 586). A student who had lost a family member in a road accident might have found seven of them in a row distressing. No warning or screening is reported.

**Some students left with a false belief.** In Experiment 2, 16 students in the *smashed* group, and 13 in the other two groups, reported a detail that was not there. Whether anyone told them so is not reported. Modern research on this question is reassuring but not complete. A review found that "fewer than 30% of misinformation papers report any debriefing at all", and that false memories created in experiments "often persist after debriefing" (Greene and colleagues, 2023). A follow-up of 630 participants, six months after a study that had shown them fabricated news stories, however, "did not find any negative long-term effects" (Murphy and colleagues, 2020). A false belief about broken glass in a film is about as harmless as a false memory can be.

## The standards of the time

The APA published its first research-specific principles, *Ethical Principles in the Conduct of Research with Human Participants*, in 1973. They asked researchers to tell participants about the study, and to take extra care when full disclosure was not possible. Once the data were collected, researchers were to provide "information about the nature of the study" and to "remove any misconceptions" (Principle H, as quoted in later editions). They were a professional code with no legal force.

Legal rules arrived in the same months as this paper, but after the study was done. The United States Department of Health, Education, and Welfare published its human-subjects regulations on 30 May 1974. The National Research Act, which required institutions receiving certain federal grants to set up review boards, became law on 12 July 1974. Both came after the paper was submitted in April. Both applied to research funded through health programmes, and this study was funded by the Department of Transportation. So no law required this study to be reviewed. Whether the University of Washington reviewed it anyway is not known.

Deception was common in American psychology at the time. A review of the leading social psychology journal found that by 1969 about two-thirds of its studies used it (Sieber, Iannuzzo and Rodriguez, 1995). Next to the heavy deceptions of that period, such as Milgram's obedience studies (1963), withholding the purpose of a memory questionnaire was a very small one. By the standard of 1974 the study was entirely ordinary.

## What the codes require today

Three codes matter for a student in India. The ICMR's *National Ethical Guidelines for Biomedical and Health Research Involving Human Participants* (2017) are the rules an Indian ethics committee applies, and they cover behavioural research. The APA's *Ethical Principles of Psychologists and Code of Conduct* (2017 amendments) and the BPS *Code of Human Research Ethics* (2021) are the codes most journals and textbooks refer to.

| Requirement today | What the codes say | The 1974 study |
|---|---|---|
| Review before the study starts | ICMR Table 4.2 sends "studies involving deception of participants" to full committee review, and section 9.2.9 says research "employing any type of deception should undergo full committee review". | None reported; none was required by law. In India today it would need full review, even though its risk is minimal. |
| Informed consent | <Term>Informed consent</Term> means agreeing to take part after being told what will happen, including any risks, and knowing one can refuse. APA 8.02 lists what participants must be told, including "their right to decline to participate and to withdraw". | Not reported. |
| Withholding the hypothesis | BPS section 7: "There is a difference between withholding some of the details of the hypothesis under test and deliberately falsely informing the participants of the purpose of the research". Where full disclosure would spoil the design, the withholding should be stated in the protocol the committee reviews. ICMR 5.11.2 allows approval "in circumstances where some information requires to be withheld for validation until the completion of the research". | The purpose was withheld. This is the mildest kind of deception the codes describe. |
| Deception only when justified | APA 8.07(a): only when "justified by the study's significant prospective scientific, educational, or applied value" and "effective nondeceptive alternative procedures are not feasible". 8.07(b): never about research expected to cause "physical pain or severe emotional distress". | Easily met. A student who knew the wording was being tested would not answer naturally, and the risk is small. |
| Low risk, with warning of upsetting content | BPS section 2.4: the risk of harm "should be no greater than that encountered in ordinary life". APA 8.02 asks researchers to tell participants about "potential risks, discomfort, or adverse effects". | Crash films could upset some viewers. Today a committee would want a content warning and an easy way to stop watching. |
| No pressure through grades | APA 8.04(b): when participation is a course requirement or earns extra credit, students must be offered "equitable alternative activities". | Not reported. |
| Debriefing | <Term>Debriefing</Term> means explaining the study afterwards and correcting any false beliefs. APA 8.07(c) requires deception to be explained "as early as is feasible" and lets participants withdraw their data; 8.08 requires "reasonable steps to correct any misconceptions". BPS section 10 and ICMR 5.11.1 and 5.11.3 say the same. | Not reported. Today it would be required, at the end of the second session in Experiment 2, explaining the verb and that there was no broken glass. |

Measured against these codes, the study has no serious failure. It has gaps: no reported consent, warning, alternative to course credit or debriefing. Each would be easy to fill without changing the design.

## What critics and defenders have said

**About this study.** This guide found no published criticism of the ethics of the 1974 study itself. Its critics have attacked its methods and its conclusions, as the [strengths and limitations page](/cases/car-crash/strengths-limitations/) shows, not its treatment of participants.

**About the research programme it started.** The ethical debate came with later studies that went much further. In the "lost in the mall" studies of the 1990s, researchers worked with family members to persuade adults that they had been lost in a shopping mall as children, an event that never happened. L. S. Crook and M. C. Dean (1999) called the first of these studies "a breach of professional ethics", alleging weak supervision and a rush to publicise pilot results. Loftus (1999) replied that their essay was "partisan" and full of "errors, exaggerations, and omissions". In a separate dispute, Loftus and a colleague investigated a published case of a woman who said she had recovered a memory of childhood abuse. The woman complained to the University of Washington, which seized Loftus's files in 1999. After an inquiry of more than 21 months, the university cleared her of misconduct (Tavris, 2002, an account by a defender of Loftus). One of the original case authors, Erna Olafson (2014), later published a detailed list of what she called errors in Loftus's articles on the case. The woman later sued; in *Taus v. Loftus* (2007) the California Supreme Court dismissed most of her claims but allowed one to go forward.

**Defenders** point out that misinformation studies are among the safest in psychology. The distortions are small, usually about trivial details, and debriefing appears to leave no lasting harm (Murphy and colleagues, 2020). They argue that the knowledge gained, about how easily honest witnesses can be misled, protects innocent people from wrongful conviction.

## The ethics of how the findings were used

**For the defence in court.** Loftus became one of the best-known expert witnesses on memory in the United States, mostly called by the defence to explain to juries how memory can be distorted. In 2020 she was a defence expert in the trial of the film producer Harvey Weinstein, and in 2021 in the trial of Ghislaine Maxwell, both cases involving sexual abuse. Critics argue that general laboratory findings about broken glass and barns are being used to cast doubt on particular victims. Some memory researchers made a related scientific objection early on: Michael McCloskey and Maria Zaragoza (1985) wrote that "there is insufficient scientific basis for expert testimony to the effect that postevent information may alter a witness's memory". Supporters reply that juries tend to trust confident witnesses too much, and that explaining how memory works helps prevent wrongful convictions. The [importance page](/cases/car-crash/importance/) gives the evidence on wrongful convictions.

**In the "memory wars".** In the 1990s, many adults in the United States reported recovering memories of childhood sexual abuse, often during therapy, and many parents denied the accusations. Loftus's research became one of the main scientific arguments that some such memories could be created by suggestion. The False Memory Syndrome Foundation, founded in 1992 by Pamela and Peter Freyd after their adult daughter, the psychologist Jennifer Freyd, accused her father of abuse, drew on research on false memory, including Loftus's, to support accused parents. Critics, especially clinicians who work with trauma, argue that the research was used to discredit genuine survivors. The [psychoanalytic](/cases/car-crash/lenses/psychoanalytic/) and [critical](/cases/car-crash/lenses/critical/) lens pages take this debate further.

**For reform.** The same findings have been used to change how police interview witnesses, so that questions are open and do not suggest answers. Here the research protects witnesses and suspects alike. The importance page describes these reforms.

The ethical weight of this study, then, lies less in what happened to 195 students in Seattle than in what the knowledge was used for, and by whom.

## Would it be approved today?

Yes, very probably, as a minimal-risk study. In India it would go to full committee review because it involves withholding information, but the likely outcome would be approval. A committee would want:

1. An information sheet saying the study is about memory for short traffic films and that some details will be explained at the end.
2. A content note that the films show car crashes, with permission to look away or stop at any time, and no footage of real injuries.
3. A clear right to withdraw, including the right to withdraw one's answers after the debriefing.
4. An equal alternative if participation earns course credit.
5. A debriefing at the end of each student's last session, explaining that the verb was varied and that there was no broken glass, so that nobody leaves with a false belief.
6. Secure, coded storage for linking each student's answers across the two sessions in Experiment 2.

None of these changes would weaken the design. This is one of the few classic studies that could be run almost unchanged today.

<Note title="Was it unethical? A measured answer">
No, not in any serious sense. The study withheld its purpose, used one suggestive word and showed crash films, all of which would be approved today with a consent form, a content warning and a debriefing. The paper reports none of these, which is a gap in reporting typical of 1974, not evidence of harm. A good evaluation says this plainly, and then moves the ethical discussion to where it belongs: the later false-memory studies, and the use of memory research in court.
</Note>

The committee would question three things. First, the risk to participants: footage of real injured people is far more distressing than staged crashes, and BPS section 2.4 asks that the risk of harm be no greater than in ordinary life. A participant who has been in an accident, or lost someone in one, could be badly affected. Second, the people in the footage: they are real, may be identifiable, and never agreed to be shown to a class of psychology students, which raises privacy and dignity concerns. Third, deception: in India any withholding of the study's purpose sends the study to full committee review under ICMR Table 4.2, with a debriefing plan required.

The student could keep realism by using clips where no injuries or identifiable faces are visible, such as minor collisions filmed from a distance, or professionally staged footage. She should give a content warning, let participants stop at any time, and debrief everyone at the end, explaining the verb manipulation and correcting any false reports. The concepts at work are minimal risk, informed consent and debriefing.
