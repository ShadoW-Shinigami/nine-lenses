## The lens in brief

<Term>Decolonial psychology</Term> starts from history. Modern psychology grew mainly in Europe and North America, at a time when European powers ruled much of the world. Through universities, textbooks, journals and funding, this psychology spread globally and came to be treated as simply "psychology", the science of the human mind. Knowledge from elsewhere was often treated as culture, religion or folklore, or as raw data for Western theories.

In India, Durganand Sinha argued over several decades that psychology imported from the West often did not fit Indian lives. He described a first phase of "replicative research" in countries like India, followed by "disillusionment with western concepts and models" and a turn towards <Term>indigenisation</Term>: building psychology from local concepts and problems (Sinha, 1989). Ajit Dalal and Girishwar Misra (2010) have written on the core concepts of an Indian psychology rooted in Indian thought. Sunil Bhatia's *Decolonizing Psychology* (2018) studies how Western psychological ideas shape the identities of young people in India today.

This lens differs from the [cultural lens](/cases/cognitive-dissonance/lenses/cultural/). The cultural lens asks whether the finding holds elsewhere. The decolonial lens asks who gets to produce knowledge, whose experience becomes theory and whose becomes data, and why one local result is taught as a universal law.

## How this lens reads the study

**Indian experience at the start of the story.** One of the puzzles that led Festinger to the theory came from India. In 1935 the Indian psychologist Jamuna Prasad published a study of rumours that spread after the great Bihar earthquake of January 1934. In the foreword to his 1957 book, Festinger described the puzzle that these rumours posed: why would frightened people spread rumours predicting even worse disasters? His answer was that the rumours were not "anxiety provoking" but "anxiety justifying" (Festinger, 1957, foreword, as quoted by Harmon-Jones, 2000). The data came from Prasad's study, but the foreword does not name him (Stenner, 2025). So the theory has Indian roots, and an Indian psychologist collected the evidence it began from. Another Indian psychologist, D. Sinha (1952), published a study of reports and rumours after a later disaster.

**A social idea made individual.** The historian Eleonor Marcussen (2023) adds two points. First, Prasad himself argued that rumour is a social process, something that happens between people and in communities. His study became famous, but not for that argument: it became raw material for a theory about tension inside one person's mind. Second, Festinger described the rumours as spreading among people who felt the shock but suffered no damage. Marcussen shows that Patna, where Prasad began collecting rumours, had about 150 recorded deaths and many ruined buildings. The Indian account was simplified to fit the theory.

**But who appears as the knower?** In later retellings, the Indian researcher drops out. Hock's chapter says the theory "grew out of" rumours that spread "throughout India", and tells the story as Festinger's own insight, but does not name Prasad. Indian people appear as frightened rumour-spreaders whose behaviour needed explaining. The American appears as the theorist who explained it. The decolonial lens calls this pattern the division between the knower and the known: some people produce theory, others provide the material.

**From 60 young men to "people".** The test of the theory used 60 male students at one private American university. Like the [Bobo doll study](/cases/bobo-doll/lenses/decolonial/), it was soon taught around the world as a finding about human minds in general. Joseph Henrich, Steven Heine and Ara Norenzayan (2010) called the usual participants of psychology research <Term>WEIRD</Term>: from Western, Educated, Industrialised, Rich and Democratic societies. Drawing on Jeffrey Arnett (2008), they reported that 96% of participants in leading psychology journals came from countries with only 12% of the world's population.

**A local norm read as a law of the mind.** The paper says that a person who believes X would state X "at least in our culture" (p. 203). That rule, say what you believe, everywhere and to everyone, is a moral ideal of sincerity. The poet and scholar A. K. Ramanujan (1989) suggested that many Indian traditions think in a <Term>context-sensitive</Term> way: what is right depends on who you are, to whom you speak, where and when. Western modern thought, he argued, tends to prefer <Term>context-free</Term> rules that apply to everyone in the same way. In a context-sensitive framework, saying different things to different people in different settings is not automatically a contradiction to be repaired. Ramanujan presented these as broad tendencies found in both traditions, not as fixed national characters.

**Whose theory of action?** The theory measures reasons for acting in rewards: little money, much dissonance. Indian traditions offer other ways of thinking about why one acts. The Bhagavad Gita, for example, teaches action performed as duty without attachment to its results, often called <Term>nishkama karma</Term>. Would a person who understands a task as duty, not as an exchange, experience "insufficient justification" when the reward is small? Nobody has tested this. The decolonial point is that psychology has rarely asked, because it began from one tradition's picture of the person.

## What this lens values in the study

It values Festinger's engagement with an Indian study at all, which many Western researchers of the time would not have read, even though his foreword left its author unnamed. It values the paper's caveat, "at least in our culture", which leaves the door open to other ways of being a person. And it values the fact that the theory has since been tested in several cultures and found to work differently, because testing a claim of universality is exactly what this lens asks for.

## What it would question

- The use of Indian experience as raw data for a theory built and named elsewhere.
- The disappearance of the Indian researcher from textbook accounts.
- The claim to universality from a sample of 60 American men.
- The assumption that sincerity across all contexts is the human norm.
- The way the study enters Indian classrooms through American textbooks, as part of a canon of "studies that changed psychology".

## Evidence from this tradition

**The sampling problem.** Henrich and colleagues (2010) reviewed cross-cultural research on perception, fairness, reasoning and self-concepts, and concluded that WEIRD participants are often unusual, not typical, among humans. Arnett (2008) titled his paper "The neglected 95%", referring to the share of the world's people who live outside the United States.

**An Indian test of the idea of choice.** The research of Krishna Savani with Indian and American colleagues found that Americans were more likely than Indians to see ordinary actions as choices (Savani et al., 2010). Since the modern version of dissonance theory depends on feeling that you chose your act, this is direct evidence that one of the theory's building blocks is cultural. This research was done with Indian participants, by a team that included Indian researchers.

**Indian scholarship on its own terms.** Ramanujan's essay, Sinha's work on indigenisation, and Dalal and Misra's account of Indian psychology all offer concepts, such as context, duty and the relation between self and role, that could generate new questions about dissonance. We found few studies that have used them to do so.

**Evidence against the lens.** The decolonial lens should not assume that every Western finding is wrong for India. People everywhere seem to justify their actions in some way; the cross-cultural studies on the [cultural lens](/cases/cognitive-dissonance/lenses/cultural/) page found dissonance effects in Japanese and Asian Canadian participants once the right social conditions were present. And Indian psychology has never been a simple copy of the West: Prasad and Sinha were Indian academic psychologists who published their studies of Indian events in a British journal, using the methods of their discipline for their own questions. The lens asks for universality to be tested, and for local researchers to be credited, not for Western psychology to be rejected.

## A study this lens would design

1. Start with Indian concepts. Ask people in a particular community, for example a Marathi-speaking town, how they talk about saying something they do not mean, and when they think it is right, wrong or simply polite.
2. Build the study with local researchers, in the local language, using the categories people themselves use, such as duty, respect, harmony or personal gain.
3. Design an experiment from those categories: a statement made as a duty to a teacher, as a favour to a friend, or for money.
4. Measure both opinion change and how people themselves explain what they did.
5. Publish in Indian journals and in the local language as well as in English, name every local contributor, and return the findings to the community.

**Aim:** a theory of justification that starts from Indian ways of understanding action, and is then compared with Festinger's, rather than the other way round.

The decolonial lens asks who is treated as the knower and who as the known. Naming Prasad restores an Indian researcher as a producer of knowledge, not only as a source of data. The lens would also ask why a theory with Indian roots is taught back to Indian students as an American discovery, and what Indian ways of thinking about action and context could add to it.
