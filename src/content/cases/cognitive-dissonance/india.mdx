This page follows the questions in Section D, part 7, of the outline. It tries to be specific. "India" is not one culture: it includes cities and villages, many languages and religions, castes and classes, and families of every shape. Treat every statement below as a starting point for your own thinking, not as a fact about all Indians.

## A theory with an Indian beginning

Before asking whether the theory travels to India, notice that part of it came from India. On 15 January 1934 a great earthquake struck north India and Nepal. Bihar was the worst-hit region in India, and estimates of deaths in India range from about 7,000 to 20,000 (Marcussen, 2023). The psychologist Jamuna Prasad began collecting the rumours that spread afterwards, starting in Patna, through interviews and newspaper reports (Prasad, 1935). Many rumours predicted that even worse earthquakes were coming.

Two decades later, in the foreword to his 1957 book, Festinger said this puzzle was where his idea began. Why would frightened people spread frightening rumours? Perhaps, he suggested, the rumours were not "anxiety provoking" but "anxiety justifying": they gave people a reason for the fear they already felt. The foreword uses Prasad's data but does not name him (Stenner, 2025). The historian Eleonor Marcussen (2023) adds that Prasad had argued for a social view of rumour, as something that happens between people, and that this argument was lost when his data became evidence for a theory about one person's inner tension. She also shows that Festinger's description of the rumours, as spreading where people felt the shock but saw no damage, does not fit Patna, which had about 150 recorded deaths. Another Indian psychologist, D. Sinha (1952), studied reports and rumours after a later disaster. We could not check what that study found or how Festinger used it.

<SourceCheck title="Source check: Hock on the earthquake">
Hock says the theory "grew out of" rumours that spread "throughout India", and tells the story as Festinger's own insight. Three corrections. The rumours belonged to north India, especially Bihar, and Nepal, not all of India. The data came from an Indian psychologist, Jamuna Prasad, whom Hock does not name. And Festinger's biographer describes the theory as growing mainly out of his earlier work on group pressure and social comparison (Schachter, 1994), so the rumours were one starting point among several. A fair summary: "Festinger said the idea began with a puzzle in Jamuna Prasad's study of rumours after the 1934 Bihar-Nepal earthquake. It also grew out of his earlier work on group pressure and social comparison."

## What assumptions seem Western?

**The person as the author of their acts.** Dissonance in this study depends on the student feeling that *he* chose to tell the lie. A polite request, an offer he could refuse, a receipt he signed: everything was arranged so that the act would feel like his own choice. This builds on a particular picture of the person, common in the United States, as an independent individual whose actions express his own will. Cultural psychologists call this an <Term>independent</Term> view of the self, and contrast it with an <Term>interdependent</Term> view, in which a person is understood mainly through relationships, roles and duties (Markus & Kitayama, 1991).

**Words should match beliefs.** The theory assumes that saying something you do not believe creates a clash. The paper itself admits that this holds "at least in our culture" (p. 203). In many Indian settings, saying the expected, polite or role-appropriate thing may count as ordinary good manners, not a lie that needs justifying. We found no Indian research that measures how people judge such statements, so treat this as a question to test, not a fact.

**A request is a free choice.** The experimenter was a stranger of slightly higher status making a polite request. In a hierarchical setting, a request from a teacher, an elder or a boss is often heard as an instruction. If an act feels like a duty rather than a choice, the theory itself predicts little dissonance.

**Money as the main reason.** The study treats the payment as the student's main outside reason for lying. In India, the meaning of being paid by a teacher for a favour could be quite different: a polite token, an embarrassment, or an insult.

## What experiences may be missing?

**An invented scene, to think with.** The young man is telling his aunt the dish is wonderful. His cousin's smile suggests he does not think so. Is he lying, in the sense the 1959 study meant? Will he come to like the dish more because he praised it? The theory says that depends on whether he feels he freely chose to say it, or simply did what a nephew does.
</Figure>

**Role-appropriate speech.** Every day, people say what their role requires: a nephew praises his aunt's cooking, a junior agrees with a senior in a meeting, a student tells a visiting inspector that the college library is excellent. These statements are not always believed by the speaker, and nobody expects them to be. The 1959 study had no way to separate a lie from a courtesy.

**Many selves in many settings.** Many Indians move daily between languages, between home and college, between a joint family and a city workplace, and present themselves differently in each. The anthropologist and poet A. K. Ramanujan (1989) argued that much Indian thought is <Term>context-sensitive</Term>: what is right depends on who you are, where you are and whom you are with, rather than on a rule that holds in every context. If consistency across contexts is not expected, saying different things in different places may not feel inconsistent at all.

**Unequal power to refuse.** Three of the 71 Stanford students refused to be hired. For a student in an Indian college, refusing a professor's request in front of the professor could carry real costs. That changes both who would comply and what complying would mean.

## Would the findings hold in India?

The answer has two parts.

**Some form of the process very probably occurs.** People in every culture studied so far adjust their attitudes after some kinds of choice. Cross-cultural research suggests the question is not *whether* dissonance occurs outside the West, but *when* and *about what*.

**Research on choice points both ways.** Krishna Savani, Hazel Markus and colleagues compared middle-class Indian and American participants. Indians were less likely than Americans to choose according to their personal preferences (Savani, Markus & Conner, 2008), and less likely to describe ordinary actions as choices at all (Savani, Markus, Naidu, Kumar & Berlia, 2010). Since dissonance depends on feeling that you chose your act, this suggests weaker effects. But the same 2010 studies found that Indians were *more* likely to see an action as a choice when it involved responding to other people. And Joan Miller and colleagues found that Indian participants felt a sense of choice and satisfaction in helping family and friends even when it was expected of them (Miller, Das & Chakravarthy, 2011). The student in the 1959 study was doing a favour for another person. So an Indian student might well feel that agreeing was his own choice. The prediction is genuinely open, which makes it a good question for your journal.

**Explaining behaviour by the situation.** Miller (1984) found that Hindu Indian adults in Mysore explained everyday behaviour more by the situation and social roles, and less by the person's character, than Americans in Chicago did. A student who explains his own lie through the situation ("I was helping the department") has a ready outside reason, like the $20 students, and less need to change his opinion.

**Dissonance methods used in India.** Several Indian studies have used dissonance ideas in practice:

- In a randomised trial with 1,517 dental students in Guntur, Andhra Pradesh, students who were asked about COVID-19 precautions and then immediately about their own adherence, so that the gap became obvious, reported better adherence two weeks later than students in comparison groups (Chandu et al., 2022). This is close to the hypocrisy method described on the [importance page](/cases/cognitive-dissonance/importance/). Adherence was self-reported.
- A dissonance-based body image programme with 117 girls in India's guiding movement improved body esteem slightly, in a pilot with no control group (Paraskeva et al., 2024).
- In a qualitative study of meat eating in Mumbai and Sydney, many Mumbai participants who had seen animals slaughtered in markets described distancing and emotional numbing to cope, and they discussed inconsistencies with religious and caste-based norms (Khara, Riedy & Ruby, 2021). What counts as "inconsistent" depended on community norms.

**The specifics might not hold.** Who makes the request, who hears the statement, whether the speaker feels personally responsible, and what the payment means are all likely to differ. A direct replication in an Indian college would have to rethink almost every step.

We found no Indian replication of the 1959 study, or of any induced-compliance study. Most Indian research with "cognitive dissonance" in the title is consumer research on doubts after a purchase. The closest test in Asia is Paul Hiniker's 1964 study with refugees from mainland China in Hong Kong, described on the [cultural lens](/cases/cognitive-dissonance/lenses/cultural/) page: bigger gifts produced more compliance, but private attitudes did not change. The 2024 multi-lab replication had no Indian or South Asian laboratory. For India, this is a real gap, and one an Indian research group could fill.

## What cultural factors matter?

| Factor | Why it matters for this study |
|---|---|
| Sense of choice | If saying something is felt as a duty rather than a choice, the theory predicts little dissonance and little opinion change |
| Hierarchy and respect | A teacher's or elder's request may not be refusable, so compliance means something different |
| Politeness and role speech | Courteous untruths may not count as lies, so they may not create a clash |
| Context-sensitive selves | Saying different things in different settings may feel normal rather than inconsistent |
| Who is watching | Studies in Japan suggest dissonance can depend on others' views of one's choices; family and peer audiences may matter more than private consistency |
| Meaning of money | A payment from a teacher could feel like a token, a bribe or an insult, changing how much justification it gives |
| Language | Interview questions and rating scales translated into Hindi, Tamil or Bengali may not carry the same meaning |
| Class, caste, gender and place | Urban college students, rural youth, women and men may differ in how freely they can refuse and what honesty requires of them |

## What examples support or challenge the findings?

**Supporting: tobacco.** Festinger opened his 1957 book with smoking: a person "may know that smoking is bad for him and yet continue to smoke". India's second Global Adult Tobacco Survey (GATS-2, 2016-17), of adults aged 15 and over, shows exactly this kind of gap.

| Finding (GATS-2, 2016-17) | Figure |
|---|---|
| Adults who use tobacco in any form | 28.6%, about 267 million people |
| Current smokers who believe smoking causes serious illness | 91.3% |
| Current smokers who say smoking has not harmed their own body | 47.9% |
| Adults who believe smokeless tobacco causes serious illness | 95.6% |
| Smokeless tobacco users who say it has not harmed their own body | 61.8% |

Almost every smoker knows the general risk, but about half say it does not apply to them. This fits dissonance reduction through "self-exempting" beliefs. It does not prove it, because many young or light users may genuinely have no symptoms yet, and a single survey cannot show what causes what. Use of tobacco also varies hugely by state, from 9.7% in Goa to 64.5% in Tripura, a reminder that India is not one place (GATS-2 fact sheet, and full report Tables 9.1 and 9.4, Ministry of Health and Family Welfare and Tata Institute of Social Sciences). A study of 1,112 Indian smokers found that those who had quit by the second survey believed less in smoking's supposed benefits than those still smoking, and the authors concluded that Indian smokers show "similar patterns of dissonance reduction" to smokers in richer countries (Sidhu et al., 2022).

**Supporting: dissonance-based programmes.** The Guntur trial and the Free Being Me pilot, described above, suggest that methods built on dissonance can change behaviour and attitudes in Indian settings.

**Challenging: choice and authority.** Savani's finding that Indians construe fewer actions as personal choices, and Miller's finding that Indians explain behaviour more through situations, both suggest that a requested lie might create less dissonance. India's own research ethics guidelines make a related point. They warn that students and others "in a hierarchical position may not be in a position to disagree to participate for fear of authority" (ICMR, 2017, Section 6.9.2), and that "power differences between participants and researchers in India make it difficult for people to explicitly refuse" (Box 9.4). If refusal is hard, the "free choice" the 1959 design depends on is harder to create.

**Challenging: what counts as inconsistent.** The Mumbai meat-eating study shows that caste and religious norms shape which acts feel inconsistent at all. A clash that is obvious in one community may not exist in another.

The request may not feel like a choice. Students may hear the head of department's request as an instruction they cannot refuse, so they will have a strong outside reason for complying in both payment conditions: respect and duty. The theory then predicts little dissonance in either condition, and the $1 against $20 difference might disappear. The large payment could also embarrass students or seem improper, which adds a new meaning the original never had.

A fairer test would use a requester of lower status, such as a student assistant, and make refusal clearly acceptable. It would also measure how free each student felt to refuse, because perceived choice is the condition the theory depends on. Comparing a high-status and a low-status requester would turn the cultural difference into something the study can measure.
</Try>
