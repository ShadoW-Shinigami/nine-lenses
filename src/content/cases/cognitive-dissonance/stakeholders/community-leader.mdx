## What you care about

Imagine you lead a residents' welfare association in a city apartment complex, or you are an elected member of a gram panchayat. You want your community to act together on shared problems: segregating waste, saving water, getting children vaccinated. You care about results, but also about trust. If people feel tricked or pressured by you, the next campaign will fail. You also know that your community is not the same as the next one. An apartment complex in Bengaluru and a village in Odisha differ in language, hierarchy, and how people respond to a leader's request.

## What worries you about the original study

- **It shows how easily a polite request from authority gets compliance.** Most students agreed to mislead another student once a hesitant, embarrassed man in charge asked them (p. 205). Only three refused. As a leader, you hold that kind of authority. The study is a warning about how much people will do when someone they respect asks nicely.
- **It suggests that small rewards change minds more than large ones.** That finding could be misused. A leader could argue that volunteers should be paid very little so that they "come to love" the work. Low pay would then be justified by psychology instead of by fairness.
- **It worked through deception.** The students' change of opinion depended on a cover story, a fake absent assistant and a hidden recorder. A campaign that depends on people not knowing what is being done to them would destroy trust once it came out.
- **It changed what people said, not what they did.** The study measured a rating given in an interview. Your problem is whether waste is actually segregated tomorrow and next month.
- **Its people are not your people.** Sixty young American men, and a paper that admits its key assumption may hold only "in our culture" (p. 203).

## Your redesign

1. **Ask the community first.** Hold an open meeting to choose one shared goal, for example segregating wet and dry waste at source, and agree together how to test ways of encouraging it.
2. **Compare approaches openly.** In some buildings or wards, households receive information leaflets. In others, households are invited, with no pressure, to sign a public pledge displayed in the building or at the panchayat office. Households can say no without anyone noting it.
3. **Keep the pledge freely chosen.** No payment, no gifts, and no lists of who refused. The dissonance literature says commitment matters only when people feel they chose.
4. **Measure real behaviour.** With the community's agreement, volunteers check a sample of bins each week for two months and record how well waste is separated, without naming households.
5. **Share the results with everyone**, including the ones that did not work, and decide together what to continue.

## Why

Many dissonance studies after 1959 found that people are most likely to change, and to stay changed, when they have committed to something freely and publicly. A pledge chosen in front of neighbours creates the kind of gap between word and deed that people work to close, without any deception. It also fits a real legal duty: India's Solid Waste Management Rules, 2016, require households to segregate waste at source, so the campaign supports an existing rule rather than inventing a new demand.

Working with the community, and not on it, also follows Indian research guidance. The ICMR guidelines (2017) say that in community research the permission of gatekeepers, such as community heads or leaders, comes first, but that "community traditions do not substitute for individual consent" (Box 9.4). As a leader, your approval is not enough. Each household must be free to decide.

## What your redesign gives up

- **Results may not travel.** A pledge that works in a close-knit apartment complex may not work in a large ward where neighbours do not know each other, and the reverse.
- **Social pressure is hard to remove.** Even with "no lists", people may feel watched when a pledge is public, especially when the leader is respected or feared. Some compliance will come from pressure, not commitment.
- **Less control.** Buildings and wards differ in many ways besides the campaign, so differences in waste segregation cannot be traced to the pledge alone.
