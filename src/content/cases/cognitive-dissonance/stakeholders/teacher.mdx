## What you care about

Imagine you teach introductory psychology at an Indian university, and your department, like Stanford in 1959, expects students to take part in research. You care about two things that can pull against each other. You want your students to learn how research works, and taking part can teach that. And you want them to trust you: what you say in class should be true, and your requests should never feel like orders they cannot refuse.

## What worries you about the original study

- **A teacher's announcement became part of the cover story.** In the first week of the course, the instructor told students about a department study of experiments and urged them to be completely honest in its interviews (p. 204). The paper explains that this announcement let the researchers measure opinions in a context that seemed unconnected with the experiment. The paper does not say whether the instructor knew. Either way, the classroom was used to mislead students.
- **Taking part was a requirement.** Students had to spend "a certain number of hours" in experiments (p. 204). The paper mentions no alternative way to meet the requirement.
- **Students were asked to deceive a classmate.** The paid students believed they were misleading another undergraduate. Students who later study together, or share a hostel, may not feel comfortable with that.
- **The lesson most students took away is not reported.** The paper says the true purpose was explained "in detail" (p. 206), but not what students felt about being deceived. Students who feel tricked may come to distrust psychology itself.
- **Students who refused were set aside.** Three refused the money and two told the woman the truth (p. 207). From a teacher's point of view, these students showed integrity. In the paper they are simply excluded data.

## Your redesign

1. **Never use your own teaching as part of a cover story.** If a study needs a separate measure of opinion, the department runs a real survey, honestly described, and the teacher announces only true things.
2. **Run a fair participant pool.** Students earn the credit either by taking part in studies or by an equal alternative, such as reading a study and writing a one-page summary. Marks never depend on which option a student chooses.
3. **Separate the teacher from the research.** Students should not be recruited, run or debriefed by the person who grades them.
4. **Warn the class about deception in general.** At the start of term, explain that some studies hide part of their purpose until the end, and that students will always be told the truth afterwards. Students can then choose studies knowing this.
5. **Turn the debriefing into teaching.** After the study ends, use a class session to explain the design, the reasons for the deception, and its ethical costs, and ask students what they think.
6. **Treat refusals as legitimate.** Make clear, in class, that saying no to a researcher is acceptable and will never be counted against anyone.

## Why

The American Psychological Association's code says that when research participation is a course requirement, students must be offered "equitable alternative activities" (Standard 8.04). The British Psychological Society's *Code of Human Research Ethics* (2021) asks researchers to be "particularly diligent" with people in "a dependent or unequal relationship", such as students, and says that where participation is a course requirement this should be clearly stated in course handbooks (Section 8.1). India's ICMR guidelines (2017) say that when students are recruited, enrolling them should be "pertinent to the research questions and is not merely a matter of convenience" (Section 6.9.1), and that "mechanisms to avoid coercion due to being part of an institution or hierarchy" should be described in the research plan (Section 6.9.4).

There is also a teaching reason. A study like this one is an excellent lesson in experimental design, but only if students feel the deception was explained and justified. A student who leaves feeling foolish learns to distrust research. A student who leaves understanding why the cover story was needed has learned how psychologists think, including about ethics.

## What your redesign gives up

- **The perfect disguise is gone.** The 1959 interview worked because the students had heard about the department study from someone they trusted. A real, honestly described survey is a weaker cover, and some students will link it to the experiment.
- **Fewer participants.** With an equal alternative, some students will choose the written task, so studies may take longer to fill.
- **A more aware participant pool.** Students warned about deception at the start of term will be more suspicious in every study, not just this one.
