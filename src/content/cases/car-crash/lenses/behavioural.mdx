## The lens in brief

Behaviourism explains behaviour through what can be observed: the situations people are in, what they do, and what follows. John B. Watson founded the school in the United States in 1913. B. F. Skinner developed it from the 1930s onwards. Behaviourists do not explain behaviour by pointing to things inside the head, such as memories or beliefs. They look for the conditions that make a behaviour more or less likely. The key idea is <Term>reinforcement</Term>: a consequence that makes a behaviour more likely to happen again. A person's <Term>reinforcement history</Term> is the record of which of their past behaviours were followed by which consequences.

Skinner treated speaking as behaviour like any other. In his book *Verbal Behavior* (1957) he gave names to different kinds of verbal response. A <Term>tact</Term> is a verbal response controlled by something in the non-verbal world, such as saying "red" because you see something red. An <Term>intraverbal</Term> is a verbal response controlled by other words, such as answering "four" to "two plus two". The listener whose reactions shape what a person says is called the <Term>audience</Term>.

The study is close to this lens in method and far from it in explanation. Loftus and Palmer changed one stimulus, a word, and measured observable responses, a number and a tick. But they explained the result by a change in a "memory representation" (p. 586), which a behaviourist would not use.

## How this lens reads the study

**A speed estimate is verbal behaviour with two sources of control.** For a behaviourist, a student who writes "40" is not reading a number off a stored memory. The student is emitting a verbal response in a situation. Part of the response may be controlled by the film, as a tact. Part may be controlled by the words of the question, as an intraverbal. The study's own data show which source was stronger. Four of the films were staged crashes at 20, 30, 40 and 40 miles per hour, but the average estimates were 37.7, 36.2, 39.7 and 36.1 (p. 586). The film hardly controlled the answer at all. The paper says so in words a behaviourist would accept: "The actual speed of the vehicles controlled little variance in subject reporting, while the phrasing of the question controlled considerable variance" (p. 586).

**Response bias is a behavioural explanation, and the paper names it.** The authors offered two explanations for Experiment 1. The first was that "the differential speed estimates result merely from response-bias factors. A subject is uncertain whether to say 30 mph or 40 mph, for example, and the verb *smashed* biases his response towards the higher estimate" (p. 586). This is very close to a behavioural account: when the non-verbal stimulus gives weak control, verbal stimuli in the question take over. The behaviourist need not add the second explanation, a changed memory, unless the evidence forces it.

**The experimenter is an audience, even on paper.** The questions were printed, so no experimenter could smile or frown at an answer. But the participants were university students, answering questions set by a university researcher, in a setting much like an examination. A long history of classrooms has reinforced answers that fit the teacher's words. The paper itself says that the experimenter "is effectively labeling the accident a smash" (p. 588). A behaviourist would add that a student has learned, over years, that agreeing with the way a question is framed is usually the answer that pays.

**The same word, again and again.** In Experiment 1, the 9 students in each verb condition answered a speed question after each of the seven films. The paper implies that each student read the same verb after all seven films. Repetition strengthens control by a stimulus. The study cannot separate the effect of the verb on one occasion from its effect when repeated.

**Experiment 2 is the hard case.** A week later, students answered "yes" or "no" to "Did you see any broken glass?" The verb was not repeated in that question. A behaviourist can still point to a history in which "smash" and "broken glass" go together in everyday talk, so that one word makes the other more likely as a verbal response. But this is a stretch. The broken glass result is the part of the study that a simple account of answering one question explains least well.

## What this lens values in the study

It manipulates one observable stimulus and measures observable responses, with no reliance on what students say about their inner states. Its measures are counted, not interpreted, and printed questions remove differences in how the stimulus was delivered. It also takes the response-bias explanation seriously, instead of jumping straight to claims about the mind.

## What it would question

- Calling the result a change in "memory" when only answers were observed.
- Ignoring what followed each answer: the study measured responses but never varied their consequences.
- Treating the written questionnaire as a neutral channel, when students have long histories of answering teachers' questions in the expected way.
- Not testing whether paying for accurate answers would reduce the effect of the verb.
- Collecting only verbal responses, a number and a tick, when a less verbal response, such as choosing which of several video clips matches the crash, might show weaker control by the words.

## Evidence from this tradition

**Reinforcement shapes what children report.** Garven, Wood, Malpass and Shaw (1998) used interview techniques taken from the McMartin Preschool case, an American child abuse investigation of the 1980s, with 36 preschool children. Interviews that included social influence and reinforcement led to 58% false accusations, against 17% with suggestive questions alone. The authors concluded that "social influence and reinforcement appeared to be more powerful determinants of children's answers than simple suggestive questions." Garven, Wood and Malpass (2000) then ran a follow-up with 120 children aged 5 to 7. Praising some answers raised false allegations about a classroom visitor from 12% to 35%. It also raised false "fantastic" claims, such as being taken from school in a helicopter, from 5% to 52%. The children repeated the allegations in a second interview even after the praise had stopped.

**Feedback changes adult witnesses too.** Gary Wells and Amy Bradfield (1998) studied 352 witnesses who all picked the wrong person from a set of photographs in which the culprit was absent. Witnesses who were told "Good, you identified the actual suspect" later reported more certainty, a better view and a clearer memory. The effect was as strong in those who said the feedback had not influenced them. A single consequence after an answer changed what witnesses said about their own memory.

**Remembering can be treated as operant behaviour.** <Term>Operant behaviour</Term> is behaviour that is shaped by its consequences. In two recent experiments, recall and recognition were higher when correct responses were reinforced, and higher still with a larger reinforcer and a shorter delay (de Olives, Polin and Perez, 2024). The authors conclude that "operant processes have an important role in traditional human memory tasks".

**Reinforcement histories differ.** Critchfield (1996) found that adults were more likely to delay or avoid reporting their own failures than their successes. He linked this to a history of punishment for unflattering self-reports, which he called "a common experience in United States culture". This links the behavioural lens to the [cultural lens](/cases/car-crash/lenses/cultural/): what a witness has learned to say depends on where they learned it.

**Evidence against the lens.** The broken glass result is hard to explain as a bias in answering one question, because the leading word was not repeated a week later (pp. 587-588). Brain imaging studies, described on the [biological lens](/cases/car-crash/lenses/biological/) page, find some differences in sensory activity between true and false memories, which a purely behavioural account does not address. Even behaviour analysts who argue for treating "memory as behavior" admit that the cognitive account "has been the only one that has made and tested useful predictions" in this area (Delaney and Austin, 1998).

<Note title="A related challenge from cognitive psychology">
Michael McCloskey and Maria Zaragoza (1985) were not behaviourists, but their critique supports a response-level reading. They argued that the standard misinformation procedure "is inappropriate for assessing effects of misleading information on memory", and after six experiments concluded that "misleading postevent information has no effect on memory for the original event". The [importance page](/cases/car-crash/importance/) describes the debate.
</Note>

## A study this lens would design

1. Show the same accident films as in Experiment 1, and ask the speed question with *smashed* or *contacted*.
2. Vary what follows each answer. One group gets no reaction. One group hears "Good, thank you" after any answer. One group hears "Are you sure?" after any answer.
3. Vary the audience. Some students answer a police officer in uniform, some answer a fellow student, and some fill in a computer form alone.
4. Pay half the students a small sum for every estimate within 5 miles per hour of the true speed, using staged crashes of known speed.
5. As well as asking for a number, ask every student to pick, from five short clips of the same crash at different speeds, the one that matches what they saw.

**Prediction:** if the verb effect is mainly verbal control, it will be largest when an authority figure asks and praises answers, smaller when accuracy is paid for, and smaller for the clip choice than for the spoken number.

The behavioural lens looks at what followed each answer. The vice-principal reinforced one answer, "Rohan pushed first", with approval, and followed uncertainty with mild pressure. For children, an adult's approval is a powerful reinforcer, so the reinforced answer became more likely as the morning went on. The added details, such as Rohan laughing, can be read as verbal behaviour that fits the reinforced story and earns more approval. Nothing here requires a change in what the children "remember".

Garven, Wood and Malpass (2000) found exactly this kind of effect: praise for certain answers raised false allegations among young children from 12% to 35%, and the allegations persisted after the praise stopped. Wells and Bradfield (1998) showed that confirming feedback also raises adults' confidence. The practical lesson is that the interviewer's reactions are part of the stimulus. A neutral response to every answer, as in a printed questionnaire, removes one source of contamination that the 1974 study never had to face.
