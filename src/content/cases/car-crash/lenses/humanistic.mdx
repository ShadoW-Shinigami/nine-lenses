## The lens in brief

Humanistic psychology grew in the United States in the 1950s and 1960s, led by Carl Rogers and Abraham Maslow. It presented itself as a "third force" against both behaviourism and psychoanalysis. It starts from the person's own experience. Its central ideas are that people are active agents who make choices, and that they are driven to grow and to become who they can be. It also holds that each person lives in their own <Term>phenomenal field</Term>: the world as they experience it. For Rogers, this perceived world is, for the individual, reality. He developed this idea in *Client-Centered Therapy* (1951).

Two terms matter here. The <Term>self-concept</Term> is a person's picture of who they are. <Term>Narrative identity</Term>, a later idea from the psychologist Dan McAdams, is a person's "internalized and evolving life story, integrating the reconstructed past and imagined future to provide life with some degree of unity and purpose" (McAdams and McLean, 2013). Notice the phrase "reconstructed past". This tradition accepts that memories are rebuilt. It asks what the rebuilding is for.

The study is far from this lens. It measured numbers and ticks, not experience, and it treated memory as a store to be checked for accuracy rather than as part of a person's life.

## How this lens reads the study

**People treated as recording devices.** The study asks one kind of question about memory: is it correct? A speed in miles per hour was compared with the real speed. A "yes" to broken glass was counted as an error (pp. 586-587). This treats a person who remembers as an instrument, like a camera, to be tested for faults. The humanistic lens does not deny that accuracy matters, especially in court. But it notes that this is only one thing memory does, and the study looks at nothing else.

**The person's own words were collected, then set aside.** Every student first wrote "an account of the accident you have just seen" in their own words (p. 586). This was the one moment when students could say what they saw, in their own order, with their own emphasis. The paper does not report these accounts. For a humanistic psychologist, the person's own description of their experience is the most important evidence there is, and here it was discarded in favour of answers to the researchers' questions.

**The word changed the meaning of the event.** The word *smashed* told the student, in effect, what kind of event they had witnessed: a violent one. The paper says the verb may make the student "see" the accident "as being more severe than it actually was" (pp. 586-587). From the humanistic point of view, this is a change in the person's phenomenal field. The accident the student now lives with, in memory, is a different accident. That is a stronger claim than a change in a number.

**Most people were not misled.** Of the 50 students who read *smashed*, 34 correctly said they had not seen broken glass (p. 587). Of the 150 students in Experiment 2, 121 said no. The study is usually told as a story about how easily people are misled. A humanistic reading notices the agency in the result: most students, even after a leading question, reported what they had actually seen. The study does not ask what helped them resist.

**Nobody asked how it felt.** The paper does not report whether students were told, at the end, that there had been no broken glass. A student who learns that they confidently "remembered" something false may feel foolish, or may start to doubt their memory in general. The humanistic lens asks how the experience was for the participants, and treats that question as part of good research, not an extra.

## What this lens values in the study

It shows, in a simple way, that memory is not a passive copy, and that people actively build their accounts of what happened. That fits the humanistic view of people as active makers of meaning. The study also began each questionnaire with an open question, inviting the student's own account before any specific question. That was a respectful first step, even if the paper did not use the answers.

## What it would question

- Reducing memory to accuracy, when memories also carry meaning, identity and feeling.
- Discarding the students' free accounts.
- Describing participants only as "students", with nothing about who they were or what the films meant to them.
- Focusing on the minority who were misled rather than on the majority who were not, and on what helped them.
- The silence about debriefing, and about how participants felt on learning they had been wrong.
- The use of such findings in court to question the memories of real people, whose experience of being disbelieved the research never studies.

## Evidence from this tradition

**Life stories are reconstructed, and that can be healthy.** McAdams (2001) proposed a "life story model of identity", in which people "provide their lives with unity and purpose by constructing internalized and evolving narratives of the self". Research on narrative identity treats the reshaping of memories as a normal part of making sense of one's life. Some ways of retelling a hard experience, for example as a story of growth after suffering, go with greater well-being (McAdams and McLean, 2013). From this angle, reconstruction is not only a source of error. It is how people make their past bearable and meaningful.

**Memory belongs to someone.** The sense that a memory is my own experience is central to the humanistic view of the self. A similar point was made long ago by Kumarila, a philosopher of the Mimamsa school of Indian philosophy. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy summarises his view: "it doesn't make sense to think one might remember an experience, and at the same time be uncertain about whose experience it is that's remembered" (Arnold, 2024). Yet the misinformation research shows that a detail can enter one's own memory from someone else's words. The study puts pressure on exactly this first-person sense of ownership, which the [decolonial lens](/cases/car-crash/lenses/decolonial/) also discusses.

**Evidence against the lens.** The main finding of this research is a direct challenge to trusting experience at face value. A person can be confident, sincere and wrong. Wells and Bradfield (1998) found that witnesses who were told "Good, you identified the actual suspect", after picking the wrong person, later reported more certainty and a clearer memory. The effect was as strong in witnesses who said the feedback had not influenced them. People do not always know what has shaped their own experience. A purely humanistic approach, which takes the person's account as the truth for that person, would miss this. In court, where an innocent person's freedom may depend on a witness, that is a serious limitation.

## A study this lens would design

1. Show the accident film. Ask each participant first to describe, in their own words and at their own pace, what they saw and how it felt to watch. Record these accounts and analyse them.
2. Ask the speed question with *smashed* or *hit*, as in the original.
3. A week later, ask participants again to describe the accident freely, before any specific question. Compare the two accounts for changes in detail, tone and meaning.
4. At the end, explain the study fully. Then interview each participant about how they experienced being questioned, whether they noticed the verb, and how they feel about any change in their memory.
5. Invite participants to comment on the researchers' interpretation of their accounts before the results are written up.

**Aim:** to understand how the person experiences a leading question and a changed memory, and what helps people keep their own account, alongside the numbers the original study collected.

**Prediction:** the second free account of the *smashed* group will describe the accident as more violent than their first, and participants who say they noticed the verb will change their accounts less.

The humanistic lens would notice Ramesh's experience: a man who tried to help, and whose sincerity and dignity were attacked in public. The 1974 study measures whether answers are accurate. It says nothing about what it is like to have your memory questioned, or how that affects a person's willingness to come forward again. For Ramesh, being disbelieved is not a technical matter about verbs. It touches his self-concept as an honest person and a respected teacher.

The lawyer's point is a fair one: the research shows that words like "racing", if they came from the officer, could shape a witness's account. The way to respect both concerns is to record the first interview fully, ideally on video, so that the court can see who first used which words, instead of relying on attack and denial. The questioning in court can test the evidence without humiliating the witness, for example by asking Ramesh to describe what he saw in his own words. The humanistic lens adds that a witness is a person, not only a source of evidence, and that how witnesses are treated affects whether others will come forward at all.
