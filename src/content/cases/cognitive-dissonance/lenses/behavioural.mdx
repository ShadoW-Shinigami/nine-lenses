## The lens in brief

Behaviourism explains behaviour through the environment, and above all through consequences. Its founders were John B. Watson and, later, B. F. Skinner. The key idea is <Term>reinforcement</Term>: a consequence that makes a behaviour more likely to happen again. Behaviourists study what people do and say, not unobservable inner states. Skinner's <Term>radical behaviourism</Term> went further: it treats thoughts and feelings as behaviour too, but insists they are explained by a person's history of consequences, not used to explain anything themselves.

This lens is the study's opponent. Festinger and Carlsmith designed their experiment partly to show that reward-based thinking gets attitude change wrong. A behaviourist reading therefore has two jobs: to explain why the reward-based prediction failed, and to offer a different explanation of the result.

## How this lens reads the study

**The money reinforced the lie, not the opinion.** In behavioural terms, the $1 or $20 was paid for one act: telling the woman the tasks were fun. Nobody reinforced the later rating given to the interviewer. A behaviourist would point out that reinforcement theory never actually predicted that a bigger payment for one act would change a *different* act, a rating given later, to a different person, in a different room. The "common-sense" reward prediction that the study refuted was looser than the theory itself.

**Statements that the situation explains, and statements it does not.** Skinner (1957) distinguished between kinds of verbal behaviour. A <Term>mand</Term> is a statement controlled by a reward or demand: you say it because of what you get. A <Term>tact</Term> is a statement controlled by something the speaker has experienced: you say it because it describes what you saw or felt. Listeners treat tacts as trustworthy and mands as not. A student who said "it was fun" for $20 made an obvious mand. A student who said it for $1 made something that looked much more like a tact.

**Self-perception: reading yourself like a stranger.** Daryl Bem (1967) built on this to offer a rival to dissonance theory. He proposed that people often do not know their own attitudes directly. They work them out the same way an outside observer would, from their own behaviour and the situation it happened in. The $20 student thinks: "I said it was fun, but anyone would for $20, so that tells me nothing." The $1 student thinks: "I said it was fun, and $1 is no reason to lie, so I suppose I found it fairly interesting." No inner tension is needed, only inference. This is <Term>self-perception theory</Term>.

**Bem's test: observers get the same result.** Bem's boldest move was what he called an <Term>interpersonal simulation</Term>. He gave ordinary people a recorded description of the procedure, told them about one participant in one condition, and asked them to estimate that participant's real rating of the tasks. The observers had done no tasks, told no lie and received no money, so they could have no dissonance. Yet their estimates showed the same pattern as the real results: the man paid $1 was judged to like the tasks more than the man paid $20 or the control man. If outside observers can reproduce the result by inference alone, Bem argued, "it is not necessary to postulate an aversive motivational drive toward consistency" (Bem, 1967).

| Group | Real students' average rating (1959) | Observers' estimate (Bem, 1967) |
|---|---|---|
| Control | -0.45 | -1.56 |
| Paid $1 | +1.35 | +0.52 |
| Paid $20 | -0.05 | -1.96 |

The observers' numbers are lower overall, but the pattern is the same: the $1 man stands out, and the $20 man is no higher than the control man.

<Note title="How Bem's simulation worked">
Bem played 75 undergraduates, 25 per condition, a tape describing a student called "Bob Downing" who did the tasks and, in the paid conditions, accepted money to tell the next participant they were fun. Observers then estimated Bob's rating on the same -5 to +5 scale. The $1 estimate differed from both the control (t = 2.60) and the $20 estimate (t = 3.52, p &lt; .001); the $20 and control estimates did not differ significantly (Bem, 1967, Table 1, p. 189).
</Note>

**The social reinforcement nobody counted.** The young woman, after her doubtful remark, listened "quietly, accepting and agreeing to everything" the student said (p. 206). The experimenter thanked him and wrote down his phone number. A behaviourist would note that saying "it's interesting" was socially reinforced in both paid conditions. This does not explain the $1 against $20 difference, but it shows that consequences were everywhere in the procedure, not only in the money.

## What this lens values in the study

It values the study's focus on what people actually do and say, and its tight control of the environment. The independent variable is a consequence, money, which is exactly the kind of variable behaviourists study. The result also supports a behaviourist theme in an unexpected way: behaviour can come first and attitudes follow. Saying something changed what was said later.

## What it would question

- The use of an unobservable inner state, dissonance, to explain the result, when it was never measured.
- The circular logic: dissonance is inferred from attitude change, and attitude change is explained by dissonance.
- The treatment of the interview rating as a window into a private opinion, rather than as another piece of verbal behaviour shaped by its own context.
- The claim that reinforcement theory was refuted, when the reward was attached to a different behaviour from the one measured.

## Evidence from this tradition

**Bem (1967).** Bem's interpersonal simulations of this study and of other dissonance experiments reproduced the original patterns with observers who had no dissonance to reduce. Judson Mills (1967), who had helped run the 1959 study, replied at once. Bem's observers had not been told that the payment had nothing to do with the man's opinion of the tasks, so they may have assumed that men who liked the tasks were the ones willing to say so cheaply. Others added that showing people *could* infer attitudes this way does not show that the real participants *did* (Gross summarises this objection from Stroebe and Jonas, 1996).

**When reward works the "normal" way.** Several studies found that the direction of the effect depends on choice. Linder, Cooper and Jones (1967) had students write essays they disagreed with for small or large payments. When students felt free to refuse, the smaller payment produced more attitude change, as in the 1959 study. When they were simply told to write the essay, the larger payment produced more change, as reward theory predicts. So incentives work the behaviourist way when people do not feel responsible for their act.

**Evidence against the lens.** Zanna and Cooper (1974) gave students a pill that was really a placebo, telling some that it would make them feel tense. Students who could blame their discomfort on the pill did not change their attitudes after writing a counter-attitudinal essay. Students told the pill would relax them changed their attitudes even more. Self-perception theory has no place for discomfort, so it cannot easily explain why a pill story should matter. Later studies measured physical arousal directly after counter-attitudinal acts (Croyle & Cooper, 1983). Fazio, Zanna and Cooper (1977) proposed a compromise that many textbooks accept: self-perception explains attitude change after behaviour that is only mildly different from what a person already thinks, while dissonance explains change after behaviour that clearly contradicts it.

## A study this lens would design

1. Keep the boring tasks and the $1 and $20 payments.
2. Measure behaviour, not only ratings: after the interview, offer each student the chance to sign up for another session of the same tasks, and record who signs up.
3. Vary the social consequences of the lie: in some sessions the waiting woman thanks the student warmly, in others she looks bored and unconvinced.
4. Run an interpersonal simulation alongside: show observers a video of each real student's conversation and ask them to estimate his true opinion.
5. Compare the real students' ratings with the observers' estimates.

**Prediction:** if self-perception is right, observers watching the same behaviour will estimate the same attitudes the students report, and the warmth of the woman's response will shape both.

Self-perception theory says each student infers his own attitude by looking at his behaviour and its circumstances, as an outside observer would. The secretary has an obvious external cause for his statement: it was his duty. So his statement tells him nothing about what he really thinks, and he keeps his original view.

The favour-doer has no clear external cause. He was not required to say it and gained nothing. When he looks back on his behaviour, the most reasonable inference is that he must not dislike the food that much. Dissonance theory would explain the same pattern through discomfort that the favour-doer had to reduce. Self-perception reaches the same prediction through inference alone, which is why the two theories were so hard to separate.
