## Contribution to psychology

The study made three contributions.

**It showed that a question can change an answer, and probably a memory.** Lawyers already knew that leading questions were risky. The study measured the effect under controlled conditions, showed that a single verb was enough, and showed that the effect could appear a week later as a "memory" of something that was not there.

**It moved memory research towards real events.** Most memory research of the time used word lists. This study used filmed accidents and asked about speeds and broken glass, the kind of details witnesses are asked about. It helped to create <Term>applied cognitive psychology</Term>, the use of methods from cognitive psychology to answer practical questions, here about witnesses.

**It started a research programme.** Within a year, Loftus published four more experiments with a similar design (Loftus, 1975), and within four years the standard method of the field was in place. Loftus later named this paper as the start of her work on what came to be called the <Term>misinformation effect</Term>: the change in a person's report of an event after they receive misleading information about it. In her words, "For more than 30 years, I have been studying a phenomenon called the misinformation effect (Loftus and Palmer 1974; Loftus 1975; Loftus and Hoffman 1989)" (Loftus, 2005a, p. 1).

**The misinformation effect.** The 1974 study used one leading question. Loftus and her colleagues soon developed a three-step method: people see an event, then receive misleading information about it, then take a memory test. In the best-known version, Loftus, Miller and Burns (1978) showed students slides of a red car at either a stop sign or a yield sign, then asked some of them a question that named the other sign. Shown both pictures later, only 41% of misled students chose the correct sign, against 75% of students given consistent information. Hundreds of studies have used this method since. The effect "has been observed in a variety of human and nonhuman species" (Loftus, 2005b, abstract).

**The argument about mechanism.** The 1974 paper said only that two kinds of information were combined so that the witness could not tell their sources apart (p. 588). In 1980 Loftus and the psychologist Geoffrey Loftus went further. They described "a strong suspicion that substitution has occurred", meaning that the misleading information "has irrevocably replaced the original". Even then, they added that substitution and coexistence "are not mutually exclusive" (Loftus and Loftus, 1980). Michael McCloskey and Maria Zaragoza (1985) challenged the substitution idea. In six experiments, misled participants did as well as controls when the misleading option was removed from the final test. They concluded that "misleading postevent information has no effect on memory for the original event". Other researchers found that asking people to think about where each memory came from could remove much of the effect (Lindsay and Johnson, 1989). A review of many studies found that warnings given after the misleading information cut the effect to "less than half of its size on average" (Blank and Launay, 2014). By 1989, Loftus herself concluded that several processes are involved, with "misinformation acceptance" playing "a major role" (Loftus and Hoffman, 1989).

**Source monitoring.** This line of work fed into the <Term>source monitoring framework</Term> (Johnson, Hashtroudi and Lindsay, 1993). It holds that memories do not come labelled with their source. People judge where a memory came from, whether something was seen, heard about or imagined, from its qualities, and these judgements can go wrong. The 1974 paper's phrase, "we are unable to tell from which source some specific detail is recalled" (p. 588), is very close to this later view.

**Suggestibility as a normal feature of memory.** Daniel Schacter (1999) listed <Term>suggestibility</Term> among the "seven sins of memory", and argued that these errors are "by-products of otherwise adaptive features of memory". A memory system that updates itself with new information is useful most of the time. The car crash study shows the cost.

**From details to whole events.** In the 1990s the research moved from changing details to creating whole false events. In the "lost in the mall" study, Loftus and Jacqueline Pickrell (1995) used family members' help to suggest to 24 adults that they had been lost in a shopping mall as children. Six of them, 25%, came to remember the event fully or partly. Later studies used doctored photographs (Wade, Garry, Read and Lindsay, 2002) and other false events. A combined analysis of eight such studies, with 423 participants, classified 30.4% as having false memories (Scoboria and colleagues, 2017). A sceptical review argued that only about 15% of such experiences are likely to be full memories (Brewin and Andrews, 2017). A preregistered replication of the mall study, with 123 participants, found 35% (Murphy and colleagues, 2023).

## Influence on practice

The 1974 study is one of many behind the changes described here. The official documents read for this guide do not cite it by name. The reforms were driven above all by real cases of wrongful conviction. But their advice on questioning witnesses follows directly from the research it began.

**How police question witnesses.** The US Department of Justice published a guide for police, *Eyewitness Evidence*, in 1999. It tells officers to "Use open-ended questions (e.g., 'What can you tell me about the car?')" and to add closed questions where needed. It also tells them to "Avoid leading questions (e.g., 'Was the car red?')". In England and Wales, the official guidance on interviewing victims and witnesses is *Achieving Best Evidence* (Ministry of Justice, 2023). It warns that leading questions "may also significantly distort the witness's memory in the direction implied by the leading question". It says they "should only be used as a last resort" (para. 3.69). It also notes that questions worded with "the" rather than "a" produce more errors, because "'the' presupposes the existence of an item" (para. 3.70). That is a finding from Loftus and Zanni (1975).

**The cognitive interview.** Ronald Fisher and Edward Geiselman developed the <Term>cognitive interview</Term> in the 1980s. Witnesses are asked to recall the event in their own words, to mentally return to the scene, and to report everything, however trivial, while the interviewer avoids suggestive questions. A review of 42 studies found that it produced many more correct details than a standard police interview, with a smaller rise in incorrect ones (Köhnken, Milne, Memon and Bull, 1999). It is now part of police training in the United Kingdom and elsewhere.

**Interviewing children.** Children are more open to suggestion than adults (Ceci and Bruck, 1993). Structured protocols for interviewing children about abuse, such as the protocol developed at the US National Institute of Child Health and Human Development, rely on open invitations ("Tell me everything that happened") and avoid leading questions. A review of research on this protocol concluded that interview quality "reliably and dramatically improves" when it is used (Lamb and colleagues, 2007).

**Courts.** In *State v. Henderson* (2011), the Supreme Court of New Jersey reviewed more than 200 scientific studies on eyewitness memory, with testimony from seven experts, and changed how the state's courts handle identification evidence, including new instructions to juries (Harvard Law Review, 2012). A US National Research Council report, *Identifying the Culprit* (2014), recommended reforms to police procedures and allowed for expert testimony and jury instructions about memory. The Innocence Project, an American organisation that uses DNA evidence to overturn wrongful convictions, found that 69% of the first 375 DNA exonerations in the United States (1989-2020) involved eyewitness misidentification. That figure is mostly about witnesses picking the wrong person, a related problem, not about leading questions.

**India.** Indian law has long restricted leading questions. The Indian Evidence Act of 1872 defined them in section 141, and the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam of 2023, which replaced it, keeps the same rules. The [India page](/cases/car-crash/india/) looks at how these rules, and the realities of Indian policing and courts, relate to the study.

## What later research did to the claims

| The claim | What later research found | Status today |
|---|---|---|
| The wording of a question can change what a witness reports | Many studies; in the newest survey, 100% of 76 experts agreed (Seale-Carlisle and colleagues, 2024) | Very well supported |
| Information after an event can change later reports | The misinformation effect, found in hundreds of studies; experts 100% agreed | Very well supported |
| The verb *smashed* raises speed estimates | No modern direct replication found. Two conceptual replications found no verb effect (Goldschmied and colleagues, 2017; Raghunath and colleagues, 2021). One study found it only in children aged about 12 to 14 (Lipscomb and colleagues, 1985) | Uncertain. Less reliable than textbooks suggest |
| The original memory is changed or replaced | Disputed since 1985; much of the effect can be reversed by warnings or by attention to source | Not established. Several processes are involved |
| Laboratory findings apply fully to real witnesses | Some real witnesses resisted misleading questions (Yuille and Cutshall, 1986); first, uncontaminated reports can be accurate (Wixted and Wells, 2017) | Partly. The risk is real, but its size outside the laboratory varies |

**The general finding is secure.** That questions and later information can shape what witnesses report is now accepted almost without dispute among researchers. In a 2024 survey, every one of 76 eyewitness experts agreed that "how the question is worded can influence the eyewitness's answer", and every one agreed about the influence of information received after an event (Seale-Carlisle and colleagues, 2024).

**The specific verb effect is weaker than its fame.** This guide found no large, modern, direct replication of the 1974 experiments. Two recent studies that tested the same idea in new ways found no effect of verbs on speed estimates. In one, written descriptions of an ice hockey collision as a "contact", "bump" or "smash" made no difference to how fast people thought the players were skating (Goldschmied and colleagues, 2017). In the other, people judging car crash videos gave the same speeds whether the cars "made contact" or "smashed into" each other; their estimates depended instead on what they already believed about how fast cars travel (Raghunath and colleagues, 2021). Remember too that in 1974 the broken glass difference between *smashed* and *hit* rested on 16 against 7 students (see the [findings page](/cases/car-crash/findings/)).

<Note title="Why a famous effect can be hard to replicate">
A failure to replicate does not prove the original was wrong. Effects of a single word are likely to be small, and to depend on how uncertain people are, how the event is shown and who the witnesses are. Small original samples, like 9 students per verb, tend to produce results that look larger than the true effect. Both recent conceptual replications differed from the original in important ways, one used ice hockey and the other is a short conference paper, so neither settles the question. The fair summary is that the broad principle is secure, but the exact verb effect has not been tested well enough since 1974 to be sure of its size.
</Note>

**Real witnesses and pristine conditions.** John Yuille and Judith Cutshall (1986) interviewed 13 witnesses to a real shooting in Canada about five months afterwards. Later accounts of the study report that most of them resisted the misleading questions they were asked and stayed accurate, though a study of 13 self-selected witnesses cannot settle the question. John Wixted and Gary Wells (2017) argued that, under "pristine" conditions, a witness's first identification made with high confidence is "remarkably accurate". Wixted, Mickes and Fisher (2018) extended the argument: eyewitness memory "is reliable if it is not contaminated and if proper testing procedures are used". Other researchers replied with reasons for doubt (Wade, Nash and Lindsay, 2018). These arguments do not contradict the 1974 study. They make its practical message sharper: the first report matters most, and leading questions are one way to contaminate what comes after.

**The memory wars.** The most bitter use of this research came in the 1990s, when it was used to question memories of childhood abuse recovered in therapy. The debate continues. In one survey, most hypnotherapists (82%) and psychoanalysts (69%) agreed that traumatic memories are often repressed, against far fewer research psychologists (Patihis and colleagues, 2014). The [ethics page](/cases/car-crash/ethics/) and the [psychoanalytic lens](/cases/car-crash/lenses/psychoanalytic/) discuss this.

<SourceCheck title="Source check: permanently altered">
Some popular accounts say this study showed that a single word "can permanently alter" a memory, or that "the original memory is overwritten". The 1974 paper tested memory once, a week later, and said its results were "consistent with" reconstruction (p. 585). It made no claim about permanence. Loftus's strongest statement about replacement came six years later, was hedged even then, and was softened by 1989. Write "can change what a witness later reports", not "permanently alters memory".
</SourceCheck>

The trainer is right that the wording of questions can change what witnesses report. That general finding is now accepted by every expert in the 2024 survey, and the 1974 study is one of its foundations.

But the claim goes wrong in two places. First, the study did not show that all memory is useless. Two-thirds of the *smashed* group correctly said there was no broken glass, and the specific verb effect has not been reliably replicated since. Second, later research shows that memory can be quite accurate when it is collected early and without contamination (Wixted and Wells, 2017; Wixted, Mickes and Fisher, 2018). The practical advice is not to ignore witnesses but to interview them well: early, with open questions such as "Tell me what you saw", without suggesting answers, and with a record of exactly what was asked. CCTV is useful, but it does not remove the need to question witnesses carefully.
</Try>
