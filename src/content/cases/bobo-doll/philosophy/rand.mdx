## The tradition in brief

Ayn Rand (1905 to 1982) was born Alissa Rosenbaum in St Petersburg, left the Soviet Union for the United States in 1926, and became famous as the author of *The Fountainhead* (1943) and *Atlas Shrugged* (1957). She called herself both novelist and philosopher, and named her system Objectivism. Her standing in academic philosophy is unusual. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy notes that she has a vast popular readership but that "only a few professional philosophers have taken her work seriously", partly because of her polemical style; a small scholarly literature has grown since the 2000s. This page treats her as what she is, an influential and combative thinker outside the academy, and quotes her own words.

Three ideas run through this page.

**Reason is volitional.** For Rand, <Term>volition</Term>, or free will, lives in one place: whether a person switches on their conceptual mind, which she calls focusing, or lets it idle. "Thinking is not an automatic function. In any hour and issue of his life, man is free to think or to evade that effort" ("The Objectivist Ethics", 1961).

**Imitation is counterfeit cognition.** Rand fully accepts that children pick up "sounds and motions" from others without understanding them and without any reward. She calls this the lowest rung of a mind, workable for a while and fatal if a person stays there.

**A person is never merely shaped.** Her one extended engagement with experimental psychology is a 1972 attack on B. F. Skinner. She grants the environment enormous influence over a small child, and refuses the step from influence to determination.

## What Rand says about thinking, imitation and the child

**The choice to think, or to drift.** A person can focus his mind "or he can unfocus it and let himself drift in a semiconscious daze, merely reacting to any chance stimulus of the immediate moment, at the mercy of his undirected sensory-perceptual mechanism and of any random, associational connections it might happen to make" ("The Objectivist Ethics"). Rand does not deny that people sometimes behave like stimulus-and-response machines. She says that is what an unfocused mind looks like.

**Imitation as a way to survive without thinking.** "If some men do not choose to think, but survive by imitating and repeating, like trained animals, the routine of sounds and motions they learned from others, never making an effort to understand their own work, it still remains true that their survival is made possible only by those who did choose to think" ("The Objectivist Ethics"). Children begin this way. A child who stops at imitation adopts "something as close to a parrot's psycho-epistemology as a human brain can come – learning, not concepts nor words, but strings of sounds whose referents are not the facts of reality, but the facial expressions and emotional vibrations of their elders" (*Introduction to Objectivist Epistemology*, chapter 3). <Term>Psycho-epistemology</Term> is Rand's term for a mind's habitual method of working.

**Values absorbed by osmosis.** A person "chooses his values by a conscious process of thought – or accepts them by default, by subconscious associations, on faith, on someone's authority, by some form of social osmosis or blind imitation" ("The Objectivist Ethics"). Emotions follow: they "are produced by man's premises, held consciously or subconsciously". Hence her refrain, "Emotions are not tools of cognition."

**The nursery.** "The Comprachicos" (1970) is Rand's only extended treatment of children of the Bobo age, and it is a polemic against what she called Progressive nursery schools. "At the age of three, when his mind is almost as plastic as his bones, when his need and desire to know are more intense than they will ever be again, a child is delivered ... into the midst of a pack of children as helplessly ignorant as himself." "Animals, infants and small children are exceedingly sensitive to emotional vibrations: it is their chief means of cognition." Young children "hero-worship and try to emulate an older brother or sister", seeking "cognitive superiors, people who know". A child left to adjust to a group "gets the nature of the game – wordlessly, by repetition, imitation and emotional osmosis, long before he can form the concepts to identify it". He adjusts "to incomprehensible demands, to unwanted favors ... and to the overwhelming, overpowering presence of Whim as the ruler of everything". And: "The stronger his fear, the more aggressive his behavior." The children who resist, "unable to give in, unable to fake", are her "heroic little martyrs".

**Automatization, not conditioning.** Rand has her own account of habit. "All learning involves a process of automatizing, i.e., of first acquiring knowledge by fully conscious, focused attention and observation, then of establishing mental connections which make that knowledge automatic" (*Introduction to Objectivist Epistemology*, chapter 7). "The first five or six years of a child's life are crucial to his cognitive development. They determine, not the content of his mind, but its method of functioning" ("The Comprachicos"). Adults "can help or hinder him in this crucial process", but "it has to be acquired by a child's own effort".

**Against Skinner.** Reviewing *Beyond Freedom and Dignity* in 1972, Rand summarised its claim that "'Inside his skin,' man is totally determined by his environment ... and totally malleable", and then asked the question she thought Skinner never answered: "by what means or process do these 'reinforcers' affect man's actions? In the whole of the book, no answer is given." A reinforcer, she argued, can only work on a mind that notices, remembers and evaluates, and Skinner "denies the existence of memory" by calling it "storage". She approved a reviewer's phrase for behaviourism: "psychology without a psyche" ("The Stimulus and the Response").

**Heroes, not rules.** Children need "the image of a moral person", because a child cannot yet reason out principles and cannot read the moral character of the adults around him: "to abstract, identify and judge their moral characteristics is a task beyond his capacity". "If man is to gain and keep a moral stature, he needs an image of the ideal, from the first thinking day of his life to the last" ("Art and Moral Treason", 1965).

## Reading the study through this tradition

**Learning with no reward: Rand sides with Bandura against reinforcement.** No child and no model was rewarded, and all but two of the 24 children in the aggressive condition reproduced the model's unusual acts. Rand would take this as evidence for her side of the argument with Skinner. The children watched, retained and reproduced acts with no consequence attached, which requires exactly what Skinner "denies": memory, and a consciousness that notices. She would then turn on the paper's own hypotheses, which explain copying through a general habit built from past rewards (see the [question page](/cases/bobo-doll/question/)). The study, she would say, disproved its own vocabulary and should have said so.

**The copied phrases: a parrot's psycho-epistemology.** About a third of the children repeated "He keeps coming back for more" and "He sure is a tough fella", phrases no other child said. For Rand this is a clean demonstration of pre-conceptual copying: sounds and motions taken over whole, with the adult's emotional charge, before the child can say what they mean. She would also say the children had every reason to copy an adult, since young children seek "cognitive superiors, people who know". She agrees with Bandura against the psychoanalysts that a child needs no emotional bond to copy, only an adult who seems to know what he is doing. She parts from him on status: Bandura treats this as a general theory of learning, Rand as the lowest rung, the mode of a mind not yet, or not now, thinking.

**The frustration step: an adult acting on whim.** Every child was allowed to start on the "very best toys" and then told, with no reason given, that they were kept for other children. Rand would say the researchers reproduced in miniature the lesson she accused nursery schools of teaching: adults give and withdraw at random, and no answer is given to "why?". She would reject the frustration-aggression "push" as a drive. In her nursery picture, aggression is what a bewildered, frightened child does: "The stronger his fear, the more aggressive his behavior." She would agree that the step mattered and changed the child's state, and clash with the assumption that it was a neutral manipulation rather than an act with a meaning for the child.

**The mirror and the five-second counts: psychology without a psyche.** The method counts motions and leaves out the mind that produced them. Yet the children's own remarks show judging minds at work: "That's not the way for a lady to behave"; "He's a good fighter like Daddy" (p. 581). For Rand, "One must never fail to pronounce moral judgment" ("How Does One Lead a Rational Life in an Irrational Society?", 1962), and four-year-olds who do so unprompted are the most interesting thing in the room. Peikoff states the underlying point: "percepts do not invoke emotions; only percepts as interpreted and evaluated can do so" (*Objectivism*, p. 204). The paper's assumption that behaviour is "the specific acts that can be counted", described on the [paradigm page](/cases/bobo-doll/paradigm/), is exactly what she called psychology without a psyche.

**The sex-typing remarks: premises absorbed by osmosis.** The children had already taken in, from the adults around them, a premise about what men and women do, and the premise governed what they copied. Rand would agree with the paper that "appropriateness" filtered the copying, since that is a claim about evaluation, not reflex. She would not call it "sex-typing" as a fact about the children. She would call it an unexamined premise accepted "on someone's authority". Her own views on masculinity and femininity are not in the texts used here, and this page attributes none.

**Influence is not determination: the two who did not copy.** The [paradigm page](/cases/bobo-doll/paradigm/) notes that the study treats children as shaped mainly by their environment. Rand grants adults enormous influence over a small child; that is the whole burden of "The Comprachicos". What she refuses is the step to "totally malleable". She would point to the two children who watched the attack and did not reproduce it, to the many zeros in the scores, and to the calm-model children who sat quietly for twice as long, and say that the study has no concept for a child who declines. Peikoff calls the frame she rejects "the false alternative of 'nature or nurture'" (*Objectivism*, p. 204): "The first turns man into a helpless byproduct of matter; the second, of other people's minds." She would agree with Bandura's later move towards attention, retention and the child as an active processor. She would clash with the 1961 paper's placing of the cause outside the child.

## Where it agrees, and where it clashes

Rand and the paper agree that children acquire new acts by watching, with no reward; that an emotional bond with the model is not needed; that adults have great power over what small children take in; and that the deliberate use of unusual acts made exact copying visible, which she would prize, since for her the first task of a mind is to identify what a thing is.

They clash on three points. Copying is the floor of cognition, not a general account of learning. A child who copies is doing something, judging the adult, reading the room, and the method has no way to see it. And a theory that places the cause outside the child cannot explain the child who refuses. Her one objection to the design is the same as her objection to nursery schools: an adult who gives and takes on a whim teaches a child that the world is arbitrary.

## Questions it would put to the researchers

- What did each child take the adult to be doing: playing, demonstrating, giving permission, misbehaving? Your observers recorded acts, not meanings. Why did you not ask?
- Two of your 24 children watched the attack and did not reproduce it. What were they doing instead, and what does your theory say about them?
- You wrote your hypotheses in the language of reinforcement histories and then found that reinforcement was unnecessary. Why keep the vocabulary?
- Why did you take the "best toys" away with no reason given? What does a four-year-old learn from an adult who gives and takes on whim?
- The children judged the models aloud. Why is judgement an anecdote on page 581 rather than a variable?
- What is the difference, in your categories, between hitting a doll and hitting a person, and did any child show that he knew it?

## Cautions

- **Rand never mentions Bandura, the Bobo doll or social learning theory.** Everything on this page is an application of her general views. Her only direct engagement with experimental psychology is the Skinner review, which is a polemic, not a scholarly critique.
- **Rand does not deny conditioning or habit.** She describes "automatization" and uses the word "conditions" herself. Her claim is that behaviourism cannot say how a reinforcer works without a mind.
- **"Force" means coercion of persons.** Her cardinal political wrong is to "initiate the use of physical force against others". Hitting an inflatable doll is not force in her sense. She wrote nothing about play aggression.
- **Do not call the Stanford nursery "Progressive".** The paper does not say what kind of nursery it was.
- **Her claims about early childhood cite no evidence.** "The Comprachicos" asserts that lost early cognitive development "cannot be made up" and cites no source. Treat her nursery account as a philosopher's argument, not as developmental science.
- **Tone.** Rand's prose about nursery schools is deliberately violent. Quote sparingly and frame it as polemic.
