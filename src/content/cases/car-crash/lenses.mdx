The outline asks you to interpret the study through at least three other perspectives. This guide covers all nine, so you can see the whole range before choosing. Each lens page follows the same order: the lens in brief, how it reads the study, what it values, what it questions, evidence from that tradition, and a study it would design instead.

If the perspectives are new to you, read the short course-wide introduction on [the nine lenses](/lenses/) page first.

## The nine readings side by side

Use this table to compare, not to replace the lens pages. Each row is one lens.

| Lens | What it sees in the study | Its sharpest question | The study it would run instead |
|---|---|---|---|
| Behavioural | A speed estimate as verbal behaviour, controlled partly by the film and partly by the words of the question | What do the questioner's words and reactions do to a witness's answers, with no need to talk about memory? | The same films, with the audience, the questioner's reactions and rewards for accuracy varied |
| Cognitive | Information supplied after the event merged with what was seen, until the witness cannot tell the sources apart | Is the original memory replaced, or only harder to tell apart from the suggestion? | Free accounts first, warnings, a "don't know" option and a source question for every "yes" |
| Psychoanalytic | Memory rebuilt for reasons of meaning and emotion, and a research programme that became its main opponent in the memory wars | What does a memory do for the person, and can a laboratory study of broken glass speak about memories of trauma? | Repeated free accounts by people who were in real accidents, without planting anything false |
| Biological | A word arriving while the memory is still being stored, and true and false memories that look alike in the brain | What would stress, attention, sleep and age have done to the result? | Eye tracking, stress hormones, sleep records and three age groups, with a frightening and a calm film |
| Humanistic | People treated as recording devices to be checked for errors, with their own accounts set aside | What does remembering mean to the person, and how does it feel to be told your memory is wrong? | Free accounts before and after, a full explanation, and participants commenting on the interpretation |
| Social | A leading question as something one person does to another, from someone with authority | How much of the effect comes from the questioner's status, the wish to cooperate and the people a witness talks to? | A co-witness who says "smash" or "bump", a student or a uniformed questioner, and spoken versus private answers |
| Cultural | An effect that rests on an English verb, an American road and miles per hour | Would the effect hold, and in what form, in other languages and other traffic? | An Indian collision film, verbs rated by speakers of Hindi, Tamil and English, and bilinguals tested in both languages |
| Critical | Research on unreliable memory as research on whose word is believed | Who holds the power to frame questions, and who is helped or harmed when the findings reach court? | Real police statements and testimony coded by who asked, with survivors and exonerees as co-designers |
| Decolonial | A colonial law of witnesses that survives in 2023, and Indian students learning about memory from American students | Whose idea of a reliable witness is built into law and research, and what do Indian traditions say about memory? | An Indian-led study in Indian languages, with "seen, heard or inferred" questions and community fact-finding studied as testimony |

## How the lenses connect

The lenses are not nine separate verdicts. Some agree with each other and some disagree. Noticing which is which will make your journal stronger.

- **Behavioural and cognitive disagree about whether memory is needed at all.** The paper itself offers a response-bias explanation, a push on the answer with the memory unchanged, and then argues for a change in memory (p. 586). The behavioural lens takes the first side seriously; the cognitive lens takes the second. Experiment 2, with its week's delay, is the evidence each side has to explain.
- **Cognitive and biological agree on reconstruction, and add different detail.** The cognitive lens describes how information is combined and how sources are confused. The biological lens shows that a recalled memory can be changed while it is being stored again, and that true and false memories involve much the same brain activity. Neither can yet show, for one person, which memory is true.
- **Psychoanalytic and humanistic both say memory carries meaning, but they draw different lessons.** Both object that the study treats memory as a record of facts such as speed and glass. Psychoanalysis looks for the emotional work that a memory does, and has been the main opponent of false-memory research in the argument about recovered memories. The humanistic lens asks how it feels to be a witness who is not believed.
- **Social and critical both put the questioner in the picture.** The social lens studies how authority, cooperation and other witnesses shape answers. The critical lens asks who holds the power to frame questions, and whose interests are served when memory research is used in court. Both point out that a printed questionnaire removes the person who asks.
- **Cultural and decolonial ask different questions about the same English verb.** The cultural lens asks whether the effect holds in other languages and traffic, and tests it. The decolonial lens asks why Indian law and Indian psychology students rely on rules and findings from other places, and what Indian traditions of knowledge already said about the difference between seeing, hearing and inferring.
- **The replication evidence cuts across all of them.** As the [importance page](/cases/car-crash/importance/) explains, the general finding, that wording and later information can change what witnesses report, is accepted by almost every expert. The specific verb effect has not been reliably replicated. Every lens now has to explain a principle that is secure and a famous example that is not.

These three lenses all stay inside the study's own world of controlled experiments on individuals. The cognitive lens is the study's home perspective, and the biological and behavioural lenses mostly argue about mechanism: whether the memory changed, how the brain stores it, or whether only the answer moved. The analysis would show little of the range the outline asks for, and would miss the people and institutions around the witness.

A stronger set includes at least one lens that steps outside the laboratory. The social lens brings back the questioner and the other witnesses that a printed questionnaire removes. The critical lens asks who is believed when this research reaches a courtroom, and who benefits. The cultural or decolonial lens asks whether an effect built on an English verb and American roads holds in Indian languages, traffic and courts, and whose rules about witnesses Indian law follows. This shows that you understand how paradigms disagree about what counts as evidence, and why it matters.
