## How this guide was checked

Hock's chapter was used only to get oriented. Every number, quotation and procedural detail in Sections A and E was then checked against the original 1961 paper, including its two tables. Later studies were checked against their original papers where these could be read in full, and against abstracts or reliable summaries where they could not. Where a claim could not be verified, the guide either leaves it out or says so.

The ethics page was checked against the codes it cites, read in their current published texts, and against the original paper. Every quotation on the philosophy zone pages was located in the edition listed below (or, for Mead, in the Mead Project transcription of the 1934 text; Heidegger is cited only through the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy) and carries a locator (Bekker number, Akademie page, section or page) so that it can be verified. Where a famous line could not be found in those texts, the page says so.

## Corrections to Hock's account

If you use Hock, note these differences from the original paper. Each one is explained on the page linked.

- The control-group scores for copied physical aggression are swapped between boys and girls. ([Findings](/cases/bobo-doll/findings/))
- Figures such as "38.2" and "12.7" are sums of two group averages, not averages; totals such as "270 violent acts" are not counts. ([Findings](/cases/bobo-doll/findings/))
- Hypothesis 3 is explained by reinforcement history in the paper, not by identification. ([Question and hypotheses](/cases/bobo-doll/question/))
- The calm-model results are not simply "inconclusive": the calm male model reduced aggression, the calm female model did not. ([Findings](/cases/bobo-doll/findings/))
- The paper says the sex-of-child prediction was "only partially confirmed", not "strongly supported". ([Findings](/cases/bobo-doll/findings/))
- Children were matched individually in triplets, not by group averages; the experimenter stayed in the test room; the test-room Bobo doll was 3 feet tall. ([Method](/cases/bobo-doll/method/))
- The 1963 film study did not find live > film > cartoon, and had no calm-model group. ([Why it matters](/cases/bobo-doll/importance/))
- Bandura (1965) did not find more copying after the model was rewarded than after no consequences; its key finding is the difference between learning and performance. ([Why it matters](/cases/bobo-doll/importance/))
- The 1972 follow-up study is by Hanratty, O'Neal and Sulzer, not "O'Neil". The study with a person dressed as a clown is Hanratty, Liebert, Morris and Fernandez (1969).

## The original study

Bandura, A., Ross, D., & Ross, S. A. (1961). Transmission of aggression through imitation of aggressive models. *Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology, 63*(3), 575-582. https://doi.org/10.1037/h0045925. Read in full, with both tables, via [Classics in the History of Psychology](http://psychclassics.yorku.ca/Bandura/bobo.htm). Page numbers follow the page markers in that transcription. Obvious typing errors in the transcription, such as "pick up" for "picked up", are corrected silently in quotations.
