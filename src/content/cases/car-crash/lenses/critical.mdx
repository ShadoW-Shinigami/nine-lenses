## The lens in brief

Critical psychology asks how psychological knowledge is shaped by power, and whose interests it serves. Dennis Fox and Isaac Prilleltensky, who edited an influential introduction to the field in 1997, argued that mainstream psychology often treats social problems as problems inside individuals, and so leaves the institutions that create them unexamined.

Two ideas are useful here. <Term>Discursive psychology</Term> studies what people do with their talk, rather than treating talk as a window onto fixed inner states. Derek Edwards and Jonathan Potter (1992) applied it to memory. They argued that accounts of the past "need to be examined as contextualized and variable productions that perform pragmatic and rhetorical work; no one version can be taken as a person's real memory". The second idea is <Term>epistemic injustice</Term>, a term from the philosopher Miranda Fricker (2007). It describes the wrong done to someone as a knower, for example when a person is given less credibility than they deserve because of prejudice about who they are.

This lens stands far from the study. Loftus and Palmer treat memory as a representation inside each person that can be checked against a known truth. The critical lens asks who gets to decide what the truth was, and who benefits from a science of doubting witnesses.

## How this lens reads the study

**Power lies in the question.** The whole study rests on one fact: the researchers chose the words, and the students did not know. The paper calls the speed question an interrogation (pp. 586-587). Outside the laboratory, the people who frame questions are police officers, lawyers and judges. The study shows how much power that role carries. A critical reading notices that the research then studied the witness, the person with less power, as the source of error. It did not study the questioner, whose words caused the error.

**Memory research is research about credibility.** The practical message of the study is that honest witnesses can be wrong. Whether that message helps or harms depends on whose testimony is being doubted. It can protect a suspect against a witness who was led by police questions. It can also be used to cast doubt on a woman or child reporting abuse. The science does not decide which. The people who use it do.

**Who is wrongly convicted.** The Innocence Project, an American organisation that uses DNA evidence to overturn wrongful convictions, studied the first 375 DNA <Term>exonerations</Term> in the United States (1989-2020). An exoneration is a case in which a convicted person was later proved innocent. The Innocence Project found that 69% of these cases involved eyewitness misidentification. Its current page lists "cross-racial identification" and "suggestive police practices" among the causes. The <Term>cross-race effect</Term> is the finding that people recognise faces of their own racial group more accurately than faces of other groups. A review of 39 articles, with nearly 5,000 participants, confirmed it (Meissner and Brigham, 2001). A study in Malaysia found the same bias among Malay, Chinese, Indian and white participants, "implying that growing up in a profoundly multiracial society does not necessarily eliminate" it (Wong, Stephen and Keeble, 2020). So recognition errors do not fall evenly: who is looking at whom matters. Note, though, that these figures are mostly about identifying the wrong person, a different problem from the leading questions Loftus and Palmer studied.

**The expert in the courtroom.** Loftus became one of the best-known expert witnesses on memory in the United States. In 2021 she told a court that she had testified about 300 times, and only once for the prosecution (as reported by Reuters). Her supporters see this as science protecting the accused, who face the power of the state. Her critics point to her testimony for the defence in widely reported American trials for sexual offences, such as the trials of Harvey Weinstein (2020) and Ghislaine Maxwell (2021). They argue that general laboratory findings were being used to cast doubt on particular victims. Some memory researchers raised a scientific objection early on. Michael McCloskey and Maria Zaragoza (1985) wrote that "there is insufficient scientific basis for expert testimony to the effect that postevent information may alter a witness's memory". The critical lens does not judge Loftus as a person. It asks how laboratory knowledge about broken glass became power over real people's credibility.

**The memory wars and survivors of abuse.** In the 1990s, research on false memory became central to disputes about adults who reported recovering memories of childhood abuse. The False Memory Syndrome Foundation "was launched in 1992" and "officially dissolved on December 31, 2019" (Salter and Blizard, 2022). It was founded by Pamela and Peter Freyd after their adult daughter, the psychologist Jennifer Freyd, alleged that her father had abused her as a child. Loftus is reported to have served on its scientific advisory board. In 2022, Salter and Blizard edited a special issue of the *Journal of Trauma & Dissociation* on false memory. Their call for papers argued that "the concept of false memory is still used to discredit the legal testimony of trauma survivors". Earlier, a feminist review by Carolyn Enns and colleagues (1995) examined the debate about delayed memories of abuse, including "the historical context in which this controversy occurs", and made recommendations "for future research, training, and social change". The critical lens asks whose interests were served when the doubt fell mainly on women's and children's accounts.

**Courts can use memory science either way.** The Indian Supreme Court, in *Bharwada Bhoginbhai Hirjibhai v. State of Gujarat* (1983), wrote that a witness "cannot be expected to possess a photographic memory", that "It is unrealistic to expect a witness to be a human tape recorder", and that people estimate time "by guess work". This is close to what Loftus and Palmer found. But the Court used these points to set aside minor inconsistencies and uphold a conviction for rape. The same judgment also made broad claims about how "a girl or a woman in India" behaves, which rest on assumption, not evidence. Memory science entered the courtroom as a tool, and its meaning depended on the case and the court.

## What this lens values in the study

The study shows, with unusual clarity, that the person asking the questions can change the answers. That is a critical insight about power: those who frame the questions shape the record. The research it started has helped to free innocent people who were convicted on mistaken testimony, often after suggestive police practices. It has also pushed police in several countries towards open questions that give witnesses more control over their own accounts.

## What it would question

- Studying the witness as the source of error, rather than the questioner and the institution.
- The use of laboratory findings about trivial details, such as broken glass in a film, to doubt particular people's memories of serious harm.
- Whose testimony is most often doubted, and whose is believed: by gender, race, caste, class and age.
- The absence of any information about the participants' identities, which makes it impossible to ask whom the results describe.
- An expert role that has been used mostly on one side of the courtroom.

## Evidence from this tradition

**Memory as social talk.** Edwards and Potter (1992) criticised a famous study by Ulric Neisser. Neisser had studied the testimony of John Dean to the American Senate committee that investigated the Watergate scandal. He concluded that Dean's memory was wrong in detail but right in gist. Edwards and Potter then analysed newspaper reports of a disputed briefing by a British minister, Nigel Lawson. They argued that what people say about the past is shaped by what the talk is doing: defending oneself, blaming others, persuading. On this view, a witness's report is an action in a social setting rather than a readout of a memory.

**Credibility and prejudice.** Fricker's (2007) work on epistemic injustice gave philosophers and psychologists a way to describe how prejudice reduces the credibility given to some speakers. Salter and Blizard's 2022 call for papers applied a similar argument to false-memory research, and noted that the doubt now reaches "adult victims of sexual assault, survivors of war crimes, and First Nations peoples reporting abuse by colonial powers".

**Who is misidentified.** Meissner and Brigham (2001) and Wong and colleagues (2020) show that recognition errors depend on who is looking at whom. The Innocence Project's figures show that eyewitness error has contributed to many wrongful convictions.

**Police records in India.** The official report on road accidents in India admits that police record accident data "after returning depending on his memory" (Ministry of Road Transport and Highways, 2025, p. 105). The same report says that the data are "often affected by the value judgement and biases of the policeman". Police in India also write witness statements in their own words, and the witness does not sign them. The [India page](/cases/car-crash/india/) explains these rules. A critical lens notes that the first written version of a witness's memory is often produced by a person with power over the witness.

**Evidence against the lens.** The same research has helped free innocent people from prison. It would be wrong to treat doubt about memory as always serving the powerful. A sceptical review of false-memory studies also cuts both ways: it found that fully formed false memories of childhood events are rarer than some reports suggest, about 15% of participants (Brewin and Andrews, 2017), but they do occur. The critical lens should not swing from "always doubt" to "always believe". Its question is who decides, and on what grounds.

## A study this lens would design

1. Collect real police statements and later court testimony from the same witnesses, with permission and with names removed.
2. Record who asked each question, in what language, and with what wording.
3. Code where leading questions appear, and whether they come more often from police, prosecutors or defence lawyers.
4. Compare how often witnesses' accounts change, and in which direction, for witnesses of different gender, caste, religion and class.
5. Involve survivors of crime and people who have been wrongly convicted in designing the coding scheme and interpreting the results.

**Aim:** not to measure memory in a laboratory, but to show where in the real legal process questions shape testimony, and whose testimony is most often reshaped, doubted or believed.

**Prediction:** leading questions will be most common at the police stage and in cross-examination, and the accounts of witnesses with less social power, by gender, caste or class, will be reshaped or doubted more often.
