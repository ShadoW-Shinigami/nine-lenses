This page follows Section D, part 8, of the outline. Questions of power ask who had the authority to design the study, define its terms and tell its story, and who did not.

## Who is speaking?

**A famous professor and a young colleague at an elite university.** Leon Festinger was already one of the most prominent social psychologists in the United States, a professor at Stanford with a grant from the National Science Foundation, the federal agency that funds science (p. 203). His 1957 book was the theory being tested. James Carlsmith was at the very start of his career. The paper is written in their voice, and in the voice of their discipline.

**Researchers who controlled what everyone knew.** In this study, power lay above all in information. The researchers knew the real purpose. The course instructor, the experimenter, the interviewer and the young woman each knew part of it. The students knew nothing true about what was happening to them until the end. Every step, from the class announcement weeks earlier to the interviewer's questions, was arranged so that the students would believe a false story.

**The vocabulary is the researchers'.** The paper calls the participants "subjects", or "Ss" for short, as was normal in 1959. It decides what counts as an "opinion" (a number on a scale) and what counts as "behaviour" (the lie). It calls the woman who played the waiting participant "the girl" throughout, although she was an undergraduate hired for the job.

## Who is being studied?

**Young men meeting a course requirement.** The 71 participants were male introductory psychology students who had to take part in experiments to complete their course. They chose this experiment from a list, but the paper mentions no way to meet the requirement without taking part in some experiment. Students in this position are a convenient, cheap and available group for researchers. The social psychologist David Sears (1986) called this the problem of a science built on "college sophomores in the laboratory".

**Students treated as sources of numbers.** Each student was "first encouraged to talk" about each question before giving a rating (p. 206). The paper reports only the ratings. Whatever the students said about the tasks, and about why they had agreed to help, is not reported. Their conversations with the woman were recorded and scored on five scales, but not one sentence from them is quoted, apart from one typical reply.

**Students who resisted became exclusions.** Among the 11 students whose data were set aside, three refused to be hired, two told the woman the truth, and one wanted her phone number so he could explain things to her later. These are students who refused, partly or fully, to deceive another student. In the paper they appear as reasons for discarding data. From another angle, they are the most interesting participants in the study: they show that compliance was not automatic, and that some students felt strongly enough about honesty to break the script.

## Whose voices are absent?

| Absent voice | What it might have added |
|---|---|
| The students themselves | Their own reasons for agreeing to lie, what they felt afterwards, and what the open part of the interview revealed |
| The students who refused or told the truth | How some people resist a polite request from authority, and what honesty meant to them |
| The young woman who played the waiting participant | What it was like to be lied to by dozens of male students in turn, and to hear what they said to her. She is unnamed, and the paper never asks |
| Women as participants | Whether the effect differs for women, and whether a woman lying to a man would feel the same |
| Students not at an elite university, and people who are not students | Whether people with less money see $1 and $20 the same way, and whether people with less trust in authority would comply at all |
| People outside the United States | Whether saying one thing and believing another feels like a clash at all. The paper itself says "at least in our culture" |
| The Indian communities whose rumours inspired the theory | Festinger said the theory began with data from Prasad's study of rumours after the 1934 Bihar earthquake, but his foreword does not name Prasad. The people who spread the rumours appear only as data in an American book |

## Why this matters for the study's claims

These points are about evidence as well as ethics. They limit what the study can claim.

- **A study that never reports what participants said cannot rule out other explanations.** If the students had been asked why they agreed, and whether they had changed their minds, their answers could have supported or challenged the dissonance account. The researchers chose not to use that evidence.
- **A study that removes resisters may overstate compliance.** If the people who refused are left out, the remaining group is, by selection, more willing to go along. That can make an effect look cleaner than it is.
- **A study of 60 young American men cannot show that the process is universal.** Later research in Japan and Canada, described on the [cultural lens](/cases/cognitive-dissonance/lenses/cultural/) page, found that whether and when people feel dissonance depends on culture.
- **A study that depends on deception shows how power works in the laboratory as well as how minds work.** The students complied with a polite request from a man in authority. The study's result depends on that compliance, but the paper does not treat it as something to explain.

Power also shaped what happened to the study afterwards. It became one of the most taught experiments in psychology, and Festinger one of its most cited figures, while the young woman, the students who refused and the Indian rumour studies dropped out of the story. The [decolonial lens](/cases/cognitive-dissonance/lenses/decolonial/) page looks at that last point more closely.

The researchers did explain each exclusion, which is honest reporting. But the number removed matters less than who was removed and what their removal does to the story. Six of the 11 refused the money, told the woman the truth, or wanted to put things right with her. In other words, they resisted the experimenter's request to mislead another student. Treating their responses as unusable data is a decision made by the researchers, and it defines the "normal" participant as one who complies.

It also matters for the evidence. Seven of the exclusions came from the $1 group and none from the Control group. A power-and-representation analysis asks whose behaviour counts as data and whose is set aside, and shows that this choice can shape the results.
