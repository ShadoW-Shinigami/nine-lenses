## The tradition in brief

Ayn Rand (1905 to 1982) was born in St Petersburg, left the Soviet Union for the United States in 1926, and became famous as the author of *The Fountainhead* (1943) and *Atlas Shrugged* (1957). She called herself both novelist and philosopher, and named her system Objectivism. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy notes that she has a vast popular readership but that "only a few professional philosophers have taken her work seriously"; a small scholarly literature has grown since the 2000s. This page treats her as an influential and combative thinker outside the academy, and quotes her own words.

Three ideas run through this page.

**Evasion is the basic vice.** For Rand, the root of all wrongdoing is <Term>evasion</Term>: deliberately not looking at something you know is there. It is a choice, aimed at a specific fact, driven by a feeling the person prefers to the fact.

**Honesty is the refusal to fake reality.** Rand widens honesty from "do not lie to others" to "do not pretend, to anyone, including yourself, that facts are other than they are". <Term>Integrity</Term> is the matching virtue in action: making what you do follow what you know.

**Emotions are not tools of cognition.** A feeling tells you that one of your premises has been touched. It does not tell you which belief is true.

## What Rand says about evasion, honesty and money

Each idea below starts with Rand's own words, then explains them in plain English, then says why they matter for this study.

**Evasion and the blank-out.** In Galt's speech Rand defined her basic vice as "the act of blanking out, the willful suspension of one's consciousness, the refusal to think – not blindness, but the refusal to see; not ignorance, but the refusal to know. It is the act of unfocusing your mind and inducing an inner fog to escape the responsibility of judgment – on the unstated premise that a thing will not exist if only you refuse to identify it". Peikoff adds the mechanism: "Evasion, by contrast, is an active process aimed at a specific content. The evader does expend effort; he purposefully directs his attention away from a given fact" (*Objectivism*, p. 61). And the hinge for this study: a feeling "is merely a precondition, not a cause or explanation; the choice to capitulate to it is irreducible. No matter what his emotions, a sane man retains the power to face facts" (p. 62).

In plain words: <Term>evasion</Term> is Rand's name for deliberately not looking at something you know is there. It is not ignorance, which is not knowing, and not blindness, which is not being able to see. It is a choice to keep a fact out of focus, driven by a feeling you would rather keep than face. Peikoff's gloss adds that evasion is active and specific: the evader works at it, and aims it at one particular fact. Rand called it the root of all wrongdoing, because every other vice begins with a refusal to see something. The "unstated premise" she mentions is the childish hope that a thing will go away if you refuse to name it.

Why it matters here: Rand's account differs from Festinger's on one point, what the discomfort does. For Festinger the discomfort is a drive that moves belief. For Rand it is a feeling that the person chooses to give in to, and "the choice to capitulate to it is irreducible".

**Rationalisation.** "Rationalization is a cover-up, a process of providing one's emotions with a false identity, of giving them spurious explanations and justifications – in order to hide one's motives, not just from others, but primarily from oneself." It is "a process not of perceiving reality, but of attempting to make reality fit one's emotions" ("Philosophical Detection", 1974). Smith's gloss describes the usual case: "typically, people ignore their awareness of the reasons not to take an action or they artfully distort those reasons by redescribing what they are doing" (*Ayn Rand's Normative Ethics*, p. 63).

In plain words: a <Term>rationalisation</Term> is a respectable reason invented for a feeling whose real source you do not want to admit. Rand's definition has a twist: the main person being fooled is yourself. Because a feeling arrives all at once, it is easy to forget that it was produced by beliefs you hold, and to treat it instead as a fact that needs explaining. The rationaliser then explains it with whatever makes him look good. Smith's gloss describes the everyday version. People rarely do wrong while thinking "this is wrong"; they push the reasons against it out of view, or redescribe what they are doing so that it no longer looks wrong.

Why it matters here: a student's "the tasks were interesting after all" is, for Rand, a rationalisation in her exact sense, a false identity given to the discomfort of having lied for a dollar.

**Honesty.** One "must never attempt to fake reality in any manner" ("The Objectivist Ethics", 1961). Galt: "Honesty is the recognition of the fact that the unreal is unreal and can have no value, that neither love nor fame nor cash is a value if obtained by fraud – that an attempt to gain a value by deceiving the mind of others is an act of raising your victims to a position higher than reality, where you become a pawn of their blindness." Honesty, he adds, "is not a social duty, not a sacrifice for the sake of others, but the most profoundly selfish virtue man can practice". Rand allowed one exception, which Peikoff states: "Lying is absolutely wrong – under certain conditions. It is wrong when a man does it in the attempt to obtain a value. But, to take a different kind of case, lying to protect one's values from criminals is not wrong" (*Objectivism*, p. 275). White lies, told to spare feelings, are out (Smith, p. 100).

In plain words: Rand widens honesty from "do not tell lies" to a rule against pretending, to anyone, including yourself, that things are other than they are. Her reason is unusual. Honesty is not a sacrifice you make for other people; it is in your own interest, because pretending does not change the facts, and a person who acts on a false picture of the world ends up at the mercy of the people he fooled, "a pawn of their blindness". She allowed one exception: you may lie to someone who is using force against you, such as a robber, because he is trying to take what is not his. You may not lie to gain something, and you may not tell white lies to spare feelings.

Why it matters here: by Rand's test, the students' lie was told to gain something, a dollar and an experimenter's goodwill, so it was not permitted; and the experimenters' lies were told to gain data, so they were not permitted either.

**Integrity.** "Integrity is loyalty to one's convictions and values; it is the policy of acting in accordance with one's values, of expressing, upholding and translating them into practical reality" ("The Ethics of Emergencies", 1963). Galt: "Integrity is the recognition of the fact that you cannot fake your consciousness, just as honesty is the recognition of the fact that you cannot fake existence ... he may permit no breach between body and mind, between action and thought, between his life and his convictions".

In plain words: <Term>integrity</Term>, for Rand, is honesty in action. Once you have judged something to be true or good, you act on it, rather than saying one thing and doing another. The Galt quotation puts it as a kind of wholeness: a person is one entity, mind and body, so there should be no gap between what he knows and what he does. Rand allowed ordinary trade-offs, such as taking a job or following an employer's reasonable instructions. What she did not allow was acting against one's own judgement to please others. Integrity runs in one direction: conviction first, action second.

Why it matters here: the study found that action came first and conviction followed. Rand would call that integrity run backwards.

**Emotions and contradictions.** "Emotions are not tools of cognition; to be guided by whims – by desires whose source, nature and meaning one does not know, is to turn oneself into a blind robot, operated by unknowable demons (by one's stale evasions)" ("The Objectivist Ethics"). And on contradiction, Galt: "A contradiction cannot exist ... To arrive at a contradiction is to confess an error in one's thinking; to maintain a contradiction is to abdicate one's mind and to evict oneself from the realm of reality."

In plain words: Rand's claim that "emotions are not tools of cognition" means that a feeling cannot tell you what is true; it can only tell you what you already believe, because feelings are produced by beliefs. To act on a feeling without knowing where it came from is to be driven by your own past evasions, which is what she means by "unknowable demons". On contradiction she is stricter than Festinger. A contradiction cannot exist in reality, so when you find one in your thinking, one of your beliefs is false and must be found and dropped. Reducing the discomfort by any convenient route is, for her, "to abdicate one's mind".

Why it matters here: Rand and Festinger agree that holding clashing beliefs is intolerable. They disagree about what to do. Festinger's students reduced the clash by the easiest change, and Rand would say that only one change was right: to admit the lie.

**Money and bribes.** Francisco's speech: "Money is the product of virtue, but it will not give you virtue and it will not redeem your vices", and "It's the person who would sell his soul for a nickel, who is loudest in proclaiming his hatred of money". Elsewhere Rand compared the rational mind to a judge who "may not evade the evidence available, nor accept bribes, nor allow any personal feeling, emotion, desire or fear to obstruct his mind's judgment of the facts of reality" ("How Does One Lead a Rational Life in an Irrational Society?", 1962). The <Term>second-hander</Term>, in the words of Howard Roark, the hero of *The Fountainhead*, does not ask "Is this true?" but "Is this what others think is true?", and Rand called a person who treats others' judgement as superior to the facts a "social metaphysician", using Branden's term.

In plain words: Rand admired money when it was earned, as payment for value given, and she despised it when it was used to buy a person's judgement. The second-hander is a person who lives through other people's opinions, asking, in Roark's words, not "Is this true?" but "Is this what others think is true?". Such a person can be bought even more cheaply than with money: with approval. The judge image gives her standard for the mind. A judge who takes bribes, or lets fear or desire sway him, has stopped judging, and so has a person who lets a payment or another person's wish decide what he will say is true.

Why it matters here: Rand would say the real lever in the study was not the dollar but the wish not to disappoint an authority, and that the student who sold his judgement for a dollar was "the person who would sell his soul for a nickel".

## What Rand says about knowledge and belief

Rand's ethics rests on a theory of knowledge, or <Term>epistemology</Term>: an account of what knowledge is, how a belief should be formed, and what counts as evidence. She wrote a book on it, *Introduction to Objectivist Epistemology* (1966 to 1967), and she held that most moral failures begin as failures of thinking. Her epistemology explains why she reads the $1 student so harshly, and it is where she disagrees most sharply with the pragmatists on the next page.

**Existence comes first: knowledge is gained by looking outward.** "The primacy of existence (of reality) is the axiom that existence exists, i.e., that the universe exists independent of consciousness". Its corollary is that "man gains knowledge of reality by looking outward" ("The Metaphysical Versus the Man-Made", 1973). The opposite view, which she calls the primacy of consciousness, holds "that man gains knowledge of reality by looking inward", and it comes from "the inability or unwillingness fully to grasp the difference between one's inner state and the outer world" (same essay). The same essay adds that facts made by people are still facts: "A man's actions did not have to be performed, but, once performed, they are facts of reality."

In plain words: an <Term>axiom</Term> is a starting truth that every argument has to assume, and a corollary is something that follows directly from it. For Rand the world comes first, and the mind's job is to grasp it. A belief is right when it matches what is there, and wrong when it is shaped by what the believer needs or feels. The basic error in knowledge is to let your inner state decide the facts. Note the words "inability or unwillingness": she thought this confusion can be chosen. Her point about acts matters too. A lie told an hour ago cannot be wished away. Once done, it is as much a fact as the weather.

Why it matters here: the $1 student faced two facts, both fixed by the time he met the interviewer. The hour with the pegs had been dull, and he had told the woman it was interesting. The correct move, for Rand, was to let his belief match both facts: "The tasks were dull, and I said otherwise." On the paper's reading, the $1 group's higher rating (+1.35, against the control group's -0.45) did the reverse. It made his verdict on the tasks depend on what he needed to think of himself. That is knowledge gained "by looking inward", the primacy of consciousness on a small scale. The ethical reading above calls this rationalisation. The epistemological reading adds that, besides excusing himself, he adopted a false standard of knowledge for one belief.

**Thinking is a choice, and nothing can force it.** "Man's volition is an attribute of his consciousness (of his rational faculty) and consists in the choice to perceive existence or to evade it" ("The Metaphysical Versus the Man-Made"). "Thinking is not an automatic function. In any hour and issue of his life, man is free to think or to evade that effort" ("The Objectivist Ethics", 1961). And: "Nothing can force a man to think. Others may offer him incentives or impediments, rewards or punishments ... but they cannot order his mind to function" ("The Metaphysical Versus the Man-Made").

In plain words: <Term>volition</Term> means the power to choose, so "volitional" means chosen. Rand places free will in one spot, the decision to keep the mind focused on the facts or to let it drift. She does not claim that a person can simply decide to believe something. Her claim is that a person decides whether to look. Money, praise and pressure can push on what you do, but not on whether you think. So a belief formed under pressure is still the believer's responsibility.

Why it matters here: "forced compliance", the name of the research method, is for Rand wrong in two ways. Nobody was forced to lie, and nobody can be forced to change a belief. The payment could offer an incentive to speak, but it could not reach the student's judgement of the tasks. The difference between the groups, on her view, lies in what each student had to avoid looking at. The $20 student had a true, outward explanation ("I was paid well") and kept his perception of the tasks. The $1 student had no outward explanation, so the only way to protect his picture of himself was to stop looking at his own perception.

**Real consistency is an achievement over the whole of what you know.** "No concept man forms is valid unless he integrates it without contradiction into the total sum of his knowledge" (Galt's speech, in *For the New Intellectual*). Rationality means that "one must never place any value or consideration whatsoever above one's perception of reality" ("The Objectivist Ethics"). And a person may not shrink what he knows to suit himself: "Man cannot know more than he has discovered – and he may not know less than the evidence indicates" (*Introduction to Objectivist Epistemology*, chapter 5). Leonard Peikoff, her chief follower, drew the contrast with the comfort of a quick fit: one "is not using 'logic' if one seeks consistency only among whatever ideas one happens to recall on the spur of the moment" (*Objectivism*, p. 125). "Such consistency is not a given, but an achievement, which requires a methodical, effort-demanding process" (p. 125).

In plain words: Objectivism separates two kinds of consistency. Logical consistency means checking a belief against everything you know, slowly and on purpose. The other kind is the comfort of having the two or three ideas in front of you agree. Rand thought the mind can hold only a few items in focus at once. She illustrated this with a story about crows that can count to about three, which she said she could not vouch for as data (*Introduction*, chapter 7). Objectivists call this limit the "crow epistemology" (Peikoff, *Objectivism*, p. 125). Because the mind's focus is so narrow, real consistency needs method. "He may not know less than the evidence indicates" means you may not "forget" what you saw because it has become awkward.

Why it matters here: this is Rand's sharpest answer to Festinger. His theory says that people seek consistency, and the $1 students did reach it: "I said it was interesting" and "it was interesting" now agree. Rand would say that this is the spur-of-the-moment kind. It made two ideas fit by dropping a third, the student's own memory of the hour. Logical consistency would have kept all three and found the honest link: "I said something false for a dollar." On her view, dissonance theory describes the cheap kind of consistency and calls it the mind's goal.

**Certainty is possible, and a claim with no evidence is not even false.** Peikoff developed this part of the theory. "Is man capable of certainty? Since man has a faculty of knowledge and nonomniscience is no obstacle to its use, there is only one rational answer: certainly" (*Objectivism*, p. 181). "Doubt, rationally exercised, is a temporary, transitional state" (p. 181). For simple questions about what one perceived, "the standard of validation is the direct testimony of your eyes and ears" (p. 180). And claims with no evidence behind them, including "claims based on emotion", are "arbitrary": "An arbitrary statement is neither 'true' nor 'false'" (pp. 163 to 165).

In plain words: Objectivists hold that people can be certain, as long as they can say what the certainty rests on. Being unable to know everything is no bar to knowing some things for sure. For something you saw or felt yourself, your own perception is the best evidence there is. A claim backed by nothing at all is not even worth calling false: it should simply be set aside. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy notes that this "contextual" account of certainty is Peikoff's development, and that Rand herself never states it in these terms.

Why it matters here: on the way to the interview, the experimenter told every student that most students had found the tasks interesting (p. 206). For Rand this was a man-made claim, and "the man-made ... must never be accepted uncritically: it must be judged" ("The Metaphysical Versus the Man-Made"). Against it, the student had the direct testimony of his own eyes and hands for an hour. A report of what unnamed others felt cannot outweigh that, so a student who let it tip his rating had set aside the best evidence he had for some of the weakest.

**Feelings are fast estimates, and introspection is a skill most people do badly.** Emotions "are lightning-like estimates of the things around you, calculated according to your values" ("Philosophy: Who Needs It", 1974). Rand held that people can look inward and learn what they feel and why: "In the field of introspection, the two guiding questions are: 'What do I feel?' and 'Why do I feel it?'" ("Philosophical Detection", 1974). But she thought most people do it poorly. In her epistemology workshops she said that "most people do not even report their inner states correctly – and I don't mean now anything like lying; it is an issue of personal identification, or lying to yourself" (*Introduction*, appendix, an edited transcript of discussion). Peikoff adds that what feels like a clash between thought and feeling is "at root an ideational clash" (*Objectivism*, p. 158), a clash between two ideas.

In plain words: a feeling is a quick conclusion your mind draws from what you already value, so it tells you something about you, not about the world. Unlike Peirce on the next page, Rand thinks introspection works. You can learn what you feel and why, if you ask both questions honestly. Most people ask only the first, and "lying to yourself" corrupts the answer. Peikoff's point is that the discomfort of holding two clashing thoughts is not a raw drive like hunger. It is two judgements in conflict, and a person who looked could name them.

Why it matters here: the interview asked each student how enjoyable the tasks had been, and recorded a number. It never asked why he felt that way. Rand would say that the second question is where an evasion would show, because a rationalisation gives a feeling a false explanation, and the honest answer to "why" would have been "because I said so for a dollar". Peikoff's point also gives the unmeasured discomfort a content. On his view it was not a vague tension but a clash between "I am honest" and "I lied for nothing", which the student could have named if he had looked.

## Reading the study through this tradition

**The lie to the waiting woman: a chosen act, not "forced compliance".** Rand would agree with later researchers that "forced" is the wrong word: the student chose, and three students proved it by refusing. She would also agree with Festinger that the student owns the act and must answer to himself for it. The clash is about what the act was. Festinger treats the lie as morally neutral material, a "cognition" like any other. For Rand it is the moral event of the afternoon, a small act of faking reality to another mind, "an attempt to gain a value by deceiving the mind of others". Peikoff's test settles it: lying is wrong "when a man does it in the attempt to obtain a value", and here the value was a dollar, or twenty, and the experimenter's goodwill. The woman was not a criminal using force, so the one permitted exception does not apply.

**One dollar or twenty: a bribe too small to admit.** The $20 student had a motive he could name, "I was paid", and so kept his verdict on the tasks. The $1 student had sold his judgement for a sum too small to serve as a reason, and rather than face "I lied for a dollar" he rewrote the verdict: the tasks were not so bad. That is Rand's definition of rationalisation, a "false identity" given to a feeling "in order to hide one's motives ... primarily from oneself", and it is her image of "the person who would sell his soul for a nickel". She would accept the phenomenon as real and important, and she would relish the finding that a bigger reward produced less change, because it breaks the behaviourist assumption that money is a simple reinforcer. She would not call the change "dissonance reduction" by a drive. She would call it a choice to place, in Peikoff's phrase quoting her, "an 'I wish' above an 'It is'".

**The professor, the embarrassed experimenter and the course requirement: approval as the lever.** The experimenter said the professor in charge had suggested the student might help, acted unsure of himself, and pressed anyone who hesitated. Rand would say the real pressure in the room was not money but the wish not to disappoint an authority who seemed to need help. That is what she and Branden called social metaphysics, and it is why she would expect most students to agree whatever the sum. One nuance: being hired for a task is a trade, and "Taking orders from one's employer on how to do the work for which one is hired, is not a 'compromise'" ("Doesn't Life Require Compromise?", 1962). Taking the job was not the fault. Saying what one believed to be false to a third party was.

**The students who refused or told the truth: discarded as noise.** Three students refused the money and the job, two told the woman the truth, and one wanted her number so that he could explain. The design treats them as failures of the manipulation. For Rand the people who would not fake are the moral centre of the experiment, like the nursery children who "remain aloof and withdrawn, unable to give in, unable to fake" ("The Comprachicos", 1970). She would ask what distinguished a student who said "no" from one who said "yes" for a dollar, and why that question was not the study. She would credit the paper's honest reporting of the exclusions. She would say the theory has no place for the refuser except as a missing data point.

**The experimenters' deception: faking to gain a value.** A false "department study" planted in the first week of term, a false explanation, a fake absent assistant, a hired confederate, a hidden tape recorder. By the only exception Objectivism allows, these lies are not permitted: the students were not using force, and the lies were told to gain a value, clean data. Smith's actor test fails too, since "An actor portraying a fictional character on stage would not be faking in the relevant sense, since it is understood that he is acting" (p. 76), and the students did not know they were in a play. Rand would note the irony that a study of people induced to say what they did not believe depended on scientists saying what they did not believe, and then relied on the students' honesty in the interview. She would credit the debriefing and the return of the money as justice done afterwards. Rand wrote nothing about research deception; this is her general principle applied.

**The drive, Bem, and integrity run backwards.** Dissonance was never measured, only inferred. Rand rejects Festinger's account and Daryl Bem's self-perception account as descriptions of a thinking person, while accepting both as descriptions of an unfocused one. Against Festinger: discomfort is a signal that a premise has been touched, not a force that moves belief, and the move is a choice. Against Bem: a person who reads his opinions off his own behaviour as an outside observer would has made himself a second-hander to himself, "Not to judge, but to repeat." Her standard is introspection: "one must never act like a zombie, i.e., without knowing one's own purposes and motives" ("The Objectivist Ethics"). The precision of the effect fits her account better than a diffuse drive, since evasion is "an active process aimed at a specific content", and only the belief tied to the lie moved. Finally, the headline finding, that behaviour changed belief, is for Rand integrity reversed. The student should have made action fit conviction; instead he made conviction fit action. She would also reject the paper's phrase "at least in our culture". For her the clash between saying and believing is not cultural but the law of identity, her principle that a thing is itself, "A is A", applied to one's own mind.

**The five suspicious students: keeping the whole context.** Besides the refusers, five students were set aside because, when asked at the end of the interview, they said they suspected the payment to lie was the real purpose of the study (p. 207). In Rand's terms these five showed "a state of full, focused awareness" ("The Objectivist Ethics"): they put together the odd details, the absent assistant, the awkward request, the money. Peikoff's point about the narrow focus of the mind suggests why it is hard to do this during a short, awkward request: a mind "cannot compare old contents and new in a flash" (*Objectivism*, p. 125). The paper gives no evidence about how much the other students reflected. But Rand would note that the final sample left out the students who voiced a suspicion, so the result describes students who did not.

**Does the theory exempt its own authors?** <Term>Determinism</Term> is the view that every event, including every choice and belief, is fully caused by what came before. Peikoff argues that if minds were moved irresistibly in this way, "a man could not rely on his own judgment. He could claim nothing as objective knowledge, including the theory of determinism" (*Objectivism*, p. 71). In plain words: if all beliefs are simply pushed into place by causes, then so is the belief in determinism, and there is no reason to trust it. An Objectivist would put the same question to Festinger. If beliefs are pushed into line by a pressure for consistency, why should the researchers' own belief in dissonance theory, in which they had invested years, count as knowledge rather than as their own dissonance reduction? The answer Rand would give is that thinking is volitional for everyone, scientist and student alike. Festinger's theory can describe how minds behave when they drift, but it cannot be the whole story of belief, or it would undercut itself.

## Where it agrees, and where it clashes

Rand and the paper agree that people do this, often; that a small inducement does more inner damage than a large one; that the act was the student's own; and that a theory should make a counter-intuitive prediction and test it. She would value the control question showing that the change was targeted, the tape-rating check that ruled out "trying harder", and the plain reporting of weak results.

They clash on status. Festinger says dissonance reduction is how minds work. Rand says it is how minds fail, by choice. What the paper calls a drive, she calls evasion; what it calls a consonant cognition, she calls a bribe; what it calls attitude change, she calls rationalisation; and the students it excludes are the ones she would study.

## Questions it would put to the researchers

- Three students refused, and others told the woman the truth. What distinguished them, and what would a theory built around them look like?
- By what mechanism does "dissonance" move an opinion? You never observed it. Is it a cause, or a name for the choice to stop looking?
- Did you ask the $1 students why they had agreed? If not, how do you know the belief change was not a story told to avoid that question?
- You lied to the students at five points to get honest answers. By what principle is your deception permitted and theirs the object of study?
- If "I said it was fun" can change "it was boring", what stops the same process from changing any belief a person acts against? Is that a mechanism of the mind or its failure?
- Why "forced compliance"? Nobody was forced. What does the word do to your reader's picture of responsibility?
- Why call the clash between saying and believing a fact "at least in our culture"? Is a contradiction in one's own mind cultural?
- You asked the students what they felt about the tasks. Why did you not ask why they felt it?
- You call the change "consistency". Consistent with what: everything the student knew, or only the two ideas in front of him at that moment?
- If belief is pushed into line by pressure for consistency, what makes your own belief in the theory knowledge?

## Cautions

- **Rand never mentions Festinger, cognitive dissonance or this experiment.** Everything on this page is an application of her general views, and the guide says so.
- **The famous line is not in the texts used here.** "Contradictions do not exist. Whenever you think you are facing a contradiction, check your premises" is spoken by Francisco in *Atlas Shrugged*, which is not among the works quoted. The quotations on contradiction above are from Galt's speech as reprinted in *For the New Intellectual*.
- **Whose words.** Nathaniel Branden coined "social metaphysics" in 1962 and Rand used it; Peikoff's and Smith's sentences are expositions. The page marks each.
- **Rand is not a psychologist**, and "psycho-epistemology" is her own term, not one used outside Objectivism. Her followers also disagree about how to interpret her ethics.
- **Contextual certainty is Peikoff's account.** The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy (entry "Ayn Rand", supplement on epistemology) confirms that for Rand certainty is available and differs from infallibility, but calls Peikoff's fuller account "a problematic notion" and adds that "Rand herself never makes any statement to this effect". Write "Peikoff argues" for it.
- **Rand did not say that people choose their beliefs directly.** Her claim is that people choose whether to focus. Do not write that the $1 student "chose to believe" the tasks were fun. Write that, on her view, he chose not to keep the evidence in focus.
- **Rand's picture of pragmatism is a polemic.** She wrote that for pragmatists "whatever one wishes to be true, is true" (*For the New Intellectual*). Peirce's and James's own texts, quoted on the next page, say otherwise. Present her verdict as hers.
- **Do not claim that Objectivism permits lies for a good cause.** It permits lies only against someone using force.

  scenario="In Jaipur, a junior employee is asked by her manager to tell a client that a delayed project is 'on track'. She does. That evening she tells herself that the delay 'isn't really a delay, just a reschedule'. Her friend says: 'That is dissonance reduction.' (An invented example.)"
  question="What would Rand add to the friend's diagnosis, and what would she say the employee should do?"
>
Rand would agree that the employee is relieving discomfort by changing a belief, and then dispute almost everything else about the description. The lie to the client was a chosen act of faking reality to gain a value, the manager's approval and perhaps her job, and a manager's request is not force, so the one permitted exception does not apply. The evening's thought is a rationalisation: a "false identity" given to her unease so as to hide her motive "primarily from oneself". And it was a choice, not a drive. In Peikoff's words, the feeling "is merely a precondition, not a cause or explanation; the choice to capitulate to it is irreducible".

The discomfort itself, Rand would say, was doing its proper job. Emotions are not tools of cognition, but they are a signal that a premise has been touched, and the premise here is "I am honest". The remedy is to treat the signal as information rather than as a problem to be dissolved: identify the fact (the project is late, and I said otherwise), and then let action follow conviction, by correcting the record with the client or by refusing the next such request. That is integrity, "loyalty to one's convictions and values ... translating them into practical reality". The friend's account describes the mind working as minds do. Rand's account describes the mind failing in a way the employee can still reverse.
</Try>
