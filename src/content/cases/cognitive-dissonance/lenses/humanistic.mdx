## The lens in brief

Humanistic psychology grew in the 1950s and 1960s as a "third force", in reaction to both behaviourism and psychoanalysis. Its best-known founders are Carl Rogers and Abraham Maslow. It sees people as whole persons with free will and a drive to grow, and cares most about a person's own experience and the meaning they give to it.

Rogers' central idea is the <Term>self-concept</Term>: the picture a person holds of who they are, such as "I am honest" or "I am kind". When a person's experience does not fit their self-concept, Rogers called this <Term>incongruence</Term>. He argued that incongruence feels threatening, and that people often defend against it by distorting the experience or denying it, rather than changing the self-concept (Rogers, 1959). <Term>Congruence</Term>, by contrast, means that a person's experience, awareness and words match. Rogers saw it as a sign of psychological health.

This lens is close to the study in one way and far from it in another. Its idea of incongruence is strikingly similar to dissonance, and it was published in the same years. But its view of how to study people is the opposite of a deception experiment.

## How this lens reads the study

**Incongruence between the self and the act.** Most of the students probably saw themselves as honest. Then, in two minutes, each paid student told a young woman he believed to be the next participant something false, and talked her out of her friend's warning that the experiment was boring (p. 206). She was in fact hired to play the part, but he did not know that. In Rogers' terms, the experience "I misled her" does not fit the self-concept "I am an honest person". The $1 students, on this reading, protected their self-concept by distorting the experience: if the tasks were really fairly interesting, then they had not really lied. The $20 students had a different way to fit the act into their self-picture: "It was a paid job, not a personal lie."

**The threat is to the self.** Elliot Aronson, who worked closely with Festinger, revised dissonance theory in the 1960s in a direction humanistic psychologists would recognise. He argued that dissonance is strongest when a person's behaviour threatens their self-concept as a good, competent and moral person. On this view, "I believe the tasks were boring" and "I said they were fun" are not dissonant in themselves. What hurts is "I am a decent person" set against "I misled someone for a dollar". This version brings dissonance theory close to Rogers.

**Freedom that was arranged.** Humanistic psychology puts free will at the centre of human life. The study also depended on the students feeling free: the experimenter asked politely, presented the task as a favour, and three students did refuse (p. 207). But everything about the request was designed in advance, including the experimenter's embarrassment and the lines he used if a student hesitated, such as "It will only take a few minutes" (p. 205). A humanistic psychologist would note the irony. The study needs a sense of free choice to work, yet treats that freedom as something the researcher produces and controls.

**The person missing from the numbers.** In the interview, each student was first "encouraged to talk" about each question before giving a rating (p. 206). This was the moment when students might have described what the hour, the request and the conversation had felt like. The paper reports only the numbers. For a humanistic psychologist, the students' own accounts would have been the most important data in the study.

## What this lens values in the study

It values the study's central message: people care deeply about seeing themselves as consistent and honest, and will change their beliefs to protect that picture. This supports the humanistic view that the self-concept organises experience. It also values the students who refused the money or told the woman the truth, who show that people can act on their values under polite pressure. And it would notice that once the truth was out, "All Ss, without exception, were quite willing to return the money" (p. 207).

## What it would question

- Deceiving participants about the purpose of a study, through several layers of false stories, treats them as objects to be managed rather than as partners.
- Asking people to deceive someone they believe is a fellow student, in a study that turns on honesty, uses their integrity as a research tool.
- The students' experience was collected in the interview but not reported.
- The study shows only a defensive response to incongruence. It does not ask under what conditions people can face an uncomfortable truth openly.

## Evidence from this tradition

**Rogers on incongruence and defence.** Rogers (1959) set out a theory in which threatened experiences are kept out of awareness or distorted, and in which a relationship of empathy, genuineness and unconditional positive regard reduces the need to defend. His prediction for this case would be that a student who felt fully accepted, whatever he had done, would be more able to say "I lied for a dollar" and less likely to change his opinion of the tasks.

**Self-affirmation.** Claude Steele's <Term>self-affirmation theory</Term> (1988) proposed that people can tolerate an inconsistency if they are allowed to confirm an important part of their self-worth in some other way. In studies by Steele and Liu (1983), people who had written essays against their views changed their attitudes less if, after writing, they had a chance to express values they cared about before their attitude was measured. This supports the idea that dissonance is about protecting the self as a whole.

**A critique of deception from inside psychology.** In 1967 Herbert Kelman, the same researcher whose 1953 study the 1959 paper criticises, published "Human use of human subjects". He argued that deception had become routine in social psychology, that it damaged the relationship between researcher and participant, and that researchers should develop alternatives, including role-playing studies in which participants know the purpose. Sidney Jourard, a humanistic psychologist, argued that research participants reveal more of themselves when the researcher is open with them, and called for research as a dialogue.

**Research with people, not on them.** John Heron and Peter Reason developed <Term>co-operative inquiry</Term>, a method in which participants help design the research, take part in it and interpret the results.

**Evidence against the lens.** Dissonance-like effects have been reported in amnesic patients, young children and capuchin monkeys (see the [cognitive](/cases/cognitive-dissonance/lenses/cognitive/) and [biological](/cases/cognitive-dissonance/lenses/biological/) lenses). If these findings hold, dissonance does not always need a rich, conscious self-concept. Joel Cooper and Russell Fazio's "new look" at dissonance (1984) also argued that the key ingredient is feeling responsible for unwanted consequences, which does not require any threat to a person's self-image. Kelman's proposed alternative, role-playing, has also been criticised. Jonathan Freedman (1969) argued that asking people to imagine a situation shows what they think they would do, not what they actually do, so it cannot replace studies in which people believe the situation is real.

## A study this lens would design

1. Recruit volunteers and tell them in advance that the study involves being asked to do something they may not agree with, and that some details will be explained only at the end. Ask for consent to this.
2. Before the study, ask each person how important honesty is to their sense of who they are.
3. Run the boring tasks and the $1 request.
4. Afterwards, give half the participants ten minutes with a trained listener who responds with empathy and without judgement, before any rating.
5. Interview every participant in depth about what the request and the conversation felt like, and invite them to help interpret the transcripts.

**Prediction:** participants who value honesty most will change their opinion most, if they get no support. Participants who receive empathic listening will more often acknowledge what they did and will change their opinion less, because they need less defence.

Rogers would describe her situation as incongruence. Her self-concept as a caring, honest counsellor does not fit her experience of making promises she doubts. With little money to explain her behaviour, she protected her self-concept by distorting the experience: if every child really can succeed, she is not misleading anyone. This is the same pattern as the $1 students, described in terms of the self.

The workshop adds what dissonance theory does not cover. In Rogers' view, a relationship of acceptance reduces threat, so a person no longer needs to defend and can let the denied experience into awareness. Her admission that she feels like a fraud is a move towards congruence. Dissonance theory predicts how people reduce inconsistency; the humanistic lens asks what conditions let them face it honestly and grow.
