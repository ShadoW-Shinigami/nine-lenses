## The tradition in brief

Existentialism is less a school than a family of writers who put one question first: what does it mean to exist as a being who has to choose? The French philosophers Jean-Paul Sartre (1905 to 1980) and Simone de Beauvoir (1908 to 1986) accepted the label. The Danish Christian thinker Søren Kierkegaard (1813 to 1855) and the German philosopher Friedrich Nietzsche (1844 to 1900) are counted as forerunners. Albert Camus (1913 to 1960) is often grouped with them but denied being one. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy notes that only Sartre and Beauvoir called themselves existentialists.

For this study the key idea is Sartre's <Term>bad faith</Term> (in French *mauvaise foi*): the ways a person hides their own freedom from themselves. Its background is his claim that "existence precedes essence": a person is not made to a design but becomes what they do, and so is "condemned to be free" (*Being and Nothingness*, 1943, p. 577). The other writers add their own accounts of the same move, the mind quietly rearranging what it knows so that the person can stay comfortable.

## What existentialists say about lying to yourself

Each idea below starts with the writer's own words, then explains them in plain English, then says why they matter for this study.

**Bad faith is not a lie.** "In bad faith it is from myself that I am concealing the truth. Thus the duality of deceiver and deceived is not present here. On the contrary, bad faith implies in its essence the unity of a single consciousness" (*Being and Nothingness*, p. 90). A cold, deliberate lie to oneself would collapse at once. Bad faith works instead as a kind of belief: "it believes, and it does not believe, that it is in bad faith" (p. 114).

In plain words: an ordinary lie has two people, one who knows the truth and one who is fooled. Self-deception seems to need the same two people inside one head, which looks impossible. Sartre's answer is that <Term>bad faith</Term> is not a cold lie but a kind of belief that keeps itself vague on purpose. The person in bad faith lets himself be half persuaded, decides not to demand strong evidence, and does not look too closely at what he is doing. He half knows what he is up to, which is why Sartre says bad faith "believes, and it does not believe" that it is in bad faith. Think of someone who "forgets" an appointment he did not want to keep.

Why it matters here: the $1 student knew what the hour had been like, so his new rating is not amnesia. Sartre would call it the half-belief of bad faith, a comfortable view adopted by not asking for evidence against it.

**There is no hidden censor doing the hiding.** Sartre's target here is Freud's censor: "how could the censor discern the drives to be repressed without being conscious of discerning them? Can we conceive of knowledge as being ignorant of itself?" (p. 94). So "the censor must be in bad faith".

In plain words: Freud explained self-deception by dividing the mind into parts, with a censor that keeps unwelcome thoughts out of consciousness. Sartre's objection is simple. To keep a thought out, the censor must know what the thought is; so the censor both knows and does not let itself know, which is the very puzzle it was supposed to solve, now moved inside a smaller agent. Sartre concludes that there is no mechanism that hides things from us behind our backs. Self-deception is something a person does, and the whole person does it.

Why it matters here: the same objection applies to a drive that is never measured. Whatever "reduced the dissonance" had to know which thoughts clashed and which change would help, which means it was the student himself, doing something.

**There are no excuses.** "What distinguishes human-reality is that it has no excuse" (p. 720). In the lecture he gave in 1945, published the next year, he put it bluntly: "any man who takes refuge behind the excuse of his passions, or by inventing some deterministic doctrine, is a self-deceiver" (Mairet's word for a person in bad faith). He went further: "I am responsible even for my desire to flee my responsibilities" (p. 721).

In plain words: Sartre's "no excuse" sounds harsh, and he meant it to. His claim is that pointing to a cause outside yourself, a passion, a payment, an order, a bad childhood, never fully explains what you did, because you still had to take that cause up and act on it. The reason you give afterwards is part of the act, not a motor hidden behind it. He went so far as to say that even the wish to escape responsibility is something you are responsible for. In Mairet's translation the person who hides behind such excuses is called a "self-deceiver"; the French word is the same as for bad faith.

Why it matters here: "I did it for twenty dollars" is an excuse in Sartre's sense, and so is "I did it because the experimenter asked". The theory treats the size of the payment as the cause; Sartre would say the cause is what each student made of the payment.

**Memory yields to pride.** Nietzsche compressed self-justification into three sentences. "'I have done that,' says my memory. 'I cannot have done that,' says my pride, and remains inexorable. Eventually – memory yields" (*Beyond Good and Evil*, section 68). Even philosophers, he wrote, defend conclusions that come from "a desire of the heart" with "reasons they have sought after the fact" (section 5). In the *Genealogy* he described a grander version, the "slave revolt in morality", in which the powerless re-describe their weakness as virtue through "the counterfeit and self-deception of impotence" (essay 1, section 13).

In plain words: Nietzsche's three sentences describe a quarrel inside a person. Memory says that the deed was done; pride says that it cannot have been; and pride wins, so the memory is rewritten. He thought this was how minds normally work, not an occasional failure, and he extended the point to philosophers, who, he said, reach their conclusions by wish and then hunt for reasons. The *Genealogy* tells the same story on a historical scale. People who could not fight back re-described their weakness as goodness and their enemies as evil. Nietzsche traces this to <Term>ressentiment</Term>, the resentment of the powerless, which "becomes creative" and makes a bad position bearable by renaming it.

Why it matters here: the $1 student's "it was interesting" is a small revaluation. A wasted hour and a cheap lie become a worthwhile experience, and Nietzsche would find the experiment a tidy confirmation of what he took for granted.

**Willing the good for a reward is double-mindedness.** "The person who wills the good for the sake of reward does not will one thing but is double-minded" ("Purity of Heart", Part One, section II). Such a person "stands at the crossroads; two prospects appear there: the good and the reward". The same holds for willing the good out of fear of punishment, or "only to a certain degree". And the comfortable person resists finding out: he "is usually far from wanting to be wrenched out of his error. On the contrary, he becomes indignant" (*The Sickness unto Death*, p. 43). When a person has worked to cloud their own knowledge, Kierkegaard argued, the fault is "not in the knowing but in the willing" (p. 88).

In plain words: Kierkegaard's test of a person is whether they want one thing or two. Someone who does the right thing for a reward wants the good and also wants the reward, and the two can come apart, so he stands at a crossroads with two wills. The same holds for someone who does right only to avoid punishment, or only up to the point where it starts to cost. Kierkegaard calls this split <Term>double-mindedness</Term>, and he says it is what despair is. He adds that the comfortable person does not want to be told any of this, and treats whoever tells him as an enemy. And when someone has worked to keep themselves from knowing something, the fault is in what they willed, not in what they failed to know. His frame is Christian: "the good" is willed before God.

Why it matters here: for Kierkegaard the $1 and $20 students differ only in degree. Both acted for a reward and so had two wills, and a student who later finds the experiment more "important" is giving himself a neater reason.

**Keep the tension alive.** Camus described the <Term>absurd</Term> as a confrontation between the human "appetite for the absolute" and a world that gives no answer. Religious and philosophical thinkers end the tension by a leap that drops one side. He called this "philosophical suicide" and asked for the opposite: "There can be no question of masking the evidence, of suppressing the absurd by denying one of the terms of its equation" (*The Myth of Sisyphus*, "Philosophical suicide"). His ethic in one line: "Living is keeping the absurd alive" ("Absurd freedom").

In plain words: Camus's absurd is not a word for something silly. It names the clash between two things, the human need for meaning and unity, and a universe that gives no answer. Religious and philosophical thinkers, he says, escape the clash by a leap that drops one side, usually by deciding that the universe does answer after all. He calls this "philosophical suicide", because it kills the question rather than facing it. His own demand is to keep both sides in view and go on living without resolving them. Staying clear-eyed about the contradiction is, for Camus, the only honest response.

Why it matters here: Camus's ethic is the direct opposite of dissonance reduction. The $1 student held two terms, "I said it was fun" and "it was boring", and ended the discomfort by dropping one. Camus would call that a small leap and ask for the harder thing.

## What existentialists say about knowing and believing

The ideas above are about lying to oneself. Behind them sits a view of knowledge, which philosophers call <Term>epistemology</Term>: the study of what knowledge and belief are, what counts as good evidence, and whether we can know our own minds. Each writer's epistemology shapes how they read the $1 student's new opinion. The writers disagree here more than anywhere else.

**Bad faith changes the rules of evidence.** "The problem of bad faith is essentially a problem about belief" (*Being and Nothingness*, p. 114). Bad faith "does not retain the norms and criteria of truth that are accepted by the critical thinking of good faith"; instead, "from the outset, bad faith determines the nature of truth" (p. 114). It meets a special kind of evidence, "non-persuasive evidence": it "apprehends facts that are evident, but it is resigned in advance not to be fulfilled by such evidence". It shows itself in "its resolution not to ask for too much, to consider itself satisfied when it is poorly persuaded" (p. 114). This is not a plan the person makes: "One puts oneself into bad faith as one goes to sleep, and being in bad faith is like dreaming" (p. 115).

In plain words: the earlier section said that bad faith is a half-belief. The point here is about standards. A careful thinker asks how strong the evidence must be before she accepts a claim, and she keeps the same bar for claims she likes and claims she dislikes. A person in bad faith does not break this rule openly. He quietly lowers the bar for the view he wants, and accepts weak support as "enough". Sartre uses "evidence" in a technical sense: a fact being plainly present to the mind, not proof in a court. The person sees the facts, but has decided in advance that seeing them will not settle anything. The decision is not one he could report. It is more like drifting into sleep.

Why it matters here: the student's own hour with the pegs is Sartre's non-persuasive evidence: plain to him, and, on the paper's reading, not allowed to decide the $1 group's rating. The study also supplied the weak support that bad faith accepts as enough. On the way to the interview, the experimenter told every student, in all three groups, "Most of our subjects tell us afterward that they found it quite interesting". The paper says why: the remark was meant "to make it easier for anyone who wanted to persuade himself that the tasks had been, indeed, enjoyable" (p. 206). The control students heard it and still rated the tasks a little below neutral (-0.45), while the $1 group's average was +1.35. So the remark did nothing by itself. Sartre would say that this is the point: weak support only counts as "enough" for someone who already has a reason to lower his standard, as the $1 student, who needed a kinder view of the hour, did.

**Belief never fully believes itself, and the cure is evidence.** "To believe is to know one believes, and to know one believes is to believe no longer" (p. 115). "No belief is ever enough of a belief; we never believe in what we believe" (p. 116). Bad faith makes use of this weakness, and good thinking escapes it in only one way: bad faith wills "this self-destruction on the part of belief – which science evades by moving towards evidence" (p. 117).

In plain words: Sartre's example is trust in a friend, Pierre. I believe that Pierre likes me. The moment I notice that I only *believe* it, it starts to feel like "just my belief" and loosens its hold. Every belief is open to this, so nobody believes anything completely. Bad faith uses the gap: if no belief is ever complete, a weak and hopeful belief seems no worse than any other. Sartre's remedy is telling. He does not ask for firmer belief, and he does not ask for more consistency. He asks for evidence, which is the path of science.

Why it matters here: Festinger's theory describes a mind that seeks consistency. Sartre's epistemology says that consistency is not what a good thinker should aim at. A set of beliefs can fit together perfectly and still rest on a lowered bar. The $1 student ended with two cognitions that fit, "I said it was interesting" and "it was interesting", and with less contact with the evidence of his own hour. For Sartre that is a loss, however calm it feels.

**Being aware of something is not the same as knowing it.** "Any positional consciousness of an object is at the same time a non-positional consciousness of itself" (p. 11). Sartre's example is counting cigarettes: "children who are capable of spontaneous addition are unable to explain afterwards how they did it" (p. 11). A person's deepest aim can be "fully conscious" without being "known by him" (p. 740). When we describe ourselves we use ordinary reflection, which Sartre calls <Term>complicit reflection</Term>. It turns lived experience into fixed things, "the Ego, its states, its qualities and its acts", and these are what "the psychologist studies as the so-called psychological fact" (p. 232). Its evidence "is not apodictic" (p. 235), which means it is not certain.

In plain words: when you count a pile of coins, your attention is on the coins, not on yourself. Yet you are aware that you are counting: if someone asks what you are doing, you answer at once. Sartre calls attention aimed at an object, such as the coins, positional consciousness, and this background awareness of yourself <Term>non-positional</Term> consciousness. It is real, but it is not knowledge. You could not describe it, and you might not be able to say how you reached your total. Knowledge of yourself comes only when you reflect, that is, when you turn your experience into an object and look at it. Ordinary reflection does more than look. It packages the experience into stable items such as "my opinion of the tasks". Sartre says those packages are what psychologists measure, and that the act of reflecting helps to make them. Note that Sartre rejects the Freudian unconscious. His claim is that people live their projects in full awareness and still cannot put them into words.

Why it matters here: this gives a third answer to an old question about the study, whether the $1 students knew that they had changed their minds. The dissonance account suggests that they did not notice. A cynic would say they knew and were pretending. Sartre's answer is that each student was aware of what he was doing, as the counting child is aware of adding, but could not have stated it. When the interviewer asked for a number from -5 to +5, the student reflected, and his reflection produced a stable object, "how enjoyable the tasks were", for someone he took to be an official of the department. Sartre would not call the number false. He would call it a made object, produced by a kind of reflection that already leans towards bad faith.

**Comfort as the test of truth.** Nietzsche described the same move as a general rule of the mind. "First principle: any explanation is better than none at all", and the first idea that makes the unfamiliar feel familiar "produces a feeling of such comfort that it is 'held to be true.' The proof of happiness ('of power') as the criterion of truth" (*Twilight of the Idols*, "The Four Great Errors", section 5). The spirit tends "to assimilate the new to the old, to simplify the manifold, and to overlook or repulse whatever is totally contradictory" (*Beyond Good and Evil*, section 230). Sometimes it chooses to be deceived, "perhaps with a capricious intimation of the fact that such and such is not the case" (section 230). And he warned against explaining behaviour by needs: "A need is considered the cause of the origin: in truth, it is often merely an effect of what did originate" (*The Gay Science*, section 205).

In plain words: people cannot bear to leave a feeling unexplained. They grab the first familiar explanation, and the relief it brings makes them believe it, so comfort quietly becomes the test of truth. The passage from section 230 resembles what Festinger later called dissonance reduction, though Nietzsche treated it as healthy digestion of experience, not as a problem. The mind files new things under old headings and pushes away whatever flatly contradicts them, while half knowing that it is doing so ("a capricious intimation" means a passing, half-noticed hint). The last quotation is a warning for psychologists. We often think a need came first and caused something. Nietzsche says the need is often a result of the thing it is supposed to explain.

Why it matters here: Nietzsche's view can be used for the theory and against it. It explains the $20 result: money is the most familiar explanation there is, so "I did it for the money" brought instant relief and left the opinion of the tasks alone. But it can also be turned against Festinger. The paper explains its results by a need for consistency that it never measured. Applying section 205, the wish for consistency could be a result of having justified oneself rather than its cause. Nietzsche might also ask, and this is the page's extension, whether the need is a cause the researchers observed or a name they gave to the result. He would also ask whether a single tidy drive appealed to them because, like any familiar cause, it ended their own puzzlement.

**Should we want the truth at all?** Nietzsche asked a question most philosophers had skipped: "Suppose we want truth: why not rather untruth? and uncertainty? even ignorance?" (*Beyond Good and Evil*, section 1). "The falseness of a judgment is for us not necessarily an objection to a judgment"; the question is "to what extent it is life-promoting, life-preserving" (section 4). Yet the same writer called honesty the virtue of "free spirits", "the only one left us" (section 227), and wrote: "Error (faith in the ideal) is not blindness, error is cowardice" (*Ecce Homo*, Preface, section 3).

In plain words: almost every philosopher had assumed that true beliefs are better than false ones. Nietzsche asked why. A false belief might help someone to live, and he thought much of human knowledge grew out of useful simplifications. But he did not conclude that anything goes. He praised honesty as the virtue of free spirits, and he measured a person by how much truth they could bear. Scholars still argue about how these two sides fit together (see the cautions). One workable reading is that Nietzsche questioned the value of truth in theory and demanded the courage to face it in practice.

Why it matters here: this is the sharpest disagreement inside the tradition. On Nietzsche's first side, the $1 student's comfortable belief is not obviously a failure. A little self-flattery that helped him live with a wasted hour might be "life-promoting". On Nietzsche's second side, and for Sartre and Camus throughout, it is cowardice, a refusal to bear a small truth. A journal can use the split to ask a real question: does the study picture a mind working well, or a mind failing?

**Self-deception is not a lack of facts but a refusal.** Kierkegaard described, step by step, how knowledge gives way to wishes. "[I]f a person does not do what is right at the very second he knows it – then, first of all, knowing simmers down." The will "allows some time to elapse, an interim called: 'We shall look at it tomorrow.' During all this, knowing becomes more and more obscure." In the end, "knowing has come over to the side of willing and admits that what it wants is absolutely right" (*The Sickness unto Death*, p. 94). He separated self-deception from ordinary ignorance: "The ignorant person can gradually acquire wisdom and knowledge", but the self-deceived person needs a change of heart, not more information ("Purity of Heart", pp. 23 to 24). And of a man courting a woman who has money: "Money is said to have great influence; one can easily be mistaken, and it is very difficult to know oneself" (pp. 38 to 39).

In plain words: the earlier section quoted Kierkegaard's verdict that the fault lies "not in the knowing but in the willing". These passages show how it happens. The will does not openly fight what the person knows. It postpones, and during the delay the knowledge fades, until the knowledge itself "agrees" that what the will wanted was right all along. This is why facts alone do not cure self-deception: the problem is a refusal, not a gap. The money passage adds that wherever payment is involved, a person can easily be wrong about why he acts.

Why it matters here: Kierkegaard's end state, knowing and willing in full agreement, is close to what Festinger calls consonance restored (a <Term>consonant</Term> cognition is one that fits the others). But Kierkegaard reads the $20 result the other way. On the theory, the $20 student knows his own mind best: he lied, he knows why, and his opinion is intact. For Kierkegaard he may know himself least, because money is exactly what makes it "very difficult to know oneself". "I did it for the money" can be as comfortable an account as "it was interesting". Both accounts predict a rating near the control group's, so the data cannot decide between them.

**Lucidity: keep what you know, even against yourself.** Camus defined understanding as making things fit together, which is close to what Festinger's students sought: "to understand is, above all, to unify" (*The Myth of Sisyphus*, "Absurd walls"). He named the gap between the beliefs people live by and the ones they would hold if they looked: "the regular hiatus between what we fancy we know and what we really know, practical assent and simulated ignorance which allows us to live with ideas which, if we truly put them to the test, ought to upset our whole life" ("Absurd walls"). His answer was <Term>lucidity</Term>: "What I believe to be true I must therefore preserve. What seems to me so obvious, even against me, I must support" ("Absurd freedom"). And he set a limit on what psychology can know: "In psychology as in logic, there are truths but no truth" ("Absurd walls").

In plain words: Camus grants that the wish for things to fit together is real and built into us. But a wish is not evidence that the world will grant it. Most people, he says, live with "simulated ignorance": they say yes to ideas in practice and avoid looking at where those ideas lead, because looking would upset their lives. Lucidity does not mean knowing everything. It means refusing to claim more than you know, and refusing to un-know what you do know. "Even against me" is the key phrase: a belief that hurts must be kept if it is true.

Why it matters here: "simulated ignorance" may fit the $1 student better than any other phrase in the tradition. He did not forget the hour; he lived as if its lesson did not apply. Camus also limits what the study can claim. A group average on an interview scale is one of psychology's "truths". It is not "the truth" about what any student thought, which Camus says no psychology can reach.

**Ambiguity is to be assumed, not resolved.** Beauvoir made knowledge of one's real situation the ground of ethics: "Let us try to assume our fundamental ambiguity. It is in the knowledge of the genuine conditions of our life that we must draw our strength to live and our reason for acting" (*The Ethics of Ambiguity*, Part I). Her serious man fails in knowledge as well as in morals: "the serious man puts nothing into question" (Part II). He hides from himself that he chose his values, "like the mythomaniac who while reading a love-letter pretends to forget that she has sent it to herself" (Part II). And she named the social side of ignorance: "mystification is one of the forms of oppression; ignorance is a situation in which man may be enclosed as narrowly as in a prison" (Part III).

In plain words: for Beauvoir, <Term>ambiguity</Term> is the mixed, unsettled condition of human life, and to "assume" it is to take it on as your own instead of escaping into a neat doctrine. A mythomaniac is a person who habitually invents stories and half believes them. The love-letter image describes evidence you produced yourself and then read as if it came from outside. <Term>Mystification</Term> means keeping people confused about their real situation, and she treats it as a form of control. She puts most of the blame on whoever mystifies, not on the person mystified.

Why it matters here: the waiting woman pushed back once, saying that a friend had found the experiment boring. Then she "listened quietly, accepting and agreeing to everything the S told her" (p. 206). From then on the student heard only his own claim, accepted. This resembles Beauvoir's love-letter, though she used the image for the serious man's values, not for a conversation: he wrote "it's interesting" and received it back as agreement from outside. Her word mystification also fits the study as a whole, as the next section shows.

## Reading the study through this tradition

**A lie to oneself with no liar and no deceived.** The $1 student told the waiting woman that the tasks were interesting, then rated them as enjoyable to an interviewer he believed came from the department. He had just spent an hour with the pegs and knew what he thought of them. Sartre would say his new rating is neither amnesia nor a second cynical lie. It is the half-belief of bad faith, in which a person stops demanding evidence against a comfortable view. Festinger and Sartre agree on one thing: the whole process happens inside one person and is that person's own work. They part on what to call it. Festinger describes a state that pushes. Sartre describes a project the person adopts, and he would say that the push is the person.

**The drive that was never measured, and the censor.** Dissonance was defined as an unpleasant state like hunger, but no discomfort was ever measured; it was inferred from the ratings (see [strengths and limitations](/cases/cognitive-dissonance/strengths-limitations/)). Sartre's objection to Freud's censor transfers directly. Whatever reduced the dissonance had to know which thoughts clashed and which change would help. That is to say, it had to be the student himself, doing something. A theory that explains the result through an inner force the person does not notice has, for Sartre, moved the puzzle rather than solved it. He and Festinger both reject a two-agent mind of the Freudian kind. They disagree about whether "drive" names anything beyond the person's own choosing.

**Excuses: the $20, the $1 and the ones who refused.** The $20 students rated the tasks almost exactly as the control group did. The $1 students rated them clearly higher. Three students refused to take the money, two told the woman the truth, and one wanted her number so that he could explain. For Sartre the $20 student's position, "I lied, but for twenty dollars", treats the money as a cause and is an excuse: the bad faith of the person who becomes a thing moved by a payment. The $1 student, left without such a cause, flees in the other direction by revising what he thinks, so that there is no longer anything to own. Both groups flee; only the route differs. The students who refused, or told the truth, kept the act as theirs, and the analysis set them aside. Beauvoir adds the frame. The request arrived wrapped in ready-made authority: the professor, the department, the course requirement. A student who complied because "the department" asked is her <Term>serious man</Term>, who "is no longer a man, but a father, a boss, a member" of an institution (*The Ethics of Ambiguity*, Part II). The fake "department survey" exploited exactly that trust.

**Dissonance reduction as an old habit.** Nietzsche would find the experiment a tidy confirmation of something he took for granted: the mind rewrites what it cannot bear, and reasons are collected afterwards. The $1 student's "it was interesting" is a small revaluation. A wasted hour and a cheap lie become a worthwhile experience, just as the lambs' weakness became virtue. The paper's picture of a rationalising rather than rational person is Nietzschean. But Nietzsche would reject the idea that consistency is a need. For him the force at work is pride, not harmony, and he would ask why the psychologists ever expected human beings to be consistent.

**Willing for reward, and not wanting to know.** For Kierkegaard the $1 and $20 conditions differ only in degree. Both students did a favour for payment and so had two wills. The later measures catch the double-mindedness at work: a student who now finds the experiment slightly more scientifically important is giving himself a better reason, which is Kierkegaard's reward-seeker making his motives look neater. The instructor's plea to be completely honest was addressed to someone who had every reason not to want to know. Kierkegaard's claim that the fault lies in the willing fits the paper's finding that opinion moved only on the questions the lie had touched.

**Keeping the tension alive, and the deed without a doer.** Camus's ethic is the direct opposite of dissonance reduction. The $1 student held two terms, "I said it was fun" and "it was boring", and ended the discomfort by dropping one. Camus would call that a small leap and ask for the harder thing: hold both and stay lucid. On Daryl Bem's later reinterpretation, that the student simply observed his own act and inferred his attitude, the existentialists split the difference. Sartre and Nietzsche agree with Bem that no attitude sits hidden behind the act waiting to be read: "'the doer' is merely a fiction added to the deed – the deed is everything" (*Genealogy*, essay 1, section 13), and "feeling is formed by the deeds that one does" (*Existentialism Is a Humanism*). But Bem's student looks at himself as an outsider would, and that is the stance Sartre calls the sincere man's bad faith. His "sincere man" is the person who declares once and for all what he is, "I am a liar", and so turns a free person into a fixed thing; that too is a flight, constituting oneself "as what he is in order not to be it" (pp. 110 to 111). For all of these writers the interesting question is not which mechanism moved the number, but whether the student was willing to own what he had done. The experiment had no measure for that.

**The excluded students were the lucid ones.** Eleven students were set aside, all from the paid groups. Five said in the interview that they suspected the payment to lie was the real purpose of the experiment. Two told the woman they had been hired. Three refused the money and the job. One asked for the woman's phone number so that he could explain (p. 207). In the tradition's terms, the five who voiced a suspicion showed lucidity: they saw, or guessed, what was being done to them. The two who told the truth refused, in Camus's words, to mask the evidence. The paper tells us nothing about how much the other students saw. But the design needed students who did not see through the setup, so, strictly, the finding is about belief under what Beauvoir would call mystification. Beauvoir would put the main responsibility for that on the people who arranged it. She would not reject the method outright, though. She held that inquiry always goes ahead without full knowledge, since the scientist "does not wait for the light of completed knowledge to break upon it" (*The Ethics of Ambiguity*, Part III). In plain words: no scientist waits until everything is known before starting to study something.

**Does being in error hurt? Kierkegaard doubts it.** Festinger's theory assumes that holding two clashing thoughts is unpleasant, and the paper inferred the discomfort from the ratings without measuring it. Kierkegaard wrote: "to be in error is, quite un-Socratically, what men fear least of all" (*The Sickness unto Death*, p. 43). In plain words: Socrates thought that nobody wants to be wrong, and Kierkegaard says the opposite. Most people are quite comfortable in error and do not want to be shown it. He also held that "the greater the degree of consciousness, the more intensive the despair" (p. 42). His despair is a religious idea, a failure to be one's true self before God, and it can be present without being felt. On a loose reading, though, his view suggests that a student who never looked closely at his two thoughts may have felt little discomfort at all. Nietzsche adds that a need is often an effect, not a cause (*The Gay Science*, section 205), and Camus grants that the longing for unity is real but denies that it gives anyone a right to be satisfied. Each objection points to something the study could have measured: discomfort directly, before and after the change, and in each student rather than as a group average.

**Where the writers split on knowledge.** Sartre and Nietzsche disagree about where the real work happens. For Nietzsche the causes sit below consciousness. He describes forgetting as "like a doorkeeper, a preserver of psychic order" (*Genealogy*, essay 2, section 1): an active force that keeps unwanted memories out, as a doorkeeper keeps out unwanted visitors. And a stated motive is only "a ripple on the surface of consciousness" (*Twilight*, "The Four Great Errors", section 3), meaning that the reasons we give are small signs of deeper forces. That picture is close to Festinger's and Bem's. Sartre's argument against the censor is aimed at exactly such a doorkeeper. On the value of truth they split again: Nietzsche asks why we should prefer truth, while Sartre, Camus and Beauvoir make clear sight the first duty. Sartre even says of the self-deceiver: "it is not for me to judge him morally, but I define his self-deception as an error" (*Existentialism Is a Humanism*). In plain words: Sartre's complaint is not only moral; the self-deceiver has simply got the facts wrong. Kierkegaard sides with the demand for truth, but on Christian grounds: for him a self is healthy only when it rests openly, hiding nothing from itself, in God.

## Where it agrees, and where it clashes

The agreements are real. The study shows that belief follows action, and "existence precedes essence" is a claim of just that shape. The study locates the change inside one person's own activity, not in a hidden second self. And the study found that a large payment produced less change, which for Sartre shows what an excuse does: once it is good enough, it lets a person keep their old self untouched.

The clashes are about vocabulary and about what the design leaves out. The paper speaks of a drive that pushes; the existentialists speak of a flight the person chooses. The paper treats the size of the payment as the cause; Sartre would say the cause is what each student made of the payment. The paper's measure of success is a number on a scale; the existentialists' is whether a person owns their act. And the students who refused, who are the clearest cases of owning an act, appear in the paper only as discarded data.

## Questions it would put to the researchers

- You never measured discomfort. What is the difference between saying a student "reduced dissonance" and saying he chose to stop looking at what he had done?
- Three students refused and two told the woman the truth. Why are they absent from the result, and what would it take to make them the finding?
- Is twenty dollars an explanation of a lie, or an excuse for it? Is one dollar?
- Your cover story relied on students trusting "the department" and a professor. How much of your effect is the authority you borrowed, and how much is the money?
- You told every student that "most of our subjects" found the tasks interesting. Is that a help to self-persuasion, or an invitation to become like the others?
- You deceived students in layers and then asked them, through another deception, to be completely honest. What did honesty mean in that room, and for whom?
- Your interview asked each student to report a state. How do you tell a state that was there before you asked from one that the asking helped to make? (Sartre)
- You assume the $20 students knew why they lied. Why trust their unchanged rating more than the $1 students' changed one? (Kierkegaard)
- You measured whether students resolved the contradiction. Why not ask whether any of them held it clearly without resolving it? (Camus)

## Cautions

- **The label is loose.** Kierkegaard's "good" is willed before God, and his despair is a theological idea. Sartre argued from atheism. Camus called Kierkegaard's leap "philosophical suicide". Name the writer you are using.
- **Camus's subject is the meaning of life**, not a lie about peg-turning. Using his ethic for the $1 student is an analogy.
- **Nietzsche's slave revolt is a story about European morality.** His contempt for the "weak" is not something to import. Use his observation about memory and pride, not his judgements.
- **Nietzsche on truth is disputed.** The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy (entry "Friedrich Nietzsche", section 6.2) sets out several ways scholars reconcile his doubts about the value of truth with his praise of honesty, including the view that he gave up his denials of truth late in his career. Do not present one side as his settled view.
- **"Truth is subjectivity" is not on this page for a reason.** The phrase comes from *Concluding Unscientific Postscript*, written under the pseudonym Johannes Climacus. The Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy notes that "Climacus, but not Kierkegaard, proclaims" it. *The Sickness unto Death* is also pseudonymous, by Anti-Climacus.
- **Sartre's non-positional consciousness is not Freud's unconscious, and it is not knowledge.** Sartre says both things directly (pp. 116 and 740). Older translations say "accessory reflection" where Richmond says "complicit reflection".
- **Do not claim that Festinger proved an unconscious drive.** He inferred a drive; the existentialist point is that the inference is doing the work.
- **Translation.** Mairet's 1948 version of the lecture says "self-deception" where later translations say "bad faith"; the French is the same word. Sartre's famous line "Hell is other people" is from his play *No Exit* and does not appear in the works used here.
