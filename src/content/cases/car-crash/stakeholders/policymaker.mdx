## What you care about

Imagine you work in a state Home Department in India, responsible for police training and procedure. You care about convictions that are fair, and about witnesses whose evidence holds up in court. You know that cases take years, that witnesses often change their story between the police station and the courtroom, and that Indian courts are full of arguments about whether a witness's account can be trusted. You want to know whether this study says anything you can act on.

## What worries you about the original study

- **It is a laboratory study of students.** It used short films and printed questions, with nothing at stake (pp. 586-587). It cannot tell you how much a leading question changes the evidence of a frightened witness questioned by a police officer.
- **It asked the leading question once, and tested memory once more a week later.** Real witnesses in India are often questioned many times. On 8 November 2026, the National Judicial Data Grid showed over 4.09 crore criminal cases pending in district courts. About 59% were more than a year old, and about 9.5% more than ten years old (percentages calculated by this guide from the dashboard's age bands). A witness may testify years after the event, after many retellings.
- **It says nothing about how statements are recorded.** In India, the first record of what a witness remembers is usually written by the police officer. Two laws govern these statements: the old Code of Criminal Procedure (CrPC, sections 161 and 162), and now the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 (BNSS, sections 180 and 181). Under both, the officer "may reduce into writing" the statement, and the witness does not sign it. The statement can be used in court only to contradict the witness. So the witness's own words may never be recorded at all.
- **It does not test translation.** A witness may speak Marathi, the officer may write in English or Hindi, and the court may record evidence in yet another language. The law expects translation (BNSS sections 312 and 313). Every translation is a point where a word like *hit* can become *smashed*. No Indian study of this was found.

## Your redesign

A policymaker would not rerun the 1974 experiment. They would fund a programme of evidence on the questions that matter for Indian policing:

1. **A trial of audio-video recording.** In some police stations, chosen at random, witness statements in serious cases and road accidents are recorded on video, as BNSS section 180 now allows. Other stations continue with written statements. Researchers compare how often witnesses later dispute their statements, and how consistent their accounts are in court. They also count how often witnesses are declared <Term>hostile</Term>. A hostile witness is one who in court goes back on their statement to the police.
2. **Training in open questions.** Officers in some districts are trained to begin every interview with an open invitation ("Tell me everything you saw") and to avoid questions that suggest an answer. Recorded interviews are scored for leading questions before and after training.
3. **Record the questions as well as the answers.** Every recorded statement includes the officer's questions, so a court can see whether a leading question shaped an answer.
4. **Early recording before a magistrate.** In key cases, the statement of an important witness is recorded early by a magistrate (BNSS section 183, old CrPC section 164), also by audio-video means.
5. **Wider use of vulnerable witness deposition centres.** In *Smruti Tukaram Badade v. State of Maharashtra* (order of 11 January 2022), the Supreme Court directed High Courts to set up at least one permanent vulnerable witness deposition centre in every district court establishment. These are protected rooms where children and other vulnerable witnesses can give evidence away from the accused. The programme checks whether they exist and are used.
6. **A study of translation.** The same recorded statements are translated by different interpreters, and researchers measure how often key words change.

## Why

The study's best-supported lesson is that the wording of questions can change what witnesses report. All 76 experts in a recent survey agreed with it (Seale-Carlisle and colleagues, 2024).

Indian law already restricts leading questions in court. The Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023 (BSA), section 146, keeps the wording of sections 141 to 143 of the Indian Evidence Act of 1872 almost unchanged. It defines a leading question as "Any question suggesting the answer which the person putting it wishes or expects to receive". <Term>Examination-in-chief</Term> is the questioning of a witness by the side that called them. <Term>Cross-examination</Term> is the questioning of the same witness by the other side. A leading question must not be asked in examination-in-chief "if objected to by the adverse party", except with the court's permission. It may be asked in cross-examination. These rules govern questioning in court. The study suggests that the questions that matter most may come much earlier, from the first officer at the scene.

The government's own data show the problem. The Ministry of Road Transport and Highways admits two weaknesses in its accident data (*Road Accidents in India 2023*, para 10.8). The data are "not evidence based as the policeman who goes to the spot would normally record the data after returning depending on his memory". They are also "often affected by the value judgement and biases of the policeman". The ministry's finding that over-speeding accounted for 68.1% of road deaths in 2023 rests partly on such judgements of speed.

Other countries offer models. The US Department of Justice tells officers to "Avoid leading questions (e.g., 'Was the car red?')" (*Eyewitness Evidence*, 1999). In England and Wales, official guidance warns that leading questions "may also significantly distort the witness's memory" and should be used "only as a last resort" (*Achieving Best Evidence*, 2023, para. 3.69). A review of 42 studies found that the cognitive interview, a structured interview built on open questions, produced many more correct details than standard police interviews (Köhnken, Milne, Memon and Bull, 1999).

The Supreme Court has also accepted that memory has limits. A witness "cannot be expected to possess a photographic memory", and estimates of time "by guess work on the spur of the moment" are unreliable (*Bharwada Bhoginbhai Hirjibhai v. State of Gujarat*, 1983).

## What your redesign gives up

- **Money and equipment.** Cameras, storage and secure systems in thousands of police stations are expensive, and recordings must be protected from leaks.
- **Training time.** Officers who already carry heavy caseloads need days of training and supervision to change long habits of questioning.
- **Recordings can be used against witnesses.** A full record shows every hesitation and small inconsistency. Lawyers may use these to attack honest witnesses, the very "minor discrepancies" the Supreme Court warned against in *Bharwada* (1983).
- **Slow evidence.** A trial that follows cases from statement to verdict could take years, because the cases themselves do.
