The outline asks you to name the design "as clearly as possible", with a reason. Here is a full description, followed by an explanation of each part.

<Callout title="The design in one sentence">
Two laboratory experiments with independent groups. In Experiment 1, the independent variable was the verb in one question about speed, at five levels, and the dependent variable was the speed estimate, with each student judging seven films shown in different orders. In Experiment 2, the independent variable had three levels (the speed question with *smashed*, with *hit*, or no speed question), and the dependent variables were the speed estimate and a "yes" or "no" answer about broken glass one week later.
</Callout>

## Why it is an experiment

An <Term>experiment</Term> is a study in which the researcher deliberately changes one thing, keeps everything else the same, and measures the effect. This study does both.

1. **The researchers set the independent variable.** They decided which verb each student read. Students did not choose their condition, and nothing suggests they knew other versions of the question existed.
2. **Everything else was held constant.** All students in an experiment saw the same films and answered printed questionnaires that differed in a single word. In Experiment 2, the broken glass question was identical for everyone.

Because only one word changed, a difference between conditions can be traced to that word. This is the main strength of the design: few experiments in psychology isolate their cause so cleanly.

There is one gap in the report. To claim that the conditions caused the differences, an experiment also needs participants to be placed in conditions by chance, called <Term>random assignment</Term>. The paper does not say how students were assigned to verbs. Many textbooks assume it was random. If you say so, cite a textbook, not the paper.

## Why it is a laboratory experiment

A <Term>laboratory experiment</Term> takes place in a setting the researchers control. Here the "event" was a film, the "witnessing" happened in a room with a screen, and the "interview" was a printed questionnaire. The alternative would be a <Term>field study</Term> of real witnesses to real accidents. That gains realism but loses control: researchers cannot decide which witnesses hear which question, and they rarely know what really happened.

The laboratory gave the study something no field study can: a known truth. The researchers knew there was no broken glass in the film, and they knew the real speeds of four of the crashes in Experiment 1 (p. 586). So they could say with certainty that a report was wrong. In a real case, nobody can.

The price is realism. The students watched short films, not real events. Nobody was hurt, nobody they knew was involved, and nothing depended on their answers. The [strengths and limitations page](/cases/car-crash/strengths-limitations/) looks at what this means.

## Why it is an independent-groups design

In an <Term>independent-groups design</Term>, also called a between-subjects design, each participant takes part in only one condition. No student read the speed question with both *hit* and *smashed*. This was the only sensible choice. A student who read "smashed" for one film and "contacted" for the next would quickly notice the change and guess the purpose.

The risk of an independent-groups design is that the groups differ before the study starts. Some people may simply give higher speed estimates than others, for example drivers compared with non-drivers. With only 9 students per verb in Experiment 1, one or two students who always guess high could move an average. Random assignment reduces this risk but does not remove it, and the paper does not report how much answers varied within each group.

<Note title="Seven films per student">
In Experiment 1, each student judged all seven films, so the films were a <Term>repeated measure</Term>: the same people were measured several times. The verb stayed the same for a student across all seven films. Showing several films gives more than one estimate per student, which makes each student's average more reliable than a single guess. The analysis also treated the films as a sample of possible accident films, following Clark (1973), so that the result is meant to apply to accident films in general, not only to these seven (p. 586).
</Note>

## Controlling order

In Experiment 1, "A different ordering of the films was presented to each group of subjects" (p. 586). If the films had always been shown in the same order, the last film would always be judged by tired or bored students. Changing the order spreads such effects across films. This is <Term>counterbalancing</Term>. The paper does not say how many orders were used or how they were chosen.

In Experiment 2, the broken glass question "appeared in a random position" in a list of 10 questions (p. 587). This hides the question among others, so students are less likely to see it as the point of the study. It also means that whether the question came early or late in the list did not depend on the condition.

## The two comparison groups in Experiment 2

Experiment 2 had two groups to compare with *smashed*, and they control for different things.

| Comparison | What changes between the groups | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| *smashed* against *hit* | Only the verb. Both groups answered the same speed question | Whether the violent verb, rather than any speed question, produces reports of glass. This is the cleanest test |
| *smashed* against control | Two things: whether there was a speed question at all, and the verb | Whether the whole experience of the *smashed* question raises reports of glass above the level with no suggestion |
| *hit* against control | Whether there was a speed question with a mild verb | Whether simply thinking about speed raises reports of glass. It barely did: 7 against 6 out of 50 |

The <Term>control group</Term> shows the <Term>baseline</Term>: how often students "remember" broken glass when nothing has suggested it. It turned out not to be zero. 6 of 50 students said yes with no suggestion at all, probably because broken glass is part of most people's idea of a car crash.

## Measuring with closed questions

All the measures that the paper analyses are answers to <Term>closed questions</Term>, questions with a fixed form of answer: a number, or "yes" or "no". Closed questions are easy to score and need no judgement from the researcher, so scorer bias was not an issue.

They have two costs. First, the broken glass question offered only "yes" and "no". There was no "I don't know" or "not sure". A student who had no memory either way had to guess, and some guesses would be "yes". Second, the questions were themselves specific. Asking "Did you see any broken glass?" puts the idea of broken glass in front of every student, in every group. Gross cites Cohen (1993) for the point that many errors in eyewitness experiments come from forcing "yes" or "no" answers to direct questions.

The paper also collected open answers. Students first gave a free account of each accident in their own words (pp. 586-587). These accounts could have shown whether anyone mentioned broken glass, or described the crash as more violent, without being asked. The paper does not report them.

## Who knew what

| Person | Knew the condition or the purpose? | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| The students | Presumably not. The paper does not say what they were told | Students who guessed the purpose might have given the answers they thought were wanted |
| The person running each session | Not reported | Printed questions limit the risk, because the wording was fixed on paper |
| Whoever counted the answers | Not reported | Numbers and ticks leave little room for bias in scoring |

The printed questionnaire is a quiet strength. In a spoken interview, an experimenter might stress *smashed* or say it with a tone of voice. On paper, every student in a condition read exactly the same words.

## What the design can and cannot show

| Question | Can the design answer it? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Does the verb in a question change speed estimates? | Yes | Only the verb changed between conditions, in two separate experiments |
| Does a violent verb raise later reports of a detail that was not there? | Yes, for this detail | Experiment 2 compared *smashed* with *hit* and with a control group, a week later |
| Was the original memory replaced? | No | Nothing tested whether the original memory could still be retrieved |
| Would real witnesses to a real accident be affected in the same way? | No | Students watched short films with nothing at stake |
| Would a "don't know" option or open questions change the result? | No | Only forced "yes" or "no" answers were analysed |
| Does the effect last longer than a week, or grow with repeated questioning? | No | The only delay tested was one week, with one leading question |
| Does it hold for children, older adults, other languages or other countries? | No | Only students, of unreported age and background, in the United States |

<Try
  scenario="A student proposes a cheaper version of Experiment 2: 'Show the film to one class. Ask everyone the smashed question. A week later, show the film to another class and ask everyone the hit question. Then compare the broken glass answers between the two classes.'"
  question="Name two problems with this design that the real Experiment 2 avoided, and explain how they could produce a difference even if the verb had no effect."
>
First, the two conditions would come from two different classes. The classes might differ before the study starts: one might contain more drivers, more students who have seen real accidents, or simply students who say "yes" more readily. Any difference in broken glass answers could come from these differences instead of from the verb. This is the risk that random assignment is meant to reduce.

Second, the two classes would be tested at different times, a week apart, and possibly by different people in different moods or rooms. Something that happened in between, such as a crash in the news, could change how the second class answers. The real Experiment 2 ran all three conditions under the same procedure, and it also included a control group with no speed question, which this proposal drops. Without it, there is no baseline for how often students "remember" glass with no suggestion.
</Try>
