## Which perspective does the study mainly represent?

The short answer: **social psychology, explaining social behaviour through cognition.** The situation it studies is social: a request from an authority figure, a lie told to another person, a payment. The explanation it offers is cognitive: two thoughts that do not fit, and a mind that works to make them fit. Many historians treat Festinger's theory as one of the first steps towards the "social cognitive" approach that dominates social psychology today.

The outline asks you to choose one perspective, so you need a reason for your choice. Here is the evidence for each candidate, taken from the study itself.

| Perspective | What in the study fits it | What does not fit |
|---|---|---|
| Social | The topic is attitude change under social pressure. The cause is staged as a social situation: a polite request, a waiting woman, a cover story about the department. Festinger trained under Kurt Lewin, a founder of social psychology, and the paper appeared in the *Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology*. | The study measures what happens inside one person. It does not study groups, norms or relationships, and the social setting is a means to an end. |
| Cognitive | The explanation is about cognitions and whether they fit. The theory has "cognitive" in its name. The prediction depends on how the student thinks about his reasons for lying. | The paper measures no thinking directly: no memory, attention or reasoning. Cognition is inferred from ratings. |
| Behavioural | The independent variable is a reward, the classic behaviourist variable. The students' act, the lie, comes before the change in opinion. | The result goes against reinforcement theory: the smaller reward had the larger effect. The paper explains it through an inner state, which behaviourists rejected. |

Hock places the study in a chapter on motivation and emotion. That also makes sense. Festinger treated dissonance as a <Term>drive</Term>, an unpleasant inner state that pushes a person to act, as hunger does. If you choose "cognitive", say that the theory is about *motivated* thinking: the mind is not calmly processing information but working to escape discomfort.

The strongest answer names the mix. The study uses a social situation to test a cognitive and motivational explanation, and it was designed partly as an argument against the behaviourist account of reward.

<Note title="A study that started a long argument between perspectives">
Eight years later, Daryl Bem (1967) offered a behavioural reinterpretation. He argued that the $1 students did not feel any inner tension. They simply looked at their own behaviour, as an outside observer would, and concluded: "I said it was fun, and I wasn't paid enough to lie, so I must have found it fun." This self-perception theory needs no inner drive at all. The debate between dissonance and self-perception is one of the clearest examples in psychology of two perspectives explaining the same data in different ways. The [behavioural lens](/cases/cognitive-dissonance/lenses/behavioural/) page explains it in detail.
</Note>

## What the research assumes

Every study rests on beliefs that it does not test. These are its <Term>assumptions</Term>. The outline asks about five.

### Human nature

The study assumes that people **need their thoughts and actions to fit together**, and that this need is part of human nature. It also assumes that people are not simply rational. A rational person paid $1 to tell a small lie would shrug and move on. The theory says instead that people bend their beliefs to justify what they have done. In this view, humans are rationalising rather than rational.

There is a second assumption about the person. The student is treated as an individual who owns his actions: if he chose to lie, the lie is his, and he must account for it to himself. The paper notes that the two cognitions clash "at least in our culture" (p. 203). It does not explore what happens in cultures where a person's actions are understood more through relationships and duties than through personal choice.

### Knowledge

The study assumes that we gain reliable knowledge through **controlled experiments that test predictions derived from a theory**. The paper's language is formal: it states "derivations" and tests them. This is the <Term>hypothetico-deductive method</Term>: start with a theory, derive a prediction, and test it against data.

It also assumes that **deception can give access to the truth**. The researchers believed that students would only reveal their real opinions if they did not know what was being studied. So the true purpose was hidden behind several layers of false stories. Knowledge here comes from the researcher's control of what the participant knows.

### Truth

The study assumes there is **a general law of the mind to discover**, and that 60 male students at one university can reveal it. A difference between group averages is taken as evidence of a process that operates in people generally. The phrase "at least in our culture" is a small exception: the authors knew the claim might be cultural, but the rest of the paper treats the result as general.

It also assumes that a student's private opinion is a fixed, real thing inside him, and that an honest interview can read it off as a number.

### Change

The study assumes that **attitudes can follow behaviour**. Common sense says we act on our beliefs. This study says the reverse can happen: what we do changes what we believe. The practical lesson is surprising. If you want to change someone's attitude, get them to act differently first, with as little pressure as possible. Too much reward or threat gives them an outside excuse, and their attitude stays the same.

### Behaviour

The study treats **saying something as a behaviour** and the later rating as an **opinion**. The lie to the woman counts as the act; the number given to the interviewer counts as the inner state. This division is an assumption. A student who tells the woman "it's interesting" and later tells the interviewer "+2" is, in both cases, saying something to someone. Critics from discursive and social perspectives ask why one statement is treated as behaviour and the other as a window into the mind. Several lenses in Section C return to this point.

<Try
  scenario="A classmate says: 'This study must be behavioural. The independent variable is a reward, and the dependent variable is what people said.'"
  question="Is your classmate right? How would you answer?"
>
Partly right about the variables, but not about the perspective. A reward was manipulated and the measures were verbal reports, which a behaviourist would accept. But a behaviourist would predict that the larger reward has the larger effect. The study found the reverse, and explained it through an inner state, dissonance, which strict behaviourism does not allow.

A good answer is that the study borrows a behaviourist variable in order to argue against the behaviourist account. Its explanation is cognitive and motivational, and its subject matter is social. Daryl Bem's self-perception theory later showed that a behaviourist reinterpretation is possible, which makes this a good case for comparing perspectives.
</Try>
