These questions are different from the "Try this" boxes on the other pages. Each one gives you a situation you have not seen before. All eight situations, including the "replications", are invented for practice; none reports a real study. Write your own answer first, in two or three sentences, and then open the reasoning.

<Try
  label="1. Imitation or disinhibition?"
  scenario="After watching a wrestling show, a six-year-old does a very particular move he saw on screen: he climbs onto the sofa arm, shouts the wrestler's catchphrase, and drops onto a cushion with his elbow out. His sister, who watched too, simply pushes him harder than usual when they play."
  question="Which child shows clearer evidence of observational learning, and why?"
>
The boy. He reproduced a novel, specific sequence, the climb, the catchphrase and the elbow drop, that he is unlikely to have invented on his own. This mirrors the logic of the Bobo study, which built its test around unusual acts so that exact copies would point to learning.

The sister's harder pushing could be explained by disinhibition: watching aggression may have lowered her restraint, but she did not copy a new act. The Bobo paper made the same distinction. It argued that general increases in aggression could come from weakened inhibition, while "novel patterns" copied exactly provide "striking evidence for the occurrence of learning by imitation" (p. 580).
</Try>

<Try
  label="2. Learning versus performance"
  scenario="In an imagined replication, a girl watches the aggressive model but plays only with the tea set during the test. Afterwards the experimenter says, 'I'll give you a sticker for every thing you can show me that the grown-up did.' She immediately sits on the doll, punches its nose and says 'Pow!'"
  question="What does this show, and what does it imply for the 1961 results for girls?"
>
It shows that she had learned the model's acts but had not performed them in free play. Learning and performance are different: what a child does depends on motivation and expectations, not only on what she has learned. Bandura tested this directly in 1965 and found that offering incentives largely wiped out the differences between groups.

For the 1961 study, it means the lower scores of girls may partly reflect a reluctance to perform aggression, perhaps because it was seen as unsuitable for girls, rather than less learning. Bandura (1965) suggested exactly this: sex differences may reflect "differences in willingness to exhibit aggressive responses, rather than deficits in learning" (p. 594). Even with incentives, though, girls in his study still reproduced somewhat fewer acts than boys.
</Try>

<Try
  label="3. Reading a control group"
  scenario="Suppose an imagined replication, with six children per group, found these averages for copied physical aggression: aggressive model 15, calm model 2, no model 4."
  question="Which hypotheses do these results support, and which do they fail to support?"
>
They support hypothesis 1: children who saw the aggressive model copied far more than both other groups.

Hypothesis 2 is the harder judgement. The calm-model average (2) is lower than the no-model average (4), which is the direction the hypothesis predicts. But with six children per group, a difference of 2 could easily come from one or two children behaving differently. You would need a significance test before claiming that the calm model reduced aggression, and even then you should look at whether the same pattern appears on other measures. This is exactly the position the 1961 authors were in with the calm male model: a consistent direction, but small groups and weak statistics. The control group is what makes the comparison possible at all.
</Try>

<Try
  label="4. A lens applied to a new case"
  scenario="A ten-year-old in Chennai has often seen crowds cheer his favourite fast bowler for an aggressive gesture the bowler makes at batters he has just got out. The boy copies the gesture after taking a wicket in a school match. His coach, who is strict, drops him from the team for the next game."
  question="How would the behavioural lens and the cognitive lens each explain what happens next season?"
>
The behavioural lens focuses on consequences. Being benched is punishment, so the gesture should become less frequent in front of the coach. But it may continue where it is not punished, for example in street cricket with friends, because the discriminative stimuli there signal no punishment. The cricketer's fame and success may also work as <Term>vicarious reinforcement</Term>, which means learning from seeing someone else rewarded.

The cognitive lens focuses on what the boy has stored and believes. He has a mental representation of the gesture and an expectation of when it is rewarded. He may still have learned it fully but now chooses not to perform it when the coach is present. His interpretation also matters: if he sees the gesture as "what great bowlers do", he may wait to use it later. Both lenses predict reduced performance in front of the coach; they differ in whether the learning has gone.
</Try>

<Try
  label="5. A newspaper claim"
  scenario="A newspaper headline says: 'Science proves cartoons make children violent: famous Bobo doll experiment.'"
  question="Identify three problems with this claim, using what you know about the study and its follow-ups."
>
First, the 1961 study did not use cartoons. Its models were live adults. The 1963 follow-up did include a cartoon model, and children copied it, but the measure was still behaviour towards a doll.

Second, "violent" overstates the measure. Children hit a toy built to be hit, a few minutes after watching. The study says nothing about violence towards people or about long-term change.

Third, "proves" ignores the limits: 72 children from one university nursery, small groups, scoring that was not fully blind, and a laboratory setting. A fair claim would be that young children readily copy aggressive acts they have just watched, including from film, in a laboratory playroom.
</Try>

<Try
  label="6. Ethics and design together"
  scenario="An ethics committee tells you that you may not deliberately frustrate children in your replication."
  question="What problem does this create for comparing your study with the original, and how could you deal with it?"
>
In 1961 every child, including the controls, was frustrated before the test, to make aggression more likely. Without this step, your children may show less aggression overall, so lower scores would not necessarily mean the model had a weaker effect.

You could deal with it in two ways. First, compare conditions *within* your own study rather than comparing raw scores with 1961: the question is still whether the aggressive-model group copies more than the others. Second, cite Bandura's 1965 study, which found clear imitation without any frustration step. You would report the difference from the original procedure honestly, as a limitation on direct comparison.
</Try>

<Try
  label="7. Who is the model?"
  scenario="In an imagined replication in a joint family setting, the researchers find that four-year-olds copy an aggressive 12-year-old cousin far more than an aggressive unfamiliar adult."
  question="Does this contradict the 1961 finding that the male adult model had the most influence? Explain."
>
Not directly. The 1961 study compared one unfamiliar man with one unfamiliar woman. It never tested a familiar older child, so it cannot say how such a model compares. The new result adds something: the status a model has *for the child* may matter more than the model's age or sex.

This fits the social and cultural lenses. Who counts as an important model depends on the child's daily life, and in households with many older children, cousins and siblings may be the most watched models. The 1961 conclusion about the male model is best read as a finding about two particular adults in one American nursery, not as a general rule.
</Try>

<Try
  label="8. Defending a classification"
  scenario="In a viva, you say the Bobo doll study belongs to the social perspective. The examiner replies: 'Social learning theory is just behaviourism with an extra step. The study is behavioural. Defend your choice or change it.'"
  question="How would you respond, using specific features of the study?"
>
You can defend "social" if you separate the study's methods from its cause. The methods are behavioural: observable acts, counted in five-second intervals, in a controlled room. But the variable the study manipulates, and the thing it shows to be powerful, is another person: a model whose sex and behaviour change what children do. The children's remarks about how "a lady" should behave show social norms working inside the experiment.

A strong answer also concedes the examiner's point. The paper's hypotheses are written in behaviourist language, about reinforcement histories and habit strength, so the study grew out of behaviourism. The best position is that it uses behavioural methods to study a social cause, and that this is why it is often classed under learning approaches. Showing that you see both sides earns more credit than simply holding your first answer.
</Try>
