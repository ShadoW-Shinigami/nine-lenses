## What you care about

As a clinician, perhaps a counsellor or clinical psychologist, you help people change behaviour that harms them: drinking, smoking, disordered eating, avoidance. Several methods you use have roots in dissonance theory. You care whether these methods work, whether they work for the reasons claimed, and whether they respect the client's freedom to choose. Persuading someone against their will, even for their own good, is not therapy.

## What worries you about the original study

- **The change was produced by trickery.** Students were steered, through a false story and an embarrassed request, into saying something they did not believe (p. 205). In therapy, a change that depends on the client not knowing what is happening would be a breach of trust.
- **The change was about something trivial.** The opinion was whether a peg-turning task was boring. Clinical change involves beliefs people care about deeply, such as "I need to drink to cope". It is not clear the same process applies.
- **Nobody measured how anyone felt.** Dissonance is described as unpleasant tension, but the study never measured discomfort or distress. Nobody checked whether the paid students left feeling worse.
- **The change was measured once, minutes later.** A clinician needs change that lasts weeks and months.
- **Seven students were excluded from the $1 group** (p. 207), including two who told the woman the truth. In a clinical trial, dropping the people who resist the treatment would make it look more effective than it is.

## Your redesign

1. **Use dissonance openly, with people's own goals.** Recruit people who already want to change a habit, for example students who want to drink less alcohol. Explain honestly that the session involves arguing for change in their own words.
2. **Compare an advocacy session with a control session.** In the advocacy session, participants record a short message to first-year students about drinking safely, then list times they themselves drank more than they meant to. In the control session, they hear an informative talk only.
3. **Vary choice, not money.** In one version, participants are told the advocacy task is optional and are free to decline; in the other, it is simply part of the session. This tests the factor later research found most important.
4. **Measure discomfort and wellbeing.** Use a short rating of discomfort immediately afterwards, and a check on mood, so that nobody leaves distressed without support.
5. **Follow people up for three months.** Measure behaviour, not only attitudes: drinks per week and heavy-drinking days, using a standard diary.
6. **Analyse everyone who started.** Include people who dropped out, rather than excluding them.

## Why

Dissonance ideas are already part of clinical practice. In motivational interviewing, developed by William Miller and Stephen Rollnick, the counsellor helps clients notice the gap between their goals and their current behaviour, an idea known in the motivational interviewing literature as developing discrepancy. Draycott and Dabbs (1998) argued that its techniques map closely onto dissonance principles. Miller and Rose (2009) linked the change-promoting value of hearing yourself argue for change to Festinger's dissonance theory and to Bem's self-perception theory. Eric Stice's Body Project, a prevention programme for young women with body-image concerns, asks participants to argue against the thin ideal in their own words, using dissonance as the mechanism. Joel Cooper (1980) found that people afraid of snakes improved more when they had chosen to take part in an effortful treatment, and that this held whether the treatment was a recognised therapy or a set of physical exercises with no therapeutic purpose. This is <Term>effort justification</Term>: people value what they have chosen to suffer for.

All of these use dissonance with the person's knowledge and towards a goal the person holds. That is the ethical line between therapy and the 1959 procedure. The redesign also tests choice, because studies after 1959 found that people who feel they had no choice show little attitude change.

## What your redesign gives up

- **It no longer tests the money prediction.** Paying people different amounts to argue for their own health would be odd. The study tests whether dissonance helps change, not whether less reward means more change.
- **Openness may weaken the effect.** People who know the session is designed to make them uncomfortable may brace against it.
- **Self-selection.** People who already want to change are easier to help, so the results may not apply to people who are not ready.
