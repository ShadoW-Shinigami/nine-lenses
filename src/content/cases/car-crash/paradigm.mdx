## Which perspective does the study mainly represent?

The short answer: **cognitive psychology, applied to a legal problem.** The study asks how a mental process, remembering, is changed by information arriving after an event. Its explanation is about what happens inside the mind: two kinds of information combined into one memory representation. The paper appeared in the *Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior*, a leading journal of the new cognitive psychology of memory and language. Its motivation is practical: the reliability of witnesses in accidents and courts.

The outline asks you to choose one perspective, so you need a reason for your choice. Here is the evidence for each candidate, taken from the study itself.

| Perspective | What in the study fits it | What does not fit |
|---|---|---|
| Cognitive | The question is about memory and how it is changed. The explanation is a mental one: a memory representation altered by new information (p. 588). The paper builds on Bartlett's reconstruction and on studies of verbal labels and memory for shapes. | The paper never observes memory directly. It infers it from written answers, and it accepts that a response bias could explain Experiment 1. |
| Social | The cause is a question asked by one person to another. The leading question is a social act, and the paper starts from the courtroom, where lawyers question witnesses (p. 585). | The study does not examine the relationship between questioner and witness. The questions were printed, and the researchers did not vary who asked them or how. |
| Behavioural | The study manipulates a stimulus, one word, and measures a response, a number or a tick. It contains no self-report about thoughts or feelings. | The paper rejects a purely response-level account. Experiment 2 was designed to show a change in memory, not only in responding (pp. 586-587). |

The strongest answer names the mix. The study is cognitive in its explanation and its home journal, but it borrows the experimental rigour of behaviourism and takes its problem from social and legal life. Gross describes eyewitness research of this kind as "an application of cognitive psychology to real-world, social phenomena": laboratory methods from cognitive psychology used to answer questions from everyday life.

<Note title="Language and memory">
The paper's subtitle is "An example of the interaction between language and memory". In 1974 this linked two areas often studied separately. Psycholinguistics, the study of how people understand and produce language, had shown that verbs carry more than their dictionary meaning. The paper cites the linguist Charles Fillmore (1971) on this point: *hit* and *smashed* imply different speeds and different consequences (p. 586). The study shows that what a verb implies can find its way into memory. The [cultural lens](/cases/car-crash/lenses/cultural/) asks what this means for witnesses who speak other languages.
</Note>

## What the research assumes

Every study rests on beliefs that it does not test. These are its <Term>assumptions</Term>. The outline asks about five.

### Human nature

The study assumes that **people are active interpreters, not recording machines**. A witness does not store a copy of the event and play it back. A witness builds a memory from what was seen and from what was heard later, and cannot always tell the two apart. In this view, every person is open to suggestion, honest people included. Nobody in the study lied. The students who reported broken glass were, as far as anyone can tell, reporting what they believed.

The study also assumes that these processes are **universal**: the same in every person, whatever their language, culture or experience. It tested only American students, gave no details about them, and drew conclusions about "a witness" in general (p. 586).

### Knowledge

The study assumes that **knowledge about memory comes best from controlled experiments**. To learn how witnesses remember, it did not interview real witnesses. It created a small, controlled version of witnessing: a filmed accident, a printed question, a tick box. Only one word changed between groups, so any difference could be credited to that word.

It also assumes that **group averages reveal how memory works in each person**. The finding is that the *smashed* group, on average, gave higher speeds. It does not show that every student who heard *smashed* changed their answer. Most of the *smashed* group did not report broken glass.

### Truth

The study assumes that **there is a fixed truth about the event, against which memory can be checked**. The film is the truth. There was no broken glass in it, so a "yes" is an error. The staged crashes had known speeds, so the estimates could be scored for accuracy (p. 586).

This assumption is what makes the study possible. In real cases, nobody has a film of the event, so nobody can be sure which details are true. The laboratory provides what the courtroom lacks: a known past. Notice, though, that the study treats truth as a matter of physical facts, such as speed and glass. It does not ask about other kinds of truth a witness may hold, such as what the event meant or how frightening it felt.

### Change

The study assumes that **memory can be changed from outside, quickly and without the person noticing**. A single word in one question, heard once, was enough to raise false reports of broken glass a week later. The students were not trying to change their memories and, as far as the paper reports, did not know it had happened.

This is a strong claim about change. It suggests that memory is never finished: it can be revised each time it is used, and other people's words can revise it.

### Behaviour

The study assumes that **what people write reflects what they remember**. A speed written on a questionnaire, or a tick beside "yes", is treated as a window into the memory representation. The alternative explanation the paper itself raises, response bias, is a reminder that this is an assumption: an answer can change for reasons that have nothing to do with memory (p. 586).

It also assumes that **the behaviour of interest is the individual's report**. Real witnesses talk to each other, to police and to family, and their accounts take shape in those conversations. The study removed all of that. Each student answered alone, on paper, about a film.

Ananya has the stronger case. The paper's aim goes beyond showing that one word changes one answer. It explicitly separates a response-level explanation from a memory-level one, and designs Experiment 2 to support the memory explanation (pp. 586-587). Its conclusion is about a memory representation that has been changed (p. 588). A behaviourist would not use that idea at all.

Kabir is right about the method. The study measured only observable responses, a number and a tick, under tightly controlled stimulus conditions, and that experimental style comes from the behaviourist tradition. A strong answer says that the study uses behaviourist methods to make cognitive claims, and that this gap between what was measured and what was concluded is exactly where the critics later attacked.
