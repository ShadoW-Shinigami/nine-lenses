## What you care about

A policymaker in public health or education thinks at the scale of millions of people. You face two kinds of decision connected to this study. The first is about persuasion: could campaigns that get people to commit to a healthy behaviour, rather than simply giving them facts, change behaviour more cheaply? The second is about research rules: what should ethics committees allow when a study needs to mislead its participants?

## What worries you about the original study

- **It tells you nothing about real behaviour.** The study measured a rating of a boring task, minutes after a lie. It did not measure whether anyone did anything differently afterwards.
- **It is far from the population you serve.** Sixty analysed participants, all male students at one American university. Your population includes women and men, rural and urban, of every age and education level.
- **The attitude did not matter to anyone.** Health behaviour involves habits, addiction, money and identity. Whether a peg-turning task was boring involves none of these.
- **Its methods would trouble an ethics committee today.** A fake department study announced in class, a hidden tape recorder, students required by their course to take part, and payments taken back at the end.
- **The core effect may be fragile.** A large multi-country replication of the induced-compliance method, published in 2024, did not support its central prediction about choice.

## Your redesign

A policymaker would not rerun the 1959 experiment. They would ask for a programme of evidence on the question that matters to policy:

1. **A field trial of commitment-based messages.** For example, randomly assign primary health centres or colleges in two or three states to different approaches for adult smokers: standard advice, or advice plus a short exercise in which smokers say, in their own words, why they would not want their children to smoke, and then reflect on their own smoking.
2. **Measure real outcomes.** Record quit attempts and tobacco use at three and twelve months, with checks where possible, not only attitudes.
3. **Test it across groups.** Include women and men, urban and rural sites, and different languages, because a message that works in one group may fail in another.
4. **No deception.** Participants know they are in a study comparing ways of helping smokers.
5. **Cost the approach.** How much does each quit attempt cost, compared with existing campaigns?

## Why

India has a large gap between belief and behaviour on tobacco, the kind of gap dissonance theory is about. In the second Global Adult Tobacco Survey (GATS-2, 2016-17), 91.3% of current smokers said they believed smoking causes serious illness, yet 47.9% of smokers said smoking had not harmed their own body (GATS-2 report, Tables 9.1 and 9.4). In all, 28.6% of adults used tobacco in some form. Information alone is not closing that gap. Dissonance research suggests that people who state a value in their own words and then notice that their behaviour falls short may be pushed to act. This is the idea behind <Term>hypocrisy induction</Term>, which Elliot Aronson and colleagues tested in the 1990s with students and condom use.

On research rules, India's ICMR *National Ethical Guidelines* (2017) already set limits. Studies using deception should "pose no more than minimal risk", be "conducted only when the research cannot be carried out without deception", have a plan for debriefing, and undergo full ethics committee review (Section 9.2.9). The guidelines also describe <Term>authorised deception</Term>, where participants agree in advance that some details will be hidden (Box 9.5). A policymaker would ask whether these rules, written for health research, are being applied consistently to psychology departments.

## What your redesign gives up

- **Speed.** Field trials with twelve-month follow-up take years.
- **A clean test of the theory.** A field trial can show whether a commitment-based message helps people quit. It cannot easily show whether dissonance, rather than simple reminders or social pressure, is the reason.
- **Respect for autonomy may be questioned.** Some will see deliberately making people feel like hypocrites as manipulative, even when it is done openly and for their health. A public campaign built on this idea would need public debate, not only evidence.
