These questions are different from the "Try this" boxes on the other pages. Each one gives you a situation you have not seen before. All eight situations, including the "replication", are invented for practice; none reports a real study or case. Write your own answer first, in two or three sentences, and then open the reasoning.

<Try
  label="1. The insurance form"
  scenario="After a minor collision in Chandigarh, an insurance company sends both drivers the same claim form. One question reads: 'How fast was the other vehicle going when it crashed into you?' Both drivers write speeds for the other vehicle well above what the police later estimate from the damage. Each driver would pay less if the other is found to blame."
  question="Besides the two explanations Loftus and Palmer considered, what third influence is at work here, and how could an investigator tell the three apart?"
>
Two influences come from the 1974 study. The verb "crashed into" may push an uncertain driver towards a higher number while the memory stays the same, which is response bias. Or the word may become part of the memory, so that each driver now remembers a harder impact, which is a change in memory. Speed is hard to judge, as the staged crashes in Experiment 1 showed (p. 586), so there is room for both.

The third influence is motive. Each driver gains if the other vehicle seems to have been going fast, so each may exaggerate on purpose, or may come to remember the event in the way that suits them. The 1974 students had nothing at stake, so the study could not separate this from the other two. An investigator could compare the drivers with an independent witness who has no stake, given the same form. If the independent witness also gives high speeds, the wording is doing some of the work. If only the drivers do, motive is the likelier cause. A neutral form, which asks "Describe what happened" and then "How fast was each vehicle going?", would reduce the influence of the wording for everyone.
</Try>

<Try
  label="2. Reading a replication"
  scenario="Imagine a replication at a college in Thiruvananthapuram with 20 students per verb. Average speed estimates are 52 km/h for 'smashed' and 48 km/h for 'hit'. The difference is not statistically significant (p = .30). A student concludes: 'This proves Loftus and Palmer were wrong.'"
  question="What can and cannot be concluded from this result?"
>
The result does not support a verb effect in this sample. The difference is in the same direction as in 1974, but it is small and could easily have come about by chance.

It does not prove the original was wrong. With 20 students per group, a study can only reliably detect fairly large differences, so a small real effect could be missed. The 1974 study itself had only 9 students per verb in Experiment 1, which tends to make effects look larger than they are. The fair conclusion is that this replication found no clear evidence of the effect. If such a result were real, it would fit with the two conceptual replications described on the [importance page](/cases/car-crash/importance/), which also found no verb effect. Larger, preregistered studies would still be needed to settle the question.
</Try>

<Try
  label="3. Adding a 'don't know' box"
  scenario="A researcher repeats Experiment 2 exactly, except that the broken glass question now offers three answers: 'yes', 'no' and 'I don't know'."
  question="Predict how the results might change, and explain what the change would tell you about the original finding."
>
Some students who would have answered "yes" or "no" as a guess will now choose "I don't know". If many of the original "yes" answers in the *smashed* group were guesses, pushed by the idea of a violent crash, the number of "yes" answers should fall, and the difference between groups may shrink. Loftus and Zanni (1975) found that when "I don't know" was allowed, many participants used it, and the wording of the question changed how often they did.

If the *smashed* group still says "yes" more often, even with an easy way out, that is stronger evidence that some students really believed they remembered glass. So the new option helps separate guessing, a form of response bias, from a change in what people believe they saw. The original design, with only "yes" or "no", could not do this.
</Try>

<Try
  label="4. The confident witness"
  scenario="In a trial in Lucknow, the prosecutor tells the court: 'The witness has told this story three times, to the police, to the magistrate and now to you, and each time she was completely certain. A memory this certain must be accurate.'"
  question="Using this study and later research, evaluate the prosecutor's argument."
>
Certainty after repeated questioning is weak evidence of accuracy. Each retelling is a chance for information from questions, conversations and the case itself to enter the memory, as the 1974 study and the misinformation research that followed suggest. The students who "remembered" broken glass gave no sign that they thought they were guessing. A memory can become more detailed and more confident while becoming less accurate.

But confidence is not worthless. Later research found that a witness's *first* report, collected promptly and without contamination, can be quite accurate. High confidence in a first identification, made under proper conditions, is also informative (Wixted and Wells, 2017). The right question is not "Is she certain now?" but "What did she say the first time, how was she questioned, and what was she told in between?"
</Try>

<Try
  label="5. The news replay"
  scenario="A bus overturns on a highway near Patna. A television channel replays the crash footage for two days with the caption 'Horror crash: speeding bus'. A week later, a reporter interviews six passengers. Four now say the bus was going 'very fast', although two of them told the police on the day that they had been asleep."
  question="Which ideas from the study and the later research explain what may have happened, and what does the case add that the 1974 study left out?"
>
The caption and the repeated footage are information supplied after the event, the second of the paper's "two kinds of information" (p. 588). Passengers who saw the coverage may have combined it with their own fragments of memory, until they could no longer tell what they saw from what they were shown. This is the misinformation effect, and the source monitoring framework explains it as a failure to keep track of where a memory came from. The two passengers who were asleep are a clear warning sign: they now report something they could not have seen.

The case adds three things the study left out. The information came from a trusted public source, not one printed word. It was repeated many times, not once. And the passengers were personally involved, frightened and probably talking to each other, which adds the co-witness influence studied by the [social lens](/cases/car-crash/lenses/social/). The effect in real life may therefore be stronger than in the laboratory.
</Try>

<Try
  label="6. The checkbox form"
  scenario="A district police office introduces a new one-page accident report form to save time. Witnesses tick boxes: 'Was vehicle A speeding? Yes / No', 'Did vehicle A jump the signal? Yes / No', 'Was the driver drunk? Yes / No'. There is no space for a free description."
  question="Using the design of the 1974 study, explain the weaknesses of this form, and suggest a better one."
>
Every question on the form is closed and specific, and each one suggests something about vehicle A: that it may have been speeding, jumped the signal or had a drunk driver. These are the features of the broken glass question that later critics worried about. A question puts an idea in front of every witness, and a forced "yes" or "no" with no "don't know" makes guessing likely. The form also removes the witness's own words, the part of the 1974 questionnaire that the paper collected but never reported.

A better form would start with an open request, "Describe what you saw, in your own words", written before any specific question. Specific questions would come after, worded neutrally ("What did you notice about the speed of each vehicle?"), each with a "don't know" option, and would ask about both vehicles equally. The form should record the witness's language and who wrote the answers down.
</Try>

<Try
  label="7. A bolder replication"
  scenario="An MSc student in Shimla proposes to go beyond Loftus and Palmer. With help from participants' parents, she plans to convince students that they were in a minor road accident as young children, an event that never happened, and then measure how many 'remember' it."
  question="How would an ethics committee compare this plan with the 1974 study, and what would it require?"
>
The 1974 study withheld its purpose and used one suggestive word about a film. Its risk was minimal, and the [ethics page](/cases/car-crash/ethics/) concludes it would be approved today with consent, a content warning and a debriefing. This plan is different in kind. It creates a false memory about the participant's own life, uses the authority of their parents, and involves an accident, which could be upsetting for anyone who has been in a real one. It resembles the "lost in the mall" studies, whose ethics were publicly disputed (Crook and Dean, 1999; Loftus, 1999).

A committee would treat it as more than minimal risk. Under ICMR guidelines any deception goes to full committee review, and the plan would need strong justification that no milder design could answer the question. The committee would probably ask for a less distressing event, screening out people who have been in accidents, careful consent from both students and parents, a thorough debriefing that checks the false memory has been corrected, and follow-up support. It might still refuse.
</Try>

<Try
  label="8. Reading the old statement"
  scenario="A road accident case in Vadodara comes to trial four years after the crash. Before the witness, a vegetable seller, gives evidence, the prosecutor gives him a copy of the statement the police wrote down on the day, in the officer's words, and tells him to read it carefully 'so that you do not get confused'. The statement says the car was 'speeding wildly'. In court, the witness uses the same phrase."
  question="Using the study's idea of two kinds of information, explain what may have happened to the witness's memory, and why this matters when the court weighs his evidence."
>
Loftus and Palmer proposed that memory combines information from the event with information supplied afterwards, until the person cannot tell which detail came from where (p. 588). Here, after four years, the witness's own memory of the crash has probably faded. The statement is information supplied after the event, in someone else's words, and reading it just before testifying makes it fresh and vivid. "Speeding wildly" works like the verb *smashed*: a phrase that implies a violent event, which the witness may now take for his own memory.

This matters because the court may treat agreement between the statement and the testimony as a sign of reliability. In fact the agreement may only show that the witness has absorbed the statement. The testimony is no longer an independent check on what the police recorded. A court should ask what the witness remembers before reading the statement, and should know who wrote the phrase. This is one reason the [India page](/cases/car-crash/india/) stresses that statements written in an officer's words, and long delays before trial, are risks to witness memory.
</Try>
