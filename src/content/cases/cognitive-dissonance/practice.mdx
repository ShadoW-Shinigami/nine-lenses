These questions are different from the "Try this" boxes on the other pages. Each one gives you a situation you have not seen before. All eight situations, including the "replications", are invented for practice; none reports a real study. Write your own answer first, in two or three sentences, and then open the reasoning.

<Try
  label="1. Reading a replication"
  scenario="Imagine a replication at a university in Bhopal, with 25 students per group. Average enjoyment ratings on the -5 to +5 scale are: Control -1.2, paid ₹10 -1.0, paid ₹500 -1.1. None of the differences is statistically significant."
  question="What can and cannot be concluded from these results?"
>
The results do not support the 1959 prediction. If the theory were right, the ₹10 group should have rated the tasks clearly higher than both other groups. Here all three groups rated the tasks as mildly boring, and the differences are tiny. The tasks were successfully boring, as the Control group shows.

But a non-significant result does not prove there is no effect. With 25 per group, the study can only detect fairly large differences reliably, so a small real effect could have been missed. You should also check whether the procedure created the conditions the theory needs: did students feel free to refuse, did they believe they had misled someone, and did ₹10 feel like a weak reason? The 2024 multi-lab study faced exactly this debate: its authors concluded the effect was not robust, while critics argued that its low-choice condition still felt fairly free.
</Try>

<Try
  label="2. Signed or anonymous?"
  scenario="A software company in Gurugram asks staff, as a favour, to praise a new internal app that most of them find clumsy. Everyone who agrees receives a voucher, worth either ₹100 or ₹2,000. In one office, staff record a short video, with their name and face, recommending the app to new joiners. In another office, staff fill in an anonymous online form praising it."
  question="In which office would you expect the smaller voucher to produce more liking for the app, and in which might the larger voucher do so? Use later research to explain."
>
In the video office, the smaller voucher should produce more liking. Staff have made an identifiable, public statement to real people who may believe them, so they feel responsible for it. With only ₹100 as an outside reason, there is more dissonance to reduce, and coming to like the app is one way to reduce it. This is the 1959 pattern.

In the anonymous office, the result may reverse. Carlsmith, Collins and Helmreich (1966) found exactly this split: when students told a waiting person face to face that a dull task was interesting, smaller payments produced more attitude change, but when they wrote an anonymous essay, larger payments did. Helmreich and Collins (1968) found the same with identified video recordings against anonymous audio. Without personal commitment there is little dissonance, and the reward works in the ordinary way. Remember that these were small studies, and that modern replications of the wider method have been less kind to the theory.
</Try>

<Try
  label="3. The hard way in"
  scenario="Two students join the same college theatre society. Neha passed three rounds of auditions, including one in front of the whole society that left her shaking. Faraz joined in the second semester when the society needed more members and simply signed up. Both find the first month of meetings slow and disorganised. Neha tells friends the society is 'amazing'; Faraz says it is 'a bit boring'."
  question="Which dissonance idea explains the difference, and what alternative explanation should you rule out before accepting it?"
>
This is effort justification, the idea tested by Aronson and Mills (1959) in their study of severe and mild initiations. Neha holds two cognitions that clash: "I went through something difficult and embarrassing to get in" and "the society is dull". She cannot undo the auditions, so she reduces the dissonance by valuing the society more. Faraz paid almost nothing to join, so there is nothing to justify.

Before accepting this, rule out self-selection. Students who are willing to go through three rounds of auditions may simply be more enthusiastic about theatre from the start. In the real 1959 initiation study, women were assigned to severe or mild initiations by the researchers, which removes this problem. In Neha and Faraz's case nobody assigned the conditions, so the comparison cannot show cause.
</Try>

<Try
  label="4. Spot the flaw"
  scenario="In a replication, the $1 condition is run in the mornings by a warm, chatty research assistant, and the $20 condition in the afternoons by a tired, brisk one. The same assistant who makes the payment offer also conducts the final interview. The $1 group rates the tasks as more enjoyable."
  question="Identify the design problems, and explain why the 1959 study avoided at least one of them."
>
There are two confounds. A <Term>confound</Term> is a variable that changes along with the independent variable, so that you cannot tell which one caused the result. First, the condition is mixed up with time of day and with the assistant's manner: a warm assistant might make students feel better about the whole experience. Second, the person who knows the condition also collects the ratings, so his expectations could shape the answers, through tone, follow-up questions or how he records them.

The 1959 study avoided the second problem: the interviewer was a different person who was "kept in complete ignorance" of each student's condition (p. 206). It did not fully avoid the first. The same experimenter made every offer and knew each condition, so his manner during the request could have differed between conditions without his knowing. A stronger design would use a script, rotate conditions across times of day, and if possible record the request to check that it was delivered the same way.
</Try>

<Try
  label="5. Blame the room"
  scenario="Students write an essay arguing for a fee increase they oppose, under high choice. Half are told beforehand that the laboratory's ventilation system 'makes some people feel tense and on edge'. The other half are told nothing. Only the students who were told nothing change their attitudes towards supporting the fee increase."
  question="Which theory, dissonance or self-perception, does this result support, and why?"
>
It supports dissonance theory. If students change their attitudes to reduce an unpleasant feeling, then giving them another explanation for the feeling, the ventilation, should remove the need to change: they can say "I feel uneasy because of the room", not "because of what I wrote". That is what happened.

Self-perception theory has trouble with this result. It says people infer their attitudes from their behaviour, without any unpleasant feeling. On that account, a story about the ventilation should make no difference, because there is no feeling to blame on it. This is the logic of Zanna and Cooper's 1974 pill study, which used a placebo described as causing tension instead of ventilation.
</Try>

<Try
  label="6. Whose choice was it?"
  scenario="Rohan wanted to study architecture, but after long family discussions his parents chose engineering for him, and he agreed. Two years later he still says engineering 'was not my choice'. His cousin Kavya chose engineering herself, against her parents' advice, and now finds it hard. She tells everyone it was 'the best decision of my life'."
  question="Explain the difference using dissonance theory, and say what the cultural lens would add."
>
Kavya feels personally responsible for a choice that has turned out badly, and she chose it against advice. The clash between "I chose this freely" and "this is hard and disappointing" is strong, and she reduces it by valuing her choice more. Rohan has an outside reason for being where he is: his parents decided. Like the $20 students, he can explain his situation without changing his opinion, so he has less dissonance and less need to justify the choice.

The cultural lens would add that in many Indian families, decisions such as a career are made together, and a person may not experience them as purely personal choices. Savani and colleagues (2010) found that Indian participants were less likely than Americans to describe actions as choices at all. So the amount of dissonance depends on how the person understands the decision, as well as on who made it. Rohan might also come to justify the family's choice, if he sees it as his duty to make it work. This is a prediction to test, not a certainty.
</Try>

<Try
  label="7. On the ethics committee"
  scenario="An MSc student in Mumbai submits a proposal to her university's ethics committee. She wants to repeat the 1959 procedure exactly: a false explanation, a confederate posing as a participant, a hidden audio recorder, and payments of ₹20 or ₹2,000 that are taken back at the end. Participants will be first-year students who take part for course credit."
  question="As a committee member, what would you ask her to change, and why?"
>
Start with the need for deception. Under the ICMR guidelines (2017) and the BPS and APA codes, deception is allowed only when the research cannot be done without it, the risk is minimal, and there is a plan to debrief. She should explain why the question cannot be answered another way, and consider authorised deception, where participants agree in advance that some details will be hidden.

Then the specific problems. The hidden recording needs consent, at least during debriefing, with an option to have the recording destroyed. Course credit requires an equal alternative activity for students who do not want to take part, and the person collecting consent should not be the students' own teacher. Taking the payment back at the end may be seen as unfair; the committee might ask her to let participants keep it, or to explain why reclaiming it is needed. Finally, the debriefing should address the fact that students were led to mislead someone, so that nobody leaves feeling ashamed. Each change protects participants, and each has a cost in realism that she should discuss in her proposal.
</Try>

<Try
  label="8. Checking a popular claim"
  scenario="A parenting video with millions of views says: 'Science proves it. Pay your children less for doing chores and they will love chores more. Festinger and Carlsmith showed this in 1959.'"
  question="Evaluate this claim using what you know about the study and later research."
>
The claim goes well beyond the evidence. The 1959 study tested adult male students, not children. It measured one rating of a boring task, minutes after a lie told to a stranger, not lasting love of an activity. And the lower payment did not make the students "love" anything: their average was +1.35 on a scale from -5 to +5, only mildly positive.

Later research adds more caution. The effect appears only when people feel they freely chose to act, and a child told to do chores may not feel free at all. When people have no choice, larger rewards can produce more positive attitudes. And the large 2024 replication of the modern version of the method did not find the key effect, while no one has run a large modern repeat of the $1 and $20 procedure itself. A fairer summary would be: "A famous 1959 study suggested that small rewards for saying something you don't believe can shift your opinion more than large ones, under certain conditions. Whether this reliably happens, and whether it applies to children's chores, is not established."
</Try>
