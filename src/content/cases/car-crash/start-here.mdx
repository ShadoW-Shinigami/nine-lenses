In a paper published in 1974, two researchers at the University of Washington in Seattle showed students short films of car accidents. Then they asked one question about the speed of the cars, and changed a single word in it from student to student. Students asked how fast the cars were going when they "smashed into" each other gave higher speeds than students asked about cars that "hit" or "contacted" each other. A week later, the "smashed" students were also more likely to say they had seen broken glass. There was no broken glass in the film. This page explains what you need to know before reading about how and why it happened.

## The people who did it

Elizabeth F. Loftus was the senior author. She grew up in Los Angeles, studied mathematics and psychology at the University of California, Los Angeles, and earned her doctorate in psychology at Stanford University in 1970, where she trained in <Term>mathematical psychology</Term>, the use of mathematical models to describe mental processes. Her early research was on <Term>semantic memory</Term>, the part of memory that holds general knowledge, such as the meanings of words and facts about the world. After three years teaching in New York, she joined the University of Washington in 1973. So at the time of this study she was an assistant professor in her first year there. She later became one of the best-known memory researchers in the world. She moved to the University of California, Irvine, in 2002. In 2021 she told a court that she had testified as an expert witness about 300 times, and only once for the prosecution (as reported by Reuters). Her later work as an expert witness, almost always for the defence, matters when the [power page](/cases/car-crash/power/) asks who speaks for this research.

John C. Palmer, the junior author, was an undergraduate. His own academic record lists this paper as his first publication. He was working as a research technician at the University of Washington from 1973 and finished his bachelor's degree in psychology there in 1976. He later earned a doctorate at the University of Michigan and returned to the University of Washington as a researcher on vision and attention. So the paper joins a young professor and a student at the start of his career.

The research was paid for by a grant from the Urban Mass Transportation Administration, part of the United States Department of Transportation (p. 585). A study about memory for traffic accidents was funded by the government department responsible for transport.

## How psychologists studied memory at the time

In the early 1970s, a new approach called <Term>cognitive psychology</Term> was growing fast. Cognitive psychology studies mental processes such as attention, perception, memory and language. Many cognitive psychologists thought of the mind as a system that takes in information, stores it and gets it back, much as a computer does. This way of thinking is called the <Term>information-processing</Term> approach.

Most memory experiments of the time asked people to learn lists of words or sentences, and then tested how many they could recall. These studies were well controlled, but they were far from everyday life. In a paper the next year, Loftus pointed out that current theories of memory came "largely from experiments involving lists of words or sentences", while many everyday memories are of "complex, largely visual, and often fast-moving events" (Loftus, 1975, p. 560). A car accident is one such event.

## Memory as reconstruction

An older idea pointed in a different direction. In 1932 the British psychologist Frederic Bartlett published *Remembering*. He asked English participants to read and later retell a Native American folk tale, "The War of the Ghosts". Over repeated retellings, the story became shorter and more like an English story. Unfamiliar details dropped out or changed into familiar ones. Bartlett concluded that remembering is not the replaying of a stored copy. It is <Term>reconstruction</Term>: each time we remember, we rebuild the event from fragments, filling the gaps with what we expect.

What we expect comes from <Term>schemas</Term>. A schema is a mental framework of general knowledge about a type of thing or event. Your schema for a car crash includes speed, noise, dented metal and, quite likely, broken glass. Schemas help us make sense of new experiences quickly. They can also add details that were never there.

In the same year, Leonard Carmichael, H. P. Hogan and A. A. Walter (1932) showed people simple line drawings, each with a name. The same drawing was named "eyeglasses" for some people and "dumbbells" for others. When people later drew the shapes from memory, their drawings looked more like the object named. A word given at the time had changed what was remembered. Loftus and Palmer cite this study as a forerunner of their own (p. 588).

## Witnesses, courts and leading questions

An <Term>eyewitness</Term> is a person who saw an event, such as a crime or an accident, and can describe it. <Term>Eyewitness testimony</Term> is the account such a person gives, usually to the police and later in court. Judges, juries and police have long treated an eyewitness as strong evidence.

Psychologists had questioned this for decades. Early in the twentieth century, the German-American psychologist Hugo Münsterberg (1908) and the American psychologist Guy Whipple (1909) reported that honest witnesses often disagree and often make mistakes. The paper adds that people are especially poor at judging time, speed and distance (p. 585). In one test, Air Force personnel who knew in advance that they would be asked about the speed of a moving car gave estimates from 10 to 50 miles per hour. The car was actually going at 12 miles per hour (Marshall, 1969, as cited on p. 585).

The law already knew that the way a question is asked can shape the answer. This is why courts have the idea of a <Term>leading question</Term>. The paper defines it as a question that, "either by its form or content, suggests to the witness what answer is desired or leads him to the desired answer" (p. 585). Courts limit when lawyers may ask such questions. Indian law has the same idea: the Indian Evidence Act of 1872 defined a leading question as one "suggesting the answer which the person putting it wishes or expects to receive" (section 141). The [India page](/cases/car-crash/india/) looks at the current law.

## The puzzle this study addresses

If people are poor at judging speed, their answer to a speed question must come partly from somewhere other than what they saw. Loftus and Palmer asked whether one source is the wording of the question itself. Some verbs describe a gentler event than others. To "contact" or "hit" another car sounds mild. To "smash into" it sounds violent.

There was a deeper question too. Suppose the word "smashed" does produce a higher speed. Does it only push the answer to that one question? Or does it change the witness's memory of the accident, so that later answers change as well? If memory is reconstruction, as Bartlett argued, then information given after an event could become part of the memory of the event. The second experiment was designed to test this.

<Note title="Miles per hour">
The students gave speeds in miles per hour (mph), the unit used on American roads. One mile per hour is about 1.6 kilometres per hour. So 30 mph is about 48 km/h and 40 mph is about 64 km/h.
</Note>

The first student will probably give a higher speed. "Crashed into" describes a more violent event than "touched", and people are poor at judging speed, so the wording has room to influence the answer.

There are two possible explanations. The word may simply push the answer: the student is unsure whether to say 20 or 30 km/h, and "crashed" tips the answer upwards, while the memory itself stays the same. Or the word may change the memory: the student now remembers a more violent collision, and may later "remember" details that fit it, such as fruit flying across the road. The first explanation affects only one answer. The second would affect later answers too. Telling these two apart is the purpose of Loftus and Palmer's second experiment.
