The outline asks you to name the design "as clearly as possible", with a reason. Here is a full description, followed by an explanation of each part.

<Callout title="The design in one sentence">
A laboratory experiment with an independent-groups design: one independent variable, payment for telling another person something the participant did not believe, at three levels ($1, $20, and a control group asked to say nothing), with opinions measured once, afterwards, by a blind interviewer, and with deception and a confederate used throughout.
</Callout>

## Why it is an experiment

An <Term>experiment</Term> is a study in which the researcher deliberately changes one thing, keeps everything else the same, and measures the effect. This study does both.

1. **The researchers set the independent variable.** They decided which students were asked to lie and how much they were offered. Students did not choose their condition.
2. **Everything else was held constant.** The tasks, the timing, the false explanation, the experimenter's scripted words, the friendly remark on the way to the interview and the interview questions were the same for everyone. The "For Group B" sheet was even shown to the Control students.

Because of this control, a difference between the $1 and $20 groups can be traced to the money. There is one gap in the report. A true experiment also needs participants to be allocated to conditions by chance, called <Term>random assignment</Term>. Textbooks say this happened, but the 1959 paper does not describe how students were allocated. You can call the study an experiment, but if you say assignment was random, cite a textbook, not the paper.

## Why it is a laboratory experiment

A <Term>laboratory experiment</Term> takes place in a setting the researchers control. Here every step, from the waiting room to the interview, was staged. The alternative would be a <Term>field experiment</Term>, carried out in ordinary life, for example by asking real employees to promote a product they dislike for different payments. A field experiment gains realism but loses control.

Notice that the laboratory here was disguised. Students believed the interview was part of a department survey, not of the experiment. The aim was to make the situation feel real and involving, so that students reacted naturally. Carlsmith later wrote a well-known guide to designing experiments of this kind with Elliot Aronson (Aronson & Carlsmith, 1968). They called this quality <Term>experimental realism</Term>: the situation has real impact on the participant, even if it is unlike everyday life. Likeness to everyday life they called <Term>mundane realism</Term>. This study has a lot of the first and little of the second.

## Why it is an independent-groups design

In an <Term>independent-groups design</Term>, also called a between-subjects design, each participant takes part in only one condition. No student was paid both $1 and $20. This was the only possible choice. A student who had already been paid $20 to lie, and then been asked to do it again for $1, would quickly suspect the real purpose.

The risk of an independent-groups design is that the groups differ before the study starts, for example if one group happened to contain students who enjoy repetitive tasks. Random assignment reduces this risk, and 20 students per group is small enough that chance differences between groups are possible.

## Why there is a control group, and what it does not control

The Control group did everything except tell the lie. It answers a question the paid groups cannot: what did students think of the tasks, after the same hour and the same false explanation, when no dissonance was created? Without it, there would be no way to tell whether the $1 group's opinion had moved up or the $20 group's had moved down.

But the Control group differs from the paid groups in **two** ways at once: its members told no lie, and they received no money. So comparing the $1 group with the Control group mixes the effect of lying with the effect of being paid a small sum. The comparison that tests the theory most cleanly is $1 against $20, where the lie was the same and only the money changed. A fuller design would add groups who were paid $1 or $20 but told no lie.

## Why opinions were measured only once

There was no measure of the students' opinions before the lie. This is called a <Term>post-test-only design</Term>. A pre-test would have been a problem here: a student asked "Were the tasks enjoyable?" before being asked to say they were, would be more likely to notice the contradiction and suspect the purpose. Instead, the Control group's rating stands in for what the paid students would have said without the lie. This works only if the groups were similar to begin with, which is another reason random assignment matters.

## Who knew what: blinding

Researchers who know which condition a participant is in can, without meaning to, influence the results. Keeping people unaware of the condition is called <Term>blinding</Term>.

| Person | Knew the condition? | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| The student | No: he did not know the real purpose, or that there were other payment amounts | Protects against students guessing the hypothesis and acting it out |
| The interviewer | No: "kept in complete ignorance" (p. 206) | Protects the main measure from the interviewer's expectations |
| The two raters of the tape recordings | No | Protects the check on the rival explanation |
| The experimenter | Yes: he made the offer and paid the money | His manner during the request may have differed between conditions without his knowing |

The experimenter could not be blind, because he had to make the offer. But he also ran the step most likely to differ: the request, the hesitation, and the encouragement if a student hesitated. The psychologist Robert Rosenthal later showed that experimenters' expectations can change participants' behaviour in subtle ways, a problem known as the <Term>experimenter expectancy effect</Term>. The design limits this risk by keeping the experimenter out of the interview.

## How opinions were measured

The dependent variable was measured by <Term>self-report</Term>: students said what they thought, first in their own words and then as a number on an 11-point <Term>rating scale</Term>. The paper analyses only the numbers. What the students said in the open part of the interview is not reported.

The study also includes two kinds of check:

- A <Term>suspicion check</Term> at the end of the interview, asking what the student thought the experiment was about and whether he suspected anything. Five students who suspected the real purpose were excluded.
- A check on a rival explanation: the blind ratings of the tape recordings, which tested whether the $1 students had argued harder.

It does not include a <Term>manipulation check</Term> of the key idea, dissonance itself. Nobody measured whether the $1 students actually felt more discomfort. Dissonance is inferred from the change in opinion that it is supposed to cause. Critics call this reasoning circular, and later researchers, as the [importance page](/cases/cognitive-dissonance/importance/) explains, tried to measure the discomfort directly.

## What the design can and cannot show

| Question | Can the design answer it? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Did students paid $1 rate the tasks as more enjoyable than students paid $20? | Yes | Same procedure and same lie, with only the payment changed, and a blind interviewer |
| Did the lie itself, rather than the small payment, cause the change? | Only partly | The Control group differs from the $1 group in two ways: no lie and no money |
| Did the students feel dissonance? | No | Discomfort was never measured; it is inferred from the result |
| Is dissonance the only explanation? | No | The design rules out rehearsal, but not later rivals such as self-perception or self-presentation |
| Would the effect last? | No | Opinions were measured once, minutes after the lie |
| Does it hold for women, older adults or people outside the United States? | No | 71 male Stanford students only |
| Does it apply to opinions people care about? | No | The opinion was about a dull task the students had no stake in |

<Try

  question="What is likely to happen to the result, and what does this tell you about why Festinger and Carlsmith used deception?"
>
The effect would probably disappear. Students who know the hypothesis can work out what is expected and may resist it or play along. More importantly, the note would no longer feel like their own choice to mislead someone. It becomes an obvious task done for the researcher, with the money and the study's purpose as clear outside reasons. Dissonance depends on feeling personally responsible for an act that goes against your opinion, so a fully explained, low-stakes note produces little of it.

This shows that the deception was not an extra but part of the mechanism. The cover story made the lie feel real and freely chosen, and the separate interview made the opinion measure seem private. It also shows the ethical cost built into this kind of research: the method may only work if participants are misled.
</Try>
