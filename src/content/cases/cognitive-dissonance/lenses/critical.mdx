## The lens in brief

Critical psychology asks how psychological knowledge is shaped by power, and whose interests it serves. Dennis Fox and Isaac Prilleltensky, who edited an influential introduction to the field in 1997, argued that mainstream psychology often treats social problems as problems inside individuals, and so leaves the institutions that create them unexamined. Two related approaches are useful here. <Term>Discursive psychology</Term>, developed by Jonathan Potter and Margaret Wetherell, studies what people do with their talk, rather than treating talk as a window into fixed inner states. <Term>Rhetorical psychology</Term>, developed by Michael Billig, treats thinking as a form of argument.

This lens stands far from the study. Festinger and Carlsmith take for granted that each student has a real private opinion, that a number on a scale can capture it, and that a controlled experiment is the best way to learn about it. The critical lens questions all three.

## How this lens reads the study

**Deception as the method, and as the topic.** The study is about how people cope after misleading someone. It was built entirely from misleading the students: the course instructor's announcement of a department survey weeks in advance, the false explanation, the story of the absent assistant, the hired "girl" posing as a participant, and the hidden tape recorder. The critical lens notices the asymmetry. The students were asked to deceive once, briefly, and their discomfort was the object of study. The researchers deceived at every step, and their own position was never examined.

**Who held power, and over whom.** The participants were students meeting a course requirement. The cover story was planted by their own course instructor, a person who would grade them. The request came from the experimenter in the name of the professor "who is in charge of this experiment" (p. 205). The theory describes the $1 students as having "chosen" to lie with little reason. A critical reading says the reason was there, but it was institutional: young men in a university, asked by people with authority over them, in a setting designed so that saying no felt awkward. Explaining their compliance as a matter of thoughts and justifications inside each student moves attention away from the institution that set the scene.

**An attitude is something you do in talk.** For discursive psychology, an attitude is not a fixed object stored in the mind. It is an evaluation that people produce in talk, in order to do something: to be polite, to persuade, to avoid blame, to sound reasonable. In the interview, each student was "first encouraged to talk about the matter" and then asked for a number (p. 206). The paper reports only the numbers. A discursive psychologist would want the talk. How did a $1 student describe the tasks to a stranger from the department? Did he joke, hedge, or say "it was all right, I suppose"? The average of +1.35 removes exactly the variation this lens treats as the data.

**Contradiction is normal.** Billig (1987) argued that common sense is full of opposing sayings, such as "look before you leap" and "he who hesitates is lost", and that people think by arguing between such positions. Holding views that do not fit neatly together is not a malfunction. It is what makes argument and judgement possible. A theory that treats inconsistency as an unpleasant drive to be eliminated assumes a particular ideal of the person: unified, consistent and private. The critical lens asks where that ideal comes from and whose ideal it is.

**Gender in the room.** Every participant was male. The waiting participant was a young woman, called "the girl" throughout the paper. The students' task was to persuade her, and one student "demanded her phone number" (p. 207). The paper thanks two women, Ruth Smith and Marilyn M. Miller, for help, without saying what they did. The critical lens notes how a scene of young men persuading a young woman was treated as a neutral setting for studying honesty, and how the women who made the study possible are nearly invisible in it. The [power page](/cases/cognitive-dissonance/power/) develops this point.

**Reasons measured in money.** The theory turns reasons for acting into amounts: $1 is a small justification, $20 a large one. This fits a market picture of the person, in which reasons are weighed like prices. Kindness to an embarrassed experimenter, or a wish to be a good student, appear only as weights on the same scale.

## What this lens values in the study

The critical lens can use the study's main result: people led to act against their views, under mild institutional pressure, often come to defend what they did. That helps explain how people come to believe in the practices of the organisations they work for. The study also shows that attitudes are not fixed, which discursive psychology agrees with, for different reasons.

## What it would question

- Routine deception of participants who were students under a course requirement.
- The reduction of each student's talk to one number.
- An individual, mental explanation of a situation created by an institution.
- The unexamined gender scene, and the invisibility of the women involved.
- Who uses the knowledge. The finding that small inducements change attitudes more than large ones is useful to anyone who wants to change attitudes cheaply.

## Evidence from this tradition

**Deception and trust.** Herbert Kelman (1967), himself a social psychologist and the author of one of the studies Festinger and Carlsmith cite, argued that the routine use of deception in social psychology treats people as objects, damages trust between researchers and the public, and may leave participants less willing to believe what psychologists tell them. Diana Baumrind (1964) made a similar case about Milgram's obedience studies. Criticisms like these led to formal research ethics codes in psychology from the 1970s.

**Attitudes vary with what talk is doing.** In *Discourse and Social Psychology* (1987), Potter and Wetherell analysed interviews and other talk and showed that the same person could express very different evaluations of the same topic, depending on what the talk was doing at that moment. Traditional attitude research treats this variation as error. They treat it as the main thing to explain.

**Consistency as a sales tool.** Robert Cialdini's *Influence* (1984) describes commitment and consistency as a main technique of salespeople and recruiters: get a small, freely given commitment, and people will act to stay consistent with it. Knowledge from experiments like this one became a resource for those with the money to design situations.

**Evidence against the lens.** The critical lens says the effect may be only talk, shaped by the setting. But dissonance studies have found effects on measures that are not talk. Croyle and Cooper (1983) found increased physical arousal after people freely argued against their own views. Hypocrisy studies, in which people preach a behaviour and are reminded of their own failures, have changed actual behaviour, such as buying condoms (see the [importance page](/cases/cognitive-dissonance/importance/)). So something more than a change in how people talk seems to be going on, even if talk is part of it.

## A study this lens would design

1. Recruit students without any course requirement, and tell them honestly that the study is about how people talk about doing things they disagree with.
2. Ask them about real situations in their own lives where an institution asked them to say something they did not believe, for example to visitors, inspectors or new students.
3. Record and transcribe group discussions and interviews, and analyse how people justify, excuse or joke about what they said.
4. Ask who made the request, what would have happened if they had refused, and who benefited.
5. Share the analysis with the participants and include their interpretations in the report.

**Aim:** not to test a prediction but to map the ways people account for institutional pressure, and to bring the institution, not only the individual mind, into the explanation.
