## The lens in brief

Cultural psychology studies how culture and mind shape each other. <Term>Culture</Term> here means the shared practices, values, beliefs and ways of living that a group passes on. Cultural psychologists such as Hazel Markus, Shinobu Kitayama, Richard Shweder and Steven Heine argue that many "basic" psychological processes take different forms in different cultures.

A key idea for this study comes from Markus and Kitayama (1991). They described two broad ways of understanding the self. An <Term>independent self</Term> sees the person as a separate individual defined by inner qualities, such as their opinions and preferences. Being the same person across situations is a sign of honesty and maturity. An <Term>interdependent self</Term> sees the person as defined by relationships and roles. Adjusting what you say to the people and situation in front of you is a sign of maturity, not of falseness. Both views exist in every society, but cultures differ in which they stress.

The cultural lens asks of any study: what did the participants' culture contribute, and would the result look the same elsewhere?

## How this lens reads the study

**The paper names its own cultural assumption.** Explaining why saying "not X" clashes with believing "X", the authors write that if nothing else is considered, "it would follow, at least in our culture, that if he believes 'X' he would publicly state 'X'" (p. 203). This is the root of the whole theory. Festinger had said the same in his 1957 book: two cognitions "may be dissonant for a person living in one culture and not for a person living in another" (p. 15). Dissonance arises because the student has broken a rule that a person should say what he believes. That rule belongs to an independent view of the self. Where the interdependent view is strong, a statement made to be polite, to help a teacher or to keep harmony may not clash with the self in the same way.

**A choice that is "his".** Later research showed that dissonance after a counter-attitudinal act depends on feeling that you chose to do it and are responsible for it (see the [behavioural lens](/cases/cognitive-dissonance/lenses/behavioural/)). The 1959 procedure carefully created this feeling: the student was asked, not told, and could refuse. The cultural lens asks whether people everywhere experience their actions as personal choices in the same way. Research with Indian and American participants, described below, suggests they do not.

**A favour to an authority.** The request came from the experimenter on behalf of a professor. In settings where teachers and seniors are owed respect and help, agreeing might feel less like a free choice and more like a duty. On dissonance theory, a duty is an outside reason for the act, which, like the $20, should reduce dissonance. This is a prediction, not a finding, but it shows how the same procedure could mean something different in a different culture.

**The setting was private and personal.** The student lied about his own experience and then gave his own private rating. Nothing he did affected his family or friends, and nobody he cared about would know. Cross-cultural studies suggest that this kind of setting is exactly the one in which people with independent selves show dissonance most clearly, and people with interdependent selves least.

## What this lens values in the study

It values the authors' honesty in writing "at least in our culture", a caveat many textbooks drop. It also values the study's clear, repeatable logic, which let later researchers test whether the effect travels. Those tests found people justifying their own actions in every culture studied, but in different forms and in different situations.

## What it would question

- One sample of 71 young American men, and the rule "say what you believe", treated as universal.
- Personal choice treated as the normal way of experiencing an action.
- A private, individual setting treated as a neutral one.
- Rating scales treated as if they mean the same everywhere. Chen, Lee and Stevenson (1995) found that East Asian students used the middle of rating scales more, and the ends less, than North American students, which can make group averages hard to compare.

## Evidence from this tradition

**No justification in a private choice.** Steven Heine and Darrin Lehman (1997) asked Canadian and Japanese students to rate music CDs, then let them choose between two they had rated about equally. Afterwards, Canadian students rated the CD they chose higher and the one they rejected lower, the usual sign of reducing dissonance after a decision. Japanese students did not.

**Justification when others are involved.** Kitayama, Snibbe, Markus and Suzuki (2004) found that Japanese participants did justify their choices when their attention was drawn to other people, for example when they thought about what others would prefer. American participants justified their choices in private conditions. Imada and Kitayama (2010) found that whether a choice felt private or public interacted with culture: cues that others could see the choice made justification more likely among Japanese participants. Hoshino-Browne and colleagues (2005) compared European Canadians and Asian Canadians. European Canadians justified choices they made for themselves. Asian Canadians who identified strongly with their Asian heritage justified choices they made for a close friend more than choices for themselves.

**An early test outside the West.** In Hong Kong in 1964, the American researcher Paul Hiniker paid 50 refugees from mainland China, mostly men, to record a short talk arguing that rice rations in China would fall sharply. The low inducement was a Chinese lantern; the high inducement was a transistor radio. As with American participants, the bigger gift produced more compliance. But in Hiniker's summary, the private attitudes of the Chinese participants "were affected neither by increased inducement nor by consequent increased compliance" (Hiniker, 1965). He explained this through norms of "face". It is a single study with a political topic and a vulnerable group, so it shows how hard cross-cultural tests are as well as what they found.

**Choice means different things in India and the United States.** Krishna Savani and colleagues, working with Indian and American participants, found that Americans' choices were more tightly linked to their personal preferences than Indians' were (Savani, Markus & Conner, 2008). In a second set of studies, Americans were more likely than Indians to describe ordinary actions as choices at all (Savani, Markus, Naidu, Kumar & Berlia, 2010). If fewer actions are experienced as personal choices, fewer actions should create the personal responsibility that dissonance depends on.

**Explaining behaviour by person or by situation.** Joan Miller (1984) asked Hindu Indians in Mysore and Americans in Chicago to explain everyday behaviour. Indian adults referred more often to the situation and to social roles; American adults referred more often to the person's character. The difference grew with age, which suggests it is learned. This matters for the $1 condition. The dissonance account assumes the student explains his own lie by looking inward, at his attitude, because the situation gives too little reason. A person used to explaining behaviour through roles and situations might find a situational reason more easily.

**Evidence against a strong cultural reading.** None of these studies show that East Asians or Indians lack dissonance. They show that what triggers it, and whose choices it attaches to, differ. The Japanese and Asian Canadian studies found clear dissonance effects under the right social conditions. Most of the studies also used the free-choice method, not the 1959 procedure, and most compared East Asian with Western samples. Direct evidence on induced compliance in India is thin, as the [India page](/cases/cognitive-dissonance/india/) explains.

## A study this lens would design

1. Run the same basic procedure in several places, for example cities in India, Japan and Canada, with local research teams and with women as well as men.
2. Vary who makes the request: an unknown experimenter, a respected senior teacher, or a fellow student.
3. Vary who is affected: in some versions the lie is told to a stranger, in others to someone from the student's own college or community.
4. Vary who will know: the student's act stays private, or his friends will hear about it.
5. Measure how far each student felt the act was his or her own choice, and measure independent and interdependent self-views.

**Prediction:** where the independent self is stressed, dissonance will be strongest when the act is private and freely chosen. Where the interdependent self is stressed, it will be strongest when others are affected or watching, and weakest when the request comes from a respected senior.
