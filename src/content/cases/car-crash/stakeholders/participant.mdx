## What you care about

Imagine you are one of the 45 students in Experiment 1, or one of the 150 in Experiment 2. You agreed to take part in a psychology study, perhaps for course credit. You care about knowing roughly what you are in for, being able to stop if it upsets you, and being told the truth at the end. You also care about something more personal. Road accidents are common. You may have been in one, or lost someone in one. Watching crash after crash is not neutral for everyone.

If you were in Experiment 2, you care about one more thing. A week after the film, you ticked "yes, I saw broken glass". You did not. Did anyone ever tell you?

## What worries you about the original study

- **You were not told what the study was about.** The paper does not report what participants were told (pp. 586-587). The study was about the wording of one question. The design needed that purpose to be kept from you. That is the mildest kind of deception, but you still deserve to know at the end.
- **The films were made to shock.** Four of the seven films in Experiment 1 were staged crashes whose original purpose "was to illustrate what can happen to human beings when cars collide at various speeds" (p. 586). You watched seven crashes in a session of "about an hour and a half". The paper reports no warning and no chance to look away or leave.
- **The question was designed to mislead you.** The word *smashed* was chosen because it suggests a violent crash. In Experiment 2 the film probably showed a slow, bumper-to-bumper collision (see the [method page](/cases/car-crash/method/)), so the word overstated what you saw.
- **You may have left with a false belief.** 16 students in the *smashed* group and 13 in the other two groups reported broken glass that was not in the film (p. 587). The paper does not say anyone was told. A detail about a film is harmless, but it is still something false that you now believe about your own experience.
- **Your own account was ignored.** After each film you wrote a free account of the accident in your own words (pp. 586-587). The paper never reports these accounts. Only your number and your tick mattered.
- **Your answers were linked across a week.** To compare your answers on Day 1 and Day 8, the researchers had to know who you were. The paper does not say how your name and answers were stored.

## Your redesign

1. **An honest information sheet.** It says the study is about memory for short traffic films, that you will answer questions now and possibly a week later, and that some details of the study will be explained only at the end. The ethics guidelines of the Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR) call this <Term>incomplete disclosure</Term>: withholding the hypothesis without telling participants anything false (Box 9.5).
2. **A content warning and an easy way out.** The sheet says that the films show car crashes. You may look away or stop at any time without giving a reason, and without losing credit.
3. **No footage of real injuries.** Staged crashes with no visible people, or minor collisions, are enough to test the question.
4. **A debriefing at the end of your last session.** The researcher explains that different students read different verbs, and states clearly that there was no broken glass in the film. If you said yes, you hear that this is a normal effect of the question, not a sign that your memory is poor.
5. **Your own words count.** Your free account is coded and reported, so the researchers can see whether the verb also changed how you described the crash before any leading question.
6. **Coded data and a right to withdraw.** Your answers are stored under a code number, and you may withdraw them after the debriefing.

## Why

The ethics codes say much of this directly. The American Psychological Association's code requires researchers to tell participants about "reasonably foreseeable factors that may be expected to influence their willingness to participate such as potential risks, discomfort, or adverse effects" (Standard 8.02). It requires deception to be explained "as early as is feasible", with permission "to withdraw their data" (8.07(c)). It also requires "reasonable steps to correct any misconceptions" (8.08).

The British Psychological Society says that "the risk of harm should be no greater than that encountered in ordinary life" (section 2.4). It also says that "especially where any deception or withholding of information has taken place, it is important to provide an appropriate debriefing" (section 10).

The ICMR guidelines add that "an attempt should be made to debrief the participants" after the research (5.11.3).

There is evidence that debriefing matters and that it is often skipped. A review found that "fewer than 30% of misinformation papers report any debriefing at all", and that false memories created in experiments "often persist after debriefing" (Greene and colleagues, 2023). A follow-up of 630 people, six months after a study that showed them fabricated news stories, found no negative long-term effects (Murphy and colleagues, 2020). That is reassuring, but only if the debriefing happens.

The content warning matters more in India than in Seattle in 1974. In 2023, police in India reported 4,80,583 road accidents and 1,72,890 deaths (Ministry of Road Transport and Highways, 2025). In any class of students, some will have been touched by a road death.

## What your redesign gives up

- **Warned viewers may watch differently.** A student told that the study is about memory for traffic films may watch more carefully, which could make the effect smaller than in 1974.
- **Some people will stop.** If students who find the films upsetting leave, the remaining sample is less typical, and the groups may become unequal.
- **The debriefing can spread.** Students who learn the trick may tell friends who have not yet taken part, so later participants may arrive knowing the purpose.
- **Coding free accounts takes work.** Someone must read and score every account, ideally two people who do not know the condition, which adds time and cost.
