## What you care about

Imagine you are a memory researcher planning a study in 2026. The car crash study is one of the most cited experiments in psychology, and its broad message, that questions can shape what witnesses report, is now accepted by almost everyone in the field. But you care about whether a specific claim is true, how large the effect is, and why it happens. You want results that other laboratories, in other countries and languages, could reproduce.

## What worries you about the original study

- **The groups were small.** Experiment 1 had only 9 students per verb (p. 586). Small samples tend to produce effects that look larger than they really are, and one or two students who always guess high could move an average.
- **The statistics are thin.** The paper gives no standard deviations, so nobody can calculate the size of the effect in Experiment 1. It reports one overall test for the five verbs and one for the three groups in Experiment 2, but no tests between pairs (pp. 586-587). Tested on its own, the difference between *smashed* and *hit* for broken glass is borderline.
- **Table 3 rests on very few people.** The argument that *smashed* did more than raise speed estimates comes from a table with no significance test and unknown numbers in each speed band. Working backwards (this guide's estimate, not the paper's figures), the highest band probably held about 8 *smashed* students and only a handful of *hit* students (see the [findings page](/cases/car-crash/findings/)).
- **The answers were forced.** "Yes" or "no", with no "I don't know" (p. 587). The free accounts written after each film were never reported.
- **Allocation is not described.** The paper does not say how students were placed in conditions, or whether students in one room received different verbs.
- **The effect has not been reliably replicated.** This guide found no modern <Term>direct replication</Term>, a repeat of the same method with new participants. A <Term>conceptual replication</Term> tests the same idea with different materials. Two conceptual replications found no verb effect on speed. One used written descriptions of an ice hockey collision (Goldschmied, Sheptock, Kim and Galily, 2017). The other used car crash videos and images, and estimates followed people's prior beliefs about car speeds, not the verb (Raghunath, Fultz and Sanchez, 2021).

<Note title="The statistics">
The overall test for the five verbs in Experiment 1 was F'(5,55) = 4.65, p &lt; .005 (p. 586). The overall test for the three groups in Experiment 2 was χ²(2) = 7.76, p &lt; .025 (p. 587). Neither test shows which pairs differ. This guide's own test of *smashed* against *hit* for broken glass gives p of about .03, or about .06 with Fisher's exact test.
</Note>

## Your redesign

1. **Preregister everything.** <Term>Preregistration</Term> means publishing a study's plan before any data are collected. Publish the hypotheses, the sample size, the exclusion rules and the planned tests, including *smashed* against *hit* and *smashed* against control for broken glass.
2. **Plan the sample size.** Choose the number of participants so the study can detect a small effect reliably. That means hundreds per condition, not 9 or 50.
3. **Run it at many sites.** Laboratories in several countries, including India, run the same protocol. Questions are translated by native speakers into Hindi, Tamil, Spanish and other languages, so the study tests whether the verb effect depends on the language.
4. **Assign at random, inside each session.** Questionnaires with different verbs are shuffled before each session, so every room contains every condition.
5. **Use films with known speeds.** Modern staged crash videos at several known speeds let the study measure accuracy as well as the verb effect.
6. **Add better measures.** Offer "I don't know" and a confidence rating. Code every free account, blind to condition, for mentions of glass and for words that describe the crash's severity. Add a <Term>source question</Term>: "Did you see this in the film, or was it only mentioned in a question?"
7. **Check for guessing the purpose.** Orne (1962) called the cues that give a study's purpose away <Term>demand characteristics</Term>. To find out whether participants worked out what the study was testing, end with a short <Term>funnel debriefing</Term>: a series of questions that move from general to specific.
8. **Share the data.** Make all data, materials and analysis code public.

## Why

The original authors themselves raised the main alternative explanation, <Term>response bias</Term> (p. 586). In a response bias, the verb pushes an unsure student towards a higher answer while the memory of the film stays the same. Experiment 2 was the authors' attempt to rule it out. Later work kept the question open. Michael McCloskey and Maria Zaragoza (1985) showed that, with a modified final test, misled participants did as well as controls, and argued that the standard test could not show the original memory was impaired. A source question, a confidence rating and an "I don't know" option all help to separate a changed memory from a guess.

Large, preregistered replications have changed how psychologists see other classic memory findings. A preregistered replication of the "lost in the mall" study, with 123 participants, found false memories in 35%, more than the original 25% (Murphy and colleagues, 2023). A registered replication of Loftus's (1979) finding that blatant misinformation protects people against subtler misinformation failed: "In two high-powered experiments, we failed to replicate both the inoculation effect and the boundary condition effect" (O'Donnell and Chan, 2025). Either outcome is useful. Only a study of this kind can tell which result the verb effect will produce.

There is also a reason to include India and other languages. In a ten-country study of how witnesses pick up details from each other, 40 students at NIMHANS in Bengaluru showed the same memory contamination as the samples in the other nine countries (Ito and colleagues, 2019). But no Indian replication of the verb study was found, and verbs do not translate neatly.

## What your redesign gives up

- **Cost and time.** Many sites, translations, coding and coordination take years and a large budget.
- **A smaller effect by design.** An "I don't know" option and a source question will reduce false "yes" answers. That is more accurate, but it means the result cannot be compared directly with 1974.
- **Translation problems.** A verb that suggests violence in English may not have an exact match in another language. Differences between sites could come from the translation, not from culture.
- **Still a laboratory.** However large and careful, the study still uses films and students with nothing at stake. It can tell you whether the verb effect is real, not how much it matters in a police station.
