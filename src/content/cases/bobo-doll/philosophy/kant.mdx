## The tradition in brief

Immanuel Kant (1724 to 1804) spent his whole life in Königsberg, then a Prussian city. He wrote the most influential modern account of morality as a matter of reason and duty. Four of his ideas matter here.

**Autonomy.** A moral agent is a being who gives itself the law it follows. The rule a person actually acts on is their <Term>maxim</Term>. An act has moral worth only when its maxim comes from the agent's own reason, acting from duty, and not from inclination, reward, fear or imitation. Kant called self-rule <Term>autonomy</Term>, and rule by anything outside reason, including one's own desires, <Term>heteronomy</Term>.

**Two standpoints.** A human being can be viewed in two ways: as part of nature, where every event has a cause, and as a free agent answerable for what they do. Science takes the first view. Morality needs the second. Kant held that both are legitimate and that neither can be reduced to the other (*Groundwork*, 4:452).

**Persons are ends.** "So act that you use humanity, whether in your own person or in the person of any other, always at the same time as an end, never merely as a means" (*Groundwork*, 4:429). Children count. They have "an original innate (not acquired) right to the care of their parents" and "can never be considered as the property" of anyone (*Metaphysics of Morals*, 6:280 to 282).

**Example is powerful and dangerous.** Kant wrote more about children than readers of the *Groundwork* expect. His lectures on education take it for granted that children copy the adults around them, and treat that fact as a responsibility, not a method.

## What Kant says about example, habit and the child

**Imitation has no place in morality.** "Nor could one give worse advice to morality than by wanting to derive it from examples", Kant wrote. Every example "must itself first be appraised in accordance with principles of morality"; "Even the Holy One of the Gospel must first be compared with our ideal of moral perfection" (*Groundwork*, 4:408). Then the sentence that matters for this study: "Imitation has no place at all in matters of morality, and examples serve only for encouragement" (4:409). An example can show that a hard thing is possible and make a rule vivid. It cannot tell you that the rule is good. Only your own reason can do that.

**Yet imitation comes first.** Kant knew how children begin. "For a still undeveloped human being, imitation is the first determination of his will to accept maxims that he afterwards makes for himself" (*Metaphysics of Morals*, 6:479). The small child "cannot judge for himself, and the faculty of imitation is still strong in him" (*On Education*, section 28). People have "a natural tendency to compare" their behaviour with "a more important person (the child with adults ...) in order to imitate" (*Anthropology*, 7:245). But a good example "should not serve as a model but only as a proof that it is really possible to act in conformity with duty" (6:480). A teacher should never tell a naughty pupil to "take an example from that good (orderly, diligent) boy! For this would only cause him to hate that boy" (6:480).

**Example is all-powerful, and feelings pass to children too.** "Here, as everywhere, example is all-powerful, and either strengthens or destroys good precepts" (*On Education*, section 90). Kant noticed how an adult's fear passes to a child without any teaching: "fear of spiders and toads" is "suggested to children by their nurses", and a child "would probably pick up a spider as readily as anything else, were it not that the nurse's horror at the sight of spiders has affected the child by a sort of sympathy" (section 57). This is observational learning, described in 1803.

**Habit makes a person less free.** Kant separated an <Term>aptitude</Term>, a practised readiness to act rightly that is still chosen each time through "the thought of the law", from a <Term>habit</Term>, "a uniformity in action that has become a necessity through frequent repetition", which "is not one that proceeds from freedom, and therefore not a moral aptitude" (*Metaphysics of Morals*, 6:407). A habit "is a mechanism of sense rather than a principle of thought" (6:479). The lectures are blunter: "The more habits a man allows himself to form, the less free and independent he becomes" (section 44). He did allow one kind of habituation: children who repeatedly judge stories of conduct "would make a good foundation for uprightness" (*Critique of Practical Reason*, 5:154). Practice in judging is welcome. Drilled behaviour is not.

**Trained or educated.** "Man can only become man by education. He is merely what education makes of him" (*On Education*, section 7). But "Horses and dogs are broken in; and man, too, may be broken in. It is, however, not enough that children should be merely broken in; for it is of greater importance that they shall learn to think" (section 19). Moral training "must be based upon 'maxims,' not upon discipline"; "If you punish a child for being naughty, and reward him for being good, he will do right merely for the sake of the reward" (section 77).

**Psychological freedom is the freedom of a turnspit.** Moving the causes of behaviour inside the head does not make a person free. Causes "may be internal and ... produce actions by means of representations", and this may be called "psychological freedom", but it is still "natural necessity" (*Critique of Practical Reason*, 5:96). Such freedom "would at bottom be nothing better than the freedom of a turnspit, which, when once it is wound up, also accomplishes its movements of itself" (5:97). A turnspit is a clockwork that turns a roasting spit once wound.

**Anger is an affect, not yet a vice.** Kant distinguished an <Term>affect</Term>, a quick storm of feeling that "makes reflection impossible" and "forgets easily", from a <Term>passion</Term>, a settled desire that "takes its time" (*Anthropology*, 7:252). "Affect works like water that breaks through a dam; passion, like a river that digs itself deeper and deeper into its bed." A tendency to anger is "something childish and weak, which can indeed coexist with the best will"; hatred is the real vice (*Metaphysics of Morals*, 6:408). On children: "to thwart them purposely is an utterly wrong way of bringing them up", because "their inward rage will be all the stronger, for children have not yet learned to control themselves" (*On Education*, section 55).

## Reading the study through this tradition

**The copied acts: real imitation, not moral learning.** All but two of the 24 children who watched the aggressive model reproduced some of its unusual acts, and about a third repeated the harmless phrase "He sure is a tough fella". Kant would not be surprised. Children of three to five are in the stage where "imitation is the first determination" of the will. He would deny that the result shows learning in any sense that touches character. The harmless phrase is his evidence: what the children reproduced was the surface of conduct, words and gestures, exactly as "The imitator is the mimicker of the man who has a character" (*Anthropology*, 7:293). The paper is right about the fact. Kant disagrees about what it means.

**The absent reward: allies against reinforcement, for different reasons.** No child and no model was rewarded, and the children still copied. Kant would welcome this as a blow against the idea that conduct is built by reward, which the lectures condemn. But the paper's own explanation of why children imitate, a general habit laid down by past rewards for copying (see the [question page](/cases/bobo-doll/question/)), is exactly the "mechanism of sense" that Kant says makes a person less free. He would say that Bandura refuted reinforcement with a theory still in debt to it.

**The frustration step: wrong in itself, and it muddles the measure.** Every child was shown attractive toys and then told that they were kept for other children. The lectures say that to thwart children "purposely is an utterly wrong way of bringing them up". Kant would also ask what was measured afterwards. His two-level account of hostile feeling says that the normal storm of a four-year-old denied a toy is an affect, quick and soon over, not a vice. Five-second counts of punches cannot tell an affect from a passion. The study may have measured "aggression", or it may have measured a passing storm that the adults had just caused.

**The one-way mirror and the two standpoints.** Observers behind a mirror recorded behaviour in 240 five-second intervals, and the children were never asked what they thought they were doing. Kant grants the method its own logic. As beings in the world of sense, children's acts can be observed, counted and assigned causes. What the count cannot reach is the child as agent: the meaning of the act, whether play, permission or showing off. "What counts is not actions, which one sees, but those inner principles of actions that one does not see" (*Groundwork*, 4:407). The mirror is the emblem of the first standpoint. It gives the observer the child as appearance and hides the observer from the child. Kant even supplies the reason for the mirror: a person who "perceives that someone is observing him" is "in a state of constraint and inner agitation" (*Anthropology*, 7:295). He would note the price, which is concealment.

**The children's remarks: convention, not principle.** Some children said of the woman model, "That's not the way for a lady to behave", and of the man, "He's a good fighter like Daddy" (p. 581). Kant would file these under fashion and custom: "when the game of imitation is fixed, it becomes custom" (*Anthropology*, 7:245). The children were applying a social rule about who may hit, not a moral principle. That distinction is useful. But Kant shared his century's views on women, and the guide should not recruit him as a critic of the study's sex-typing.

**Adults in a nursery are examples whether they mean to be or not.** The 72 children were in a university nursery school, an educational setting. In Kant's terms every adult in it is an example whose conduct is "all-powerful", and the "experimental (technical) means for cultivating virtue is good example on the part of the teacher" (*Metaphysics of Morals*, 6:479). An adult who beats a clown, shouts "Sock him in the nose" and leaves a child alone with the same doll has, whatever the paper calls it, given a lesson. Kant would value the study as proof that example matters, and condemn the demonstration on the same ground. He would also ask who stood in the parents' place, since children have a right to care and education and are nobody's property.

## Where it agrees, and where it clashes

Kant and the paper agree on more than one might expect. Children imitate before they judge. Example is powerful and needs no reward. Adults pass on feelings and conduct without meaning to. Reward is not the engine of good conduct.

They clash on four points. Counted behaviour is not moral learning, because learning in Kant's sense means coming to act on one's own maxims. Habits of imitation reduce freedom rather than build character. Anger in a small child is an affect, not a vice, and a method that counts acts cannot tell the two apart. And a child is a person who may not be upset or shown violence for the sake of a measurement. Behind all four lies the deepest difference: the study occupies the standpoint of nature and cannot see that it does.

## Questions it would put to the researchers

- By what principle did you decide what counts as "aggression"? Did you count a passing affect or a settled disposition?
- You measured what the children did. How will you know what they took the adult to be doing: play, permission or instruction?
- You frustrated 72 small children on purpose. Who consented on their behalf, and what did the children gain?
- You showed children an adult attacking a toy and shouting "Sock him", and called it a manipulation. Why is it not education, and a bad one?
- Your hypothesis explains copying by a habit built from past rewards. If habits make a person less free, what does a successful demonstration of copying show about the children's freedom?
- Could the same procedure show the copying of a kind act? If so, what in your theory distinguishes a child who copies kindness from one who is kind from principle?

## Cautions

- **The lectures on education are not Kant's finished prose.** They were compiled from his notes by Theodor Rink in 1803, and Kant is reported to have said that he had never managed to apply his own precepts to a pupil. Cite them as lectures, and where a point matters, pair them with a published work, as this page does.
- **Kant did not deny that behaviour has causes.** He allowed "psychological causality" and wrote an empirical *Anthropology*. His claim is that causal explanation is one standpoint and cannot settle questions of freedom and worth. Scholars disagree about how the two standpoints fit together.
- **Kant did not forbid all habituation.** He recommended habituating children's judgement. His target is drilled action without a maxim.
- **Kant is no ally against sex-typing.** His texts voice the gender assumptions of his age. The lens offers the distinction between convention and principle, nothing more.
- **Kant had no theory of research ethics.** His remarks on children as persons concern parents' duties. Use them for the thought that a child is not material, not as a rule about experiments.
- **Famous lines to avoid.** "Out of the crooked timber of humanity no straight thing was ever made" is Isaiah Berlin's rendering of a sentence in Kant's *Idea for a Universal History* (8:23); it is not in the works quoted here.
