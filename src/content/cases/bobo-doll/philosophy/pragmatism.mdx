## The tradition in brief

Pragmatism is the one philosophical tradition on these pages that grew up alongside American psychology. Charles Sanders Peirce (1839 to 1914) first defined it; William James (1842 to 1910), who also wrote the founding textbook of American psychology, developed and spread it; John Dewey (1859 to 1952) and George Herbert Mead (1863 to 1931) formed a second generation at Chicago, where Dewey founded a laboratory school and Mead a social psychology. Dewey's 1896 paper on the reflex arc argued that stimulus and response are not two events but functions within one coordinated act, and Mead's lectures were published as *Mind, Self, and Society* with the subtitle "from the standpoint of a social behaviorist".

Three ideas run through this page.

**Beliefs and traits are habits of action.** A <Term>habit</Term>, for the pragmatists, is not a repeated act but a readiness to act in a certain way when the occasion arrives. The meaning of an idea is the difference it makes to what we do.

**The young are plastic, and the world shapes them, but not as wax.** <Term>Plasticity</Term>, Dewey insisted, "is essentially the ability to learn from experience" (*Democracy and Education*, chapter 4, section 1), the power to keep something from one situation for use in the next.

**Conduct is social all the way down.** For Dewey and Mead there is no such thing as behaviour with the social setting removed. Even an adult's silence is a response the child reads, and a child who seems to copy an adult is taking up a standpoint, which Mead called taking the attitude of the other.

## What pragmatists say about habit, imitation and the young

**Habit is a readiness, and it can show itself once.** "When we look at living creatures from an outward point of view, one of the first things that strike us is that they are bundles of habits", James began his chapter on the subject (*Principles*, volume 1, chapter 4). Habit "is thus the enormous fly-wheel of society, its most precious conservative agent", and "Could the young but realize how soon they will become mere walking bundles of habits, they would give more heed to their conduct while in the plastic state." Dewey made the definition precise. "The essence of habit is an acquired predisposition to ways or modes of response, not to particular acts". A man with a habit of anger may show it in "a murderous attack upon some one who has offended. His act is nonetheless due to habit because it occurs only once in his life" (*Human Nature and Conduct*, p. 42). And in the other direction, "the plasticity of the young presents a temptation to those having greater experience and hence greater power which they rarely resist. It seems putty to be molded according to current designs" (p. 64).

**"Imitation" names a result and then pretends to be the cause.** Here the pragmatists disagree with each other, and the guide must say so. James treated copying as a native impulse: "from this time onward man is essentially the imitative animal. His whole educability and in fact the whole history of civilization depend on this trait", and he described in his own small son "a blind impulse to act as soon as a certain perception occurs" (*Principles*, volume 2, chapter 24). Dewey rejected the explanation. "The basic error in the current notion of imitation is that it puts the cart before the horse. It takes an effect for the cause of the effect." To say children act alike because of imitation "is an explanation of the same order as the famous saying that opium puts men to sleep because of its dormitive power". What matters is the shared activity: a child joining a ball game "imitates the means of doing, not the end or thing to be done", and "imitation of ends ... is a superficial and transitory affair which leaves little effect upon disposition" (*Democracy and Education*, chapter 3, section 3). The 1934 Mead text goes further: "Imitation as a general instinct is now discredited in human psychology." "Why should a person wink because another person winks? What stimulus would cause another person to act in that way? The sight of another person acting in another way? This is an impossible assumption" (*Mind, Self, and Society*, pp. 52 to 53). For Dewey and Mead the right question about a copying child is: what is the child doing?

**Anger gets its meaning from other people's answers to it.** Dewey denied that there is a single thing called aggression inside a child waiting to come out. A burst of anger in a human being "is as meaningless as a gust of wind on a mudpuddle apart from a direction given it by the presence of other persons, apart from the responses they make to it. It is a physical spasm, a blind dispersive burst of wasteful energy. It gets quality, significance, when it becomes a smouldering sullenness, an annoying interruption, a peevish irritation, a murderous revenge, a blazing indignation" (*Human Nature and Conduct*, p. 90). "The notion that anger still remains a single force is a lazy mythology" (p. 153). "War is thus seen to be a function of social institutions, not of what is natively fixed in human constitution" (p. 115).

**There is no neutral adult in the room.** "Even letting a man alone is a definite response. Envy, admiration and imitation are complicities. Neutrality is non-existent. Conduct is always shared" (p. 17). Education, for Dewey, works the same way: "We never educate directly, but indirectly by means of the environment" (*Democracy and Education*, chapter 2, section 4), and "all direction is but re-direction; it shifts the activities already going on into another channel" (chapter 3, section 1). A child shaped only by what pleases and pains him "is trained like an animal rather than educated like a human being"; education adds "making the individual a sharer or partner in the associated activity so that he feels its success as his success" (chapter 2, section 2).

**Seeing an act plants its idea, and ideas move the body.** James's law of <Term>ideo-motor action</Term> is the oldest mechanism on this page for copying without reward: "every representation of a movement awakens in some degree the actual movement which is its object; and awakens it in a maximum degree whenever it is not kept from so doing by an antagonistic representation present simultaneously to the mind" (*Principles*, volume 2, chapter 26). "We think the act, and it is done; and that is all that introspection tells us of the matter."

**Taking the attitude of the other.** In the 1934 text, "The self is something which has a development; it is not initially there, at birth, but arises in the process of social experience and activity" (p. 135). "Little children play at being a parent, at being a teacher" (p. 153), and in play "the individual stimulates himself to the response which he is calling out in the other person, and then acts in some degree in response to that situation" (p. 161). The organised attitude of the whole community, which the child eventually takes on, is the <Term>generalized other</Term>: "it is in the form of the generalized other that the social process influences the behavior of the individuals involved in it" (p. 155).

## Reading the study through this tradition

**The plastic state, with a sharp limit.** The children were between three and five, the age at which James thought paths are laid down most easily, and a vivid adult example is exactly the impression a plastic brain keeps. So far the pragmatists support the study's practical message about early example. The limit comes from Dewey's definition. A habit is "an acquired predisposition to ways or modes of response, not to particular acts". A count of punches a few minutes after watching is evidence of a stored act, not of a disposition. The study measured what James would call an idea working itself out in movement. It did not measure a changed child.

**The unusual acts, and the quarrel over "imitation".** All but two of the 24 children in the aggressive condition reproduced some of the model's odd acts and words. James would call it his "blind impulse to act as soon as a certain perception occurs". Dewey would say the study has measured the one kind of copying he thought least important, the "imitation of ends" that adults rebuke "as apes, monkeys, parrots, or copy cats", and he would add that the word explains nothing: it "repeats the fact as an explanation of itself". Mead would ask what the child was doing, and the paper's own data answer him. "Al's a good socker, he beat up Bobo. I want to sock like Al." "He's a good fighter like Daddy" (p. 581). The child is taking the attitude of Al, as little children "play at being a parent, at being a teacher", and acting from inside it. That is not copying movements. The study's framing, which counts acts and sets the remarks aside, misses what its own evidence shows.

**The community inside the child.** Both sexes copied the man's physical aggression more, and children said of the woman, "That's not the way for a lady to behave. Ladies are supposed to act like ladies" (p. 581). The paper explains this by different reward histories for "sex appropriate" behaviour. The 1934 Mead text explains it by the generalized other: "The attitude of the generalized other is the attitude of the whole community", and it is through this attitude that "the community exercises control over the conduct of its individual members" (pp. 154 to 155). A four-year-old who announces what ladies are "supposed" to do is voicing the community's organised attitude, not reporting a tally of past rewards. The study found the community inside the child without naming it.

**A manufactured impulse with its social answers removed.** Every child was shown attractive toys and then told they were kept for other children, because the authors feared that watching aggression might drain the urge to aggress and wanted every child pushed a little towards it. That fear assumes the single inner force that Dewey called "a lazy mythology". Then, in the test room, the experimenter sat "avoiding any interaction with the child" and observers watched through a mirror. Dewey's reading: the researchers created a mild impulse, removed the adult responses that normally turn a burst of energy into a meaning, and recorded the result as "aggression". And since "Even letting a man alone is a definite response", the silent experimenter and the mirror were themselves social facts the child read, most likely as permission. Dewey agrees with the study against instinct theories of aggression. He clashes with its measurement of acts stripped of their social answer.

**The absent reward, and an older explanation.** No child was rewarded for copying and the model was not rewarded for hitting, yet the paper's introduction still reasons in the language of past reinforcement before its discussion claims learning without reward. James had already said in 1890 that no reward is needed for an observed act to be reproduced. "Every representation of a movement awakens in some degree the actual movement which is its object." The same law predicts the calm male model's effect, which the authors found "generally restricts the range of behavior": a quiet adult supplies antagonistic representations. The behaviourist framing made the authors treat as surprising what James treated as "the normal process stripped of disguise".

**A good instrument that cut away too much.** The paper defines aggression as countable acts and controls the environment. It is a well-made instrument in Peirce's sense, reasoning "done with one's eyes open, by manipulating real things instead of words and fancies" (EP 1:111), and Dewey wanted exactly this kind of "scientific social psychology" (*Human Nature and Conduct*, p. 323) in place of moralising about children. But habits, for Dewey, "are functions of the surroundings as truly as of a person" (p. 14). A room set apart from the nursery, a mirror, an adult who does not speak and a doll built to be hit are surroundings. The study did not observe a child's conduct with the social setting removed. It observed conduct in a strange setting of its own making, and what it demonstrated, in Dewey's terms, was training rather than education, since nothing made the child "a sharer or partner in the associated activity".

## Where it agrees, and where it clashes

The pragmatists and the paper agree that early example shapes conduct, that no reward is needed for a watched act to be reproduced, that aggression is not a fixed instinct, and that questions about children should be settled by controlled observation rather than argument. Dewey would endorse the study's practical lesson that changing what surrounds children changes what they do.

They clash on three points. A count of acts is not evidence of a habit. "Imitation" is a description, not an explanation, and the children's own remarks show attitude-taking that the design ignored. And there is no view of a child from nowhere: the experimenter's silence, the mirror and the strange room were part of what the children responded to. Dewey's practical conclusion would differ too. Not "ban violent models", but design the environment, including the adults' responses, with care, because "We never educate directly, but indirectly by means of the environment."

## Questions it would put to the researchers

- What was the child doing when she hit the doll: playing a game, pleasing the grown-ups, or being Al?
- What did the adult's silence and the mirror mean to the child? "Even letting a man alone is a definite response."
- Would the same acts appear a week later, in a different room, before other children, or with the model present and disapproving? If not, is "learned" the right word?
- Why assume there is a single quantity of aggression that watching might drain or raise?
- Who taught the children that "Ladies are supposed to act like ladies", and did the experiment measure the community inside the child without saying so?
- Which practice does this result improve, and for whom: the nursery, the parent, the broadcaster? What environment would you design next?
- You recorded some of the children's remarks. Why are they anecdotes rather than data?

## Cautions

- **The pragmatists disagree about imitation.** James called man "the imitative animal"; Dewey and Mead denied that imitation explains anything. A sentence such as "pragmatists reject imitation" would be false for James.
- **Dewey did not dismiss imitation.** He distinguished copying ends, which "leaves little effect upon disposition", from copying the means to a shared goal, which is "an intelligent act".
- **Mead's book is a posthumous compilation** from students' notes. Attribute its wording to the 1934 text.
- **Do not claim influence.** Nothing shows that Bandura read Peirce, James, Dewey or Mead. The historical links on the other pages of this guide are Miller and Dollard and the behaviourists. Keep the lens as a reading, not a lineage.
- **The generalized other is a community attitude**, not a particular audience. The model in the room is a particular other; the rule about what ladies do is the generalized other.
