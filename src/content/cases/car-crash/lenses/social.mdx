## The lens in brief

Social psychology studies how other people shape what we think, feel and do. One of its oldest findings is <Term>conformity</Term>: changing what we say or do to match other people. In Solomon Asch's studies of the 1950s, people sometimes gave an answer they could see was wrong because a group of others had given it first (Asch, 1956). A second key idea is <Term>social influence</Term>, the general process by which other people change our behaviour, whether through pressure, example or authority.

For eyewitness memory, the social lens makes one simple point. Remembering is rarely private. A witness answers questions from police officers and lawyers, talks to other witnesses, and tells the story to family and friends. Each of these is a social encounter, and each can change what the witness reports.

The study sits close to this lens in its topic and far from it in its method. A leading question is a social act, and the paper starts from the courtroom (p. 585). But the study turned the question into a line of print, and the paper's explanation is about what happens inside one mind.

## How this lens reads the study

**A question carries the questioner's authority.** In real life, a question about an accident comes from someone, usually a person with some power: a police officer, a lawyer, an insurance agent. The paper's own word for asking the speed question is "interrogated" (pp. 586-587), the language of the police station. The students did not know who wrote the questions, but they knew the questionnaire came from the researchers, who had shown the films and knew what was in them. When the people in charge describe a crash as a "smash", a student has good social reason to accept that description.

**Answering is cooperating.** Ordinary conversation runs on trust. When someone asks "How fast were the cars going when they smashed into each other?", the listener normally assumes the speaker knows the cars smashed, and answers within that frame. To reject the frame, the student would have to challenge the questioner. Loftus herself later described the verb as "additional information" (Loftus, 1975, p. 565). The social lens adds that information from a trusted source is accepted more readily than information from a doubtful one. One later study reported that leading questions distorted memory less when people were told the questioner was a lawyer acting for one side (Dodd and Bradshaw, 1980). Only a summary of that study was available for this guide, so treat the detail with caution.

**Participants try to be good participants.** Martin Orne (1962) pointed out that an experiment is itself a social situation. The participant has made "an explicit agreement" to take part in "a special form of social interaction". Participants look for clues about what the study is for and often try to help. He called these clues <Term>demand characteristics</Term>. The paper implies that each student in Experiment 1 read the same verb after all seven films. A student who noticed "smashed" again and again might sense what kind of answer was expected. The paper does not report asking students what they thought the study was about.

**Group testing, private answers.** The students watched the films "in groups of various sizes" (pp. 586-587), but each wrote alone on paper. So the study kept people together in a room while removing any discussion between them. That is the reverse of what usually happens to real witnesses, who are often alone with the police but talk freely with each other.

**The co-witness is missing.** People who see an accident together usually talk about it afterwards. In a survey of real witnesses, 86% of those who had a co-witness said they had discussed the event with that person (Paterson and Kemp, 2006). Among 60 witnesses at a police identification suite in England, 88% reported that other people had also seen the crime, and 58% of those had discussed it with them (Skagerberg and Wright, 2008). A co-witness who says "Did you see that car smash into the other one?" supplies exactly the kind of information the study's printed question supplied. The study tested one source of information after the event, the questioner, and left out the most common one, other witnesses.

## What this lens values in the study

The study shows how little it takes to influence a report: one word, from a source the witness trusts. That is a strong finding for social psychology, because it shows influence working without pressure, argument or a group. By printing the question, the study also separated the content of the influence, the word itself, from the person delivering it. Social psychologists can then ask what the person adds.

The paper's starting point is also social. It begins from the legal idea of a leading question, which exists because courts recognise that a questioner can steer a witness (p. 585).

## What it would question

- The removal of the questioner. Real leading questions come from people with authority, tone of voice and expectations.
- Whether students guessed the purpose, and whether they tried to help. No check on demand characteristics is reported.
- The absence of co-witnesses, although talk between witnesses is a common source of information after an event.
- The single, private, written answer, when real witnesses give their accounts several times, out loud, to different people.
- Whether the broken glass answers reflect a changed memory or a wish to give the answer that fits the questioner's description.

## Evidence from this tradition

**Conformity.** Asch (1956) showed that many people sometimes conform to a unanimous group, even when the group is plainly wrong. Memory researchers later asked whether witnesses do the same with each other.

**Memory conformity.** In a study by Fiona Gabbert, Amina Memon and Kevin Allan (2003), pairs of people each watched a slightly different video of the same event and then discussed it. Afterwards, "71% of witnesses who had discussed the event went on to mistakenly recall items acquired during the discussion". In another study, Daniel Wright, Gail Self and Chris Justice (2000) showed pairs slightly different versions of a crime, in one of which an accomplice was present. After discussion, most pairs agreed on one version. "Confidence ratings strongly predicted which person in the pair persuaded the other." The more confident witness tended to win, whether or not they were right. The authors discussed the investigation of the Oklahoma City bombing in the United States as a real example of the problem.

**A ten-country test, including India.** Ito and colleagues (2019) repeated a co-witness study in ten countries. One sample was 40 students at the National Institute of Mental Health and Neurosciences (NIMHANS) in Bengaluru. "Each of the 10 samples replicated" the original finding: participants "often reported on the final test a non-witnessed answer that their co-witness had stated". Errors were especially likely when the witness "had not disputed" what the co-witness said.

**Interviewers' social pressure.** In a study described on the [behavioural lens](/cases/car-crash/lenses/behavioural/) page, adding social influence and praise to suggestive questions produced far more false accusations from preschool children than suggestive questions alone (Garven and colleagues, 1998).

**Evidence against a purely social reading.** The car crash study itself is a challenge to this lens. The students answered privately, on paper, with no co-witness and no face-to-face questioner. The broken glass effect appeared a week later, when the students were no longer looking at the original question. Social pressure in the moment cannot explain everything. Gabbert and colleagues (2003) also argue that memory conformity probably involves both social and cognitive processes: a witness may go along with a partner in public, but may also come to remember the partner's version as their own.

<Note title="Two ways of conforming">
Social psychologists distinguish <Term>public compliance</Term>, saying what others say while privately disagreeing, from <Term>private acceptance</Term>, coming to believe it. The difference matters for witnesses. Public compliance changes a report but leaves the memory intact, which is close to the "response bias" explanation in the paper (p. 586). Private acceptance changes the memory itself. The broken glass result a week later fits private acceptance better, because nobody was present to comply with.
</Note>

## A study this lens would design

1. Show pairs of students the same accident film.
2. Within each pair, one person is actually a <Term>confederate</Term>, a member of the research team pretending to be a participant. Before the questionnaire, the confederate remarks either "That was quite a smash" or "They just bumped into each other".
3. Vary who asks the speed question: a fellow student, or a person introduced as a police officer in uniform.
4. Collect each answer twice: once spoken aloud in front of the confederate, and once in private on paper.
5. A week later, ask about broken glass privately, then hold a step-by-step interview, as Orne recommended, about what each student thought the study was for.

**Prediction:** if social influence drives the effect, the co-witness's word and the questioner's status will each change speed estimates, and the effect will be larger in spoken answers than in private ones. If the information itself changes memory, the effect will appear just as strongly in the private answers a week later.

Deepa's statement may contain information that came from Farhan, not from her own eyes. Farhan supplied two pieces of information after the event: the phone, and the violent verb "rammed". Research on memory conformity shows that witnesses who discuss an event often go on to report details they only heard from a co-witness (Gabbert and colleagues, 2003), and that this happens in India as elsewhere (Ito and colleagues, 2019). A confident friend is a trusted source, and Deepa did not dispute what he said, which makes the error more likely.

The police could reduce the problem in three ways. They could interview witnesses separately and as early as possible, before they have talked at length. They could ask open questions, such as "Tell me what you saw", without suggesting a phone or a violent collision. And they could ask Deepa directly which details she saw herself and which she heard from someone else. The social lens adds that a witness's account is formed in conversation, so the conversations after an event matter as much as the event.
